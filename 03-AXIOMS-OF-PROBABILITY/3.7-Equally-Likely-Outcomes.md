# 3.7 Equally Likely Outcomes

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 03: Axioms of Probability](README.md) / 3.7 Equally Likely Outcomes

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [3.6 Union Bound](3.6-Union-Bound.md)

> **Big idea:** If a finite sample space has outcomes that are truly equally likely, the axioms force each one to have probability one over the number of outcomes, so probability becomes counting; but the rule fails for infinite spaces and for outcomes that only look equal.

---

## Why It Matters

The counting rule "favorable over total" from
[1.1 What is Probability](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.1-What-is-Probability.md)
is the most used formula in elementary probability, and all of
[Module 02: Counting](../02-COUNTING/README.md) exists to feed it. Here we prove it
from the axioms, and we find its exact limits. Most wrong answers in probability
puzzles come from using it with outcomes that are not really equally likely. In
computer science the same idea appears whenever we need something "uniformly at
random": a shuffle, a random key, a random sample of data.

## Intuition

The great mathematician Gottfried Leibniz once claimed that with two dice, a sum of
11 is exactly as likely as a sum of 12, because each "can be made in only one way":
11 as 5 and 6, and 12 as 6 and 6.

He was wrong, and the reason is subtle. Paint one die red and one blue. A sum of 12
needs red 6 and blue 6: one case. A sum of 11 can be red 5 and blue 6, **or** red 6
and blue 5: two cases. The dice do not know whether you can tell them apart, so the
sum 11 comes up twice as often as 12.

The lesson: the counting rule is only as good as the list of outcomes you count.
The 36 ordered pairs are equally likely. The 21 unordered results
($\lbrace 1, 1 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$, ...) are not.

## Picture

```text
                    Die 2
               1   2   3   4   5   6
             +------------------------
         1   | D   .   .   .   .   .     D = a double such as (3,3):
         2   | .   D   .   .   .   .         only 1 cell of 36
         3   | .   .   D   .   .   .
  Die 1  4   | .   .   .   D   .   .     x = the unordered result {5,6}:
         5   | .   .   .   .   D   x         2 cells of 36,
         6   | .   .   .   .   x   D         (5,6) and (6,5)
```

The 36 cells are equally likely, so an unordered result that is not a double covers
two cells and is twice as likely as a double.

## Definition

A probability on a finite sample space $\Omega = \lbrace \omega_1, \dots, \omega_n \rbrace$
is **uniform**, or has **equally likely outcomes**, when every single outcome has the
same probability. Then, for every event $A$:

$$
P(A) = \frac{\lvert A \rvert}{\lvert \Omega \rvert} = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{n}
$$

where $\lvert A \rvert$ is the number of outcomes in $A$, and $n = \lvert \Omega \rvert$.

**In words:** if all $n$ outcomes are equally likely, each has probability
$\frac{1}{n}$, and an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by $n$.

This is sometimes called the **naive definition** of probability. It has three
limits:

1. $\Omega$ must be **finite**. On an infinite set of outcomes, there is no way to
   make all of them equally likely (proved below).
2. The equal chances must have a **reason**, usually a physical symmetry: a
   well-made die, a well-shuffled deck, a good random number generator.
3. The outcomes must be chosen **at the right level of detail**. Ordered pairs of
   dice are equally likely; unordered pairs are not.

## Why It Is True

**The formula follows from the axioms.** Say each single outcome has probability $c$.
The $n$ single outcomes are disjoint, and their union is $\Omega$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= P(\Omega) && \text{(Axiom 2)} \\
&= P(\lbrace \omega_1 \rbrace) + \dots + P(\lbrace \omega_n \rbrace) && \text{(finite additivity, 3.3)} \\
&= n c && \text{(all equal to } c \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $c = \frac{1}{n}$. Any event $A$ is the disjoint union of its own $\lvert A \rvert$
outcomes, so $P(A) = \lvert A \rvert \cdot \frac{1}{n}$.

**No uniform probability on a countably infinite set.** Let
$\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, \dots \rbrace$ and suppose each outcome had the same
probability $c$. By Axiom 3, $P(\Omega)$ is the infinite sum $c + c + c + \dots$

- If $c = 0$, the sum is $0$, not $1$.
- If $c \gt 0$, the sum grows without limit, so it is not $1$.

Either way Axiom 2 fails. So "choose a positive whole number, all equally likely"
does not describe any probability at all. A probability on $\lbrace 1, 2, 3, \dots \rbrace$
must give smaller and smaller weights to large numbers, like
$P(\lbrace k \rbrace) = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^k$ in
[3.2](3.2-Kolmogorov-Axioms.md). For uncountable spaces, such as a random point in a
square, "uniform" uses length or area instead of counting:
[3.8 Geometric Probability](3.8-Geometric-Probability.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Leibniz's Two Dice

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice. Find $P(\text{sum} = 11)$ and $P(\text{sum} = 12)$.
What would you get by wrongly treating the 21 unordered results as equally likely?

**Solution.**

1. Use the 36 ordered pairs (red die, blue die). By symmetry they are equally likely.
2. Sum 11: $(5, 6)$ and $(6, 5)$, which is 2 pairs. Sum 12: only $(6, 6)$, which is 1 pair.

$$
P(\text{sum} = 11) = \frac{2}{36} = \frac{1}{18} \approx 0.0556, \qquad P(\text{sum} = 12) = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.0278
$$

3. The unordered results are the 6 doubles plus the $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ pairs of
   different numbers: $6 + 15 = 21$. Treating them as equally likely would give
   $\frac{1}{21} \approx 0.0476$ for both sums.

**Answer.** $P(\text{sum} = 11) = \frac{1}{18}$ is twice $P(\text{sum} = 12) = \frac{1}{36}$.
The unordered model wrongly gives $\frac{1}{21}$ to both.

**Check.** The 21 unordered "probabilities" would be $\frac{1}{21}$ each, but the true
ones are $\frac{1}{36}$ for each of 6 doubles and $\frac{2}{36}$ for each of 15 others.
These add up: $6 \cdot \frac{1}{36} + 15 \cdot \frac{2}{36} = \frac{6 + 30}{36} = 1$.

### Example 2: Ordered or Unordered, Both Work

**Problem.** Draw 2 cards from a shuffled deck of 52, without putting the first one
back. Find the probability that both are aces, first with ordered outcomes, then with
unordered outcomes.

**Solution.**

1. **Ordered.** An outcome is a (first card, second card) pair with different cards.
   There are $52 \cdot 51 = 2652$ of them, all equally likely by the shuffle. Pairs of
   two aces: $4 \cdot 3 = 12$.

$$
P(\text{two aces}) = \frac{12}{2652} = \frac{1}{221}
$$

2. **Unordered.** An outcome is a set of 2 cards. There are
   $\binom{52}{2} = \frac{52 \cdot 51}{2} = 1326$ of them. Each set comes from exactly 2
   ordered pairs, so the sets are equally likely too. Sets of two aces:
   $\binom{4}{2} = 6$.

$$
P(\text{two aces}) = \frac{6}{1326} = \frac{1}{221}
$$

**Answer.** $\frac{1}{221} \approx 0.00452$ either way.

**Check.** When you draw **without replacement**, every unordered set comes from the
same number of ordered sequences ($2! = 2$ here), so both models are uniform. When
you draw **with replacement** (like two dice), a set with a repeated item comes from
fewer sequences, so the unordered model is not uniform. Just never mix them: an
ordered count on top and an unordered count on the bottom gives a wrong answer.

### Example 3: Testing Servers

**Problem.** A data center has 10 servers, and 4 of them have a hidden fault. An
engineer tests 3 servers chosen uniformly at random (every set of 3 equally likely).
What is the probability that exactly 2 of the tested servers are faulty?

**Solution.**

1. Total number of equally likely sets of 3: $\binom{10}{3} = 120$.
2. Favorable sets: choose 2 of the 4 faulty servers and 1 of the 6 good ones:
   $\binom{4}{2} \binom{6}{1} = 6 \cdot 6 = 36$.

$$
P(\text{exactly 2 faulty}) = \frac{36}{120} = 0.3
$$

**Answer.** $0.3$.

**Check.** The same kind of count for 0, 1, 2 and 3 faulty servers gives
$\frac{20}{120}$, $\frac{60}{120}$, $\frac{36}{120}$, $\frac{4}{120}$, which add to 1.
This pattern is the hypergeometric distribution of
[Module 07](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/README.md).

## Simulate It

We check Examples 1 and 2. For the dice we record only the unordered result, to see
that the 21 unordered results are not equally likely.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Roll two dice; record only the unordered result {smaller, larger}.
die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
low, high = np.minimum(die1, die2), np.maximum(die1, die2)
for a, b, exact in [(6, 6, 1 / 36), (5, 6, 2 / 36)]:
    estimate = np.mean((low == a) & (high == b))
    print(f"P({a},{b} unordered): simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"Unordered guess for both:          {1 / 21:.4f}")

# Draw 2 cards without replacement; aces are cards 0, 1, 2, 3.
first = rng.integers(0, 52, size=n_trials)
second = rng.integers(0, 51, size=n_trials)
second = second + (second >= first)        # skip the card already drawn
both_aces = (first < 4) & (second < 4)
print(f"P(two aces): simulation {both_aces.mean():.5f}, exact {1 / 221:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(6,6 unordered): simulation 0.0279, exact 0.0278
P(5,6 unordered): simulation 0.0550, exact 0.0556
Unordered guess for both:          0.0476
P(two aces): simulation 0.00451, exact 0.00452
```

The unordered result $\lbrace 5, 6 \rbrace$ really is about twice as common as
$\lbrace 6, 6 \rbrace$, and the guess $\frac{1}{21}$ fits neither.

## Common Mistakes

- **Counting outcomes that are not equally likely.** Unordered results with
  replacement (Leibniz), "0, 1 or 2 heads", or "win, lose or draw" are not equally
  likely in general. Go down to outcomes that have a real symmetry.
- **Mixing ordered and unordered counts.** The top and bottom of the fraction must
  count the same kind of outcome.
- **"Equally likely because I know nothing".** "Either it rains tomorrow or it does
  not, so 50%" is not a symmetry argument. Ignorance is not symmetry.
- **Trusting human "randomness".** People asked to pick a random number from 1 to 10
  choose 7 far too often, and many PINs are 1234. Equal chances need a real random
  process.
- **A "random whole number" with all values equally likely.** It does not exist (see
  "Why It Is True"). Say what distribution you mean.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** a correct shuffle (the Fisher-Yates algorithm) makes all $n!$
  orders equally likely. A common buggy shuffle does not (Practice 5).
- **AI and machine learning:** random train/test splits, uniform sampling of
  mini-batches, and random initial choices all rely on uniform probability over a
  finite set.
- **Cryptography:** a secret key must be uniform over all possible keys, so that
  guessing has chance $\frac{1}{\text{number of keys}}$.
- **Games and lotteries:** card games, dice games and lotteries are designed to have
  equally likely outcomes, so counting gives exact odds.
- **Everyday life:** before you count, ask "are these really equally likely?". It
  catches most mistakes in puzzles and news stories.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You pick one letter uniformly at random from the 11 letters of the word
PROBABILITY. What is the probability that it is a B?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The 11 letter positions are equally likely, and 2 of them hold a B:

$$P(\text{B}) = \frac{2}{11} \approx 0.182$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Flip 3 fair coins. Find $P(\text{exactly 2 heads})$. Why is the answer not
$\frac{1}{4}$, even though the number of heads can be 0, 1, 2 or 3?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The 8 sequences HHH, HHT, ..., TTT are equally likely. Exactly 2 heads: HHT, HTH, THH.

$$P(\text{exactly 2 heads}) = \frac{3}{8}$$

The four values 0, 1, 2, 3 are not equally likely: 0 and 3 heads have 1 sequence
each, and 1 and 2 heads have 3 sequences each.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A 5-card hand is dealt from a shuffled deck. What is the probability
that all 5 cards are hearts?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

All $\binom{52}{5} = 2{,}598{,}960$ hands are equally likely. All-heart hands:
$\binom{13}{5} = 1287$.

$$P = \frac{1287}{2598960} \approx 0.000495$$

About 1 hand in 2,000.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A friend says: "Pick a random positive whole number. The chance that
it is even is $\frac{1}{2}$." What is wrong with this, and what could the friend
sensibly mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There is no probability that makes all positive whole numbers equally likely: equal
weights $c$ would add up to $0$ (if $c = 0$) or to an unlimited amount (if $c \gt 0$),
never to 1. What the friend can sensibly mean is a limit: pick uniformly from
$1, \dots, N$, where the chance of "even" is $\frac{\lfloor N/2 \rfloor}{N}$, and this
tends to $\frac{1}{2}$ as $N$ grows. That limit is not the probability of an event in
one probability space.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A programmer shuffles a list of 3 items like this: for each position
$i = 1, 2, 3$, swap item $i$ with an item at a position chosen uniformly from all 3
positions. Prove that the 6 possible orders cannot all be equally likely.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The program makes 3 random choices, each from 3 positions, so there are
$3^3 = 27$ equally likely runs. Each run ends in one of the $3! = 6$ orders. If all 6
orders were equally likely, each would need $\frac{27}{6} = 4.5$ runs, which is not a
whole number. So some orders get more runs than others. (In fact 3 orders get 5 runs
and 3 orders get 4 runs.) The Fisher-Yates shuffle fixes this by choosing position
$j$ only from $i, \dots, n$, which gives exactly $n!$ equally likely runs.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Run the shuffle from Problem 5 many times and estimate the chance of
each order.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 270_000
counts = Counter()
for _ in range(n_trials):
    items = [1, 2, 3]
    for i in range(3):                      # naive shuffle: j from ALL positions
        j = rng.integers(0, 3)
        items[i], items[j] = items[j], items[i]
    counts[tuple(items)] += 1

for perm in sorted(counts):
    print(perm, f"{counts[perm] / n_trials:.4f}")
print(f"Uniform would be {1 / 6:.4f}; exact values are 4/27 = {4 / 27:.4f} or 5/27 = {5 / 27:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
(1, 2, 3) 0.1482
(1, 3, 2) 0.1861
(2, 1, 3) 0.1843
(2, 3, 1) 0.1860
(3, 1, 2) 0.1484
(3, 2, 1) 0.1471
Uniform would be 0.1667; exact values are 4/27 = 0.1481 or 5/27 = 0.1852
```

</details>

## Summary

- On a finite space with equally likely outcomes, the axioms force
  $P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) = \frac{1}{n}$ and $P(A) = \frac{\lvert A \rvert}{n}$.
- The rule needs a finite space: no probability makes infinitely many whole numbers
  equally likely.
- Equal chances need a reason (symmetry or a good random process), not ignorance.
- Choose outcomes at the right level of detail: ordered pairs of dice, not unordered
  ones. Count the same kind of outcome on top and bottom.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A) = \frac{\lvert A \rvert}{\lvert \Omega \rvert} \quad \text{when all outcomes of a finite } \Omega \text{ are equally likely}}
$$
