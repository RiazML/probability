# 3.9 Continuity of Probability

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 03: Axioms of Probability](README.md) / 3.9 Continuity of Probability

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [3.8 Geometric Probability](3.8-Geometric-Probability.md)

> **Big idea:** If events grow step by step (or shrink step by step), the probability of the final limit event is the limit of their probabilities, so questions about "forever" can be answered with finite calculations.

---

## Why It Matters

Many important events talk about the infinite: "a head **eventually** appears", "the
algorithm **eventually** stops", "the average **eventually** settles down". We cannot
compute these by counting, because they depend on infinitely many steps. **Continuity
of probability** lets us compute them as limits of simple finite events. It is also
the reason a CDF climbs to 1 ([5.4](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.4-Cumulative-Distribution-Function.md)),
and it is the key tool behind the strong law of large numbers and the Borel-Cantelli
lemmas later in the book.

## Intuition

Flip a fair coin again and again. Let $A_n$ be the event "at least one head in the
first $n$ flips". Only the sequence of all tails misses $A_n$, so:

- $P(A_1) = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$
- $P(A_2) = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = 0.75$
- $P(A_3) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = 0.875$
- $P(A_{10}) = 1 - \frac{1}{1024} \approx 0.999$

Each event contains the one before it: if you saw a head in the first 2 flips, you
saw one in the first 3. The events grow toward the event "a head appears at some
point". It feels natural that its probability is the limit of $0.5, 0.75, 0.875, \dots$,
which is 1. Continuity of probability says this feeling is correct, and it follows
from Axiom 3.

## Picture

```text
   Increasing events: each one contains the one before.

   +-----------------------------------------------------------+
   | A3                                                        |
   |    +---------------------------------------------+        |
   |    | A2                                          |        |
   |    |    +---------------------+                  |        |
   |    |    | A1                  |    A2 \ A1       |        |
   |    |    +---------------------+                  |        |
   |    +---------------------------------------------+        |
   |                                       A3 \ A2             |
   +-----------------------------------------------------------+

   P(A1) <= P(A2) <= P(A3) <= ...   climbs up to   P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ ...)

   The rings A1, A2 \ A1, A3 \ A2, ... do not overlap, so Axiom 3
   adds them up.
```

A growing chain of events is cut into non-overlapping rings, and Axiom 3 adds the
rings.

## Definition

A sequence of events is **increasing** if $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \dots$,
and its limit is the union $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n$ (the outcomes that are in
some $A_n$). It is **decreasing** if $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq A_3 \supseteq \dots$,
and its limit is the intersection $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n$ (the outcomes that are
in every $A_n$).

**Continuity of probability.** For an increasing sequence:

$$
P\left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n)
$$

For a decreasing sequence:

$$
P\left( \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n)
$$

**In words:** when events grow, the probability of "at some point" is the limit of
the growing probabilities; when events shrink, the probability of "every time" is the
limit of the shrinking probabilities.

By monotonicity ([3.3](3.3-Consequences-of-the-Axioms.md)), the numbers $P(A_n)$ go
up for an increasing sequence and down for a decreasing one, and they stay between 0
and 1. So the limits always exist.

## Why It Is True

**Increasing case.** Cut the growing chain into rings: $B_1 = A_1$ and
$B_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}$ for $n \ge 2$. Each $B_n$ holds the outcomes that enter
the chain at step $n$. So:

- the rings are pairwise disjoint (an outcome enters only once);
- $B_1 \cup \dots \cup B_n = A_n$ (everything that has entered by step $n$);
- $\bigcup_{n} B_n = \bigcup_{n} A_n$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \right) &= P\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right) && \text{(same union)} \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(B_k) && \text{(Axiom 3, the rings are disjoint)} \\
&= \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} P(B_k) && \text{(an infinite sum is the limit of partial sums)} \\
&= \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n) && \text{(finite additivity: } B_1 \cup \dots \cup B_n = A_n \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Decreasing case.** If the $A_n$ shrink, their complements $A_n^c$ grow. By De
Morgan's law, $\left( \bigcap_n A_n \right)^c = \bigcup_n A_n^c$. Use the increasing
case on the complements:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left( \bigcap_{n} A_n \right) &= 1 - P\left( \bigcup_{n} A_n^c \right) && \text{(complement rule and De Morgan)} \\
&= 1 - \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n^c) && \text{(increasing case)} \\
&= \lim_{n \to \infty} \left( 1 - P(A_n^c) \right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n) && \text{(complement rule)}
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Note:** The proof needs Axiom 3 for **infinitely many** events. In fact, for a
> rule that is finitely additive, continuity is equivalent to countable additivity.
> This is the deep reason Kolmogorov chose a countable Axiom 3.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Head Eventually Appears

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin forever. Show that a head eventually appears with
probability 1, and that "all flips are tails" has probability 0.

**Solution.**

1. Model: for any event that depends only on the first $n$ flips, count among the
   $2^n$ equally likely sequences of $n$ flips. (A probability on infinite sequences
   with this property exists; [Module 20](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/README.md) builds it.)
2. Let $A_n$ = "at least one head in the first $n$ flips". Only the all-tails sequence
   misses it, so:

$$
P(A_n) = 1 - \frac{1}{2^n}
$$

3. The $A_n$ increase, and their union is "a head appears at some point". By
   continuity:

$$
P(\text{a head eventually}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2^n} \right) = 1 - 0 = 1
$$

4. "All tails forever" is the complement, so its probability is $1 - 1 = 0$. You can
   also see it as the decreasing limit of $T_n$ = "the first $n$ flips are tails", with
   $P(T_n) = \frac{1}{2^n} \to 0$.

**Answer.** $P(\text{a head eventually}) = 1$ and $P(\text{all tails forever}) = 0$.

**Check.** "All tails forever" is a real outcome of the experiment, yet it has
probability 0. Events with probability 1 are said to happen **almost surely**. "Almost
surely" is not the same as "for sure".

### Example 2: Why a Single Point Has Probability Zero

**Problem.** $U$ is a uniform point in $[0, 1]$ ([3.8](3.8-Geometric-Probability.md)).
Use continuity to show that $P(U = 0.3) = 0$.

**Solution.**

1. Let $B_n$ be the event that $U$ lands within $\frac{1}{n}$ of $0.3$, the interval
   $\left( 0.3 - \frac{1}{n}, \ 0.3 + \frac{1}{n} \right)$. For $n \ge 4$ the interval is
   inside $[0, 1]$, so its probability is its length:

$$
P(B_n) = \frac{2}{n} \quad \text{for } n \ge 4
$$

2. As $n$ grows, the intervals shrink, so the $B_n$ decrease. The only point inside
   every one of them is $0.3$ itself: $\bigcap_n B_n = \lbrace U = 0.3 \rbrace$.
3. By continuity:

$$
P(U = 0.3) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2}{n} = 0
$$

**Answer.** $P(U = 0.3) = 0$, and the same proof works for every single point.

**Check.** This fits the geometric view: a point has length 0. But continuity gives a
general reason that also works when probability is not length, which is what
[Module 08](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/README.md) needs.

### Example 3: A Waiting Time Is Finite

**Problem.** A server's response time $T$ (in seconds) is always a finite number. Show
that $P(T \le n) \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$, for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ Then, if
$P(T \le n) = 1 - \frac{1}{(n+1)^2}$ is measured, find $P(T \gt n)$ for $n = 9$.

**Solution.**

1. Let $C_n = \lbrace T \le n \rbrace$. A response within $n$ seconds is also within $n + 1$
   seconds, so the $C_n$ increase.
2. Every outcome has some finite $T$, so it is in $C_n$ once $n \ge T$. So the union of
   all $C_n$ is all of $\Omega$.
3. By continuity, $\lim_{n \to \infty} P(T \le n) = P(\Omega) = 1$.
4. With the measured formula and the complement rule:

$$
P(T \gt 9) = 1 - P(T \le 9) = \frac{1}{(9+1)^2} = \frac{1}{100} = 0.01
$$

**Answer.** $P(T \le n) \to 1$ always; with this formula, $P(T \gt 9) = 0.01$.

**Check.** The formula agrees with continuity: $\frac{1}{(n+1)^2} \to 0$. A formula for
$P(T \le n)$ whose limit is **not** 1 would mean that some responses never come back.

## Simulate It

We watch both kinds of limits: the increasing events $A_n$ from Example 1 climb to 1,
and the shrinking events $B_n$ from Example 2 fall to 0.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Increasing events: A_n = "at least one head in the first n flips".
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(n_trials, 20))       # 1 = head
seen_head = np.cumsum(flips, axis=1) > 0               # column n-1 is A_n
for n in [1, 2, 5, 10, 20]:
    print(f"P(A_n), n = {n:>6,}: simulation {seen_head[:, n - 1].mean():.6f}, "
          f"exact {1 - 0.5 ** n:.6f}")

# Decreasing events: B_n = "U lands within 1/n of 0.3", for U uniform on [0, 1].
u = rng.uniform(0, 1, n_trials)
for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000]:
    print(f"P(B_n), n = {n:>6,}: simulation {np.mean(np.abs(u - 0.3) < 1 / n):.6f}, "
          f"exact {2 / n:.6f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A_n), n =      1: simulation 0.500207, exact 0.500000
P(A_n), n =      2: simulation 0.750259, exact 0.750000
P(A_n), n =      5: simulation 0.968487, exact 0.968750
P(A_n), n =     10: simulation 0.999047, exact 0.999023
P(A_n), n =     20: simulation 0.999999, exact 0.999999
P(B_n), n =     10: simulation 0.199770, exact 0.200000
P(B_n), n =    100: simulation 0.020020, exact 0.020000
P(B_n), n =  1,000: simulation 0.001970, exact 0.002000
P(B_n), n = 10,000: simulation 0.000185, exact 0.000200
```

A simulation can only check finitely many steps, but the trend is clear: one sequence
heads to 1, the other to 0, just as continuity predicts.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using it on sequences that are not monotone.** If $A_n$ is "flip $n$ is heads",
  the events neither grow nor shrink, and "the limit event" has no meaning here.
  Check $A_n \subseteq A_{n+1}$ (or $\supseteq$) first.
- **Reading probability 1 as "certain".** "A head eventually appears" has probability
  1, but "all tails" is still a possible outcome. Probability 0 does not mean
  impossible.
- **Mixing up the two limits.** Increasing events go with the **union** ("at some
  point"); decreasing events go with the **intersection** ("every time").
- **Forgetting the model behind "forever".** Example 1 assumes that every block of
  $n$ flips has $2^n$ equally likely results. With a coin that can get stuck, the
  answer changes.

## Where It Is Used

- **Random variables:** the CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ tends to 1 as $x$ grows and to 0
  as $x$ falls, and it is right-continuous. All three facts are continuity of
  probability ([5.4 Cumulative Distribution Function](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.4-Cumulative-Distribution-Function.md)).
- **Computer science:** a randomized "retry until success" algorithm (a Las Vegas
  algorithm) stops with probability 1, by the same argument as Example 1.
- **Limit theorems:** the [strong law of large numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)
  and the [Borel-Cantelli lemmas](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)
  are about events defined by infinitely many steps, and continuity is how they are
  proved.
- **AI and machine learning:** guarantees that a random search or a stochastic training
  method "eventually" visits a good region are statements of this kind.
- **Everyday life:** if each attempt has a fixed positive chance to work, and attempts
  behave like fresh coin flips, you will almost surely succeed eventually. The useful
  question is how long it takes.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Roll a fair die again and again. Let $A_n$ = "at least one six in the
first $n$ rolls". Find $P(A_n)$ and its limit. Which event is the limit of the $A_n$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Of the $6^n$ equally likely sequences, $5^n$ have no six:

$$P(A_n) = 1 - \left( \tfrac{5}{6} \right)^n$$

The $A_n$ increase, and their union is "a six appears at some point". By continuity
its probability is $\lim_{n \to \infty} \left( 1 - (5/6)^n \right) = 1$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For each sequence, say whether it is increasing, decreasing, or neither.
(a) $A_n$ = "the first $n$ flips are all heads". (b) $B_n$ = "the server has crashed at
least once in the first $n$ days". (c) $C_n$ = "flip number $n$ is heads".

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Decreasing: if the first $n + 1$ flips are heads, so are the first $n$. So
$A_{n+1} \subseteq A_n$. (b) Increasing: a crash in the first $n$ days is also a crash
in the first $n + 1$ days. (c) Neither: "flip 3 is heads" neither contains nor is
contained in "flip 4 is heads".

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $(X, Y)$ is a uniform point in the unit square. Show that the
probability that it lies exactly on the diagonal $X = Y$ is 0.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For any whole number $n$, cut the square into an $n$ by $n$ grid. The diagonal passes
through the $n$ small squares on the grid's diagonal, so it is inside their union
$D_n$. Each small square has area $\frac{1}{n^2}$, so:

$$P(\text{on the diagonal}) \le P(D_n) = n \cdot \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{1}{n}$$

This holds for every $n$, and a probability is never negative, so the only possible
value is 0.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X$ is a random whole number that is always finite (for example, the
number of emails you get tomorrow). Show that $P(X \gt n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The events $D_n = \lbrace X \gt n \rbrace$ decrease: if $X \gt n + 1$ then $X \gt n$. An
outcome lies in every $D_n$ only if $X$ is bigger than every whole number, which never
happens, so $\bigcap_n D_n = \varnothing$. By continuity:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(X \gt n) = P(\varnothing) = 0$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove the union bound for infinitely many events,
$P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)$, using only the
finite union bound from [3.6](3.6-Union-Bound.md) and continuity.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $C_n = A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n$. The $C_n$ increase, and their union is
$\bigcup_{i} A_i$. By the finite union bound, $P(C_n) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)$
for every $n$ (the terms are non-negative). By continuity:

$$P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(C_n) \le \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)$$

The limit of numbers that are all at most some value is also at most that value.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 1: estimate $P(A_n)$ for $n = 1, 5, 10, 20, 50$ and
compare with $1 - \left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^n$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The roll number of the first six follows the "trials until the first success" pattern
from [3.2](3.2-Kolmogorov-Axioms.md), with success chance $\frac{1}{6}$, and $A_n$ is
"the first six comes at roll $n$ or earlier".

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
first_six = rng.geometric(1 / 6, size=n_trials)   # roll number of the first six
for n in [1, 5, 10, 20, 50]:
    print(f"n = {n:2d}: simulation {np.mean(first_six <= n):.4f}, "
          f"exact {1 - (5 / 6) ** n:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =  1: simulation 0.1665, exact 0.1667
n =  5: simulation 0.5983, exact 0.5981
n = 10: simulation 0.8385, exact 0.8385
n = 20: simulation 0.9741, exact 0.9739
n = 50: simulation 0.9999, exact 0.9999
```

</details>

## Summary

- Increasing events: $P(\bigcup A_n) = \lim P(A_n)$. Decreasing events:
  $P(\bigcap A_n) = \lim P(A_n)$.
- The proof cuts a growing chain into disjoint rings and uses Axiom 3; the shrinking
  case follows by taking complements.
- It turns "eventually" and "forever" questions into limits of finite calculations.
- Probability 1 means "almost surely", not "certainly"; probability 0 does not mean
  impossible.
- It explains why single points have probability 0 for a uniform point and why a CDF
  climbs to 1.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \dots \implies P\left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n)}
$$
