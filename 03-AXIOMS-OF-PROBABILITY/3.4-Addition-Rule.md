# 3.4 Addition Rule

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 03: Axioms of Probability](README.md) / 3.4 Addition Rule

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [3.3 Consequences of the Axioms](3.3-Consequences-of-the-Axioms.md)

> **Big idea:** To find the chance of "A or B", add the two chances and then subtract the chance of "A and B", because the overlap was counted twice.

---

## Why It Matters

Axiom 3 lets us add probabilities only for events that **cannot** happen together.
Most real events can overlap: an email can have a link **and** an attachment; a card
can be a heart **and** a face card. The addition rule handles every pair of events,
overlapping or not. It is also the first step toward
[inclusion-exclusion](3.5-Inclusion-Exclusion-for-Probability.md) for many events and
the [union bound](3.6-Union-Bound.md).

## Intuition

A class has 40 students. 18 play cricket, 15 play football, and 7 play both. How
many play at least one sport?

If you add $18 + 15 = 33$, you count the 7 students who play both **twice**: once as
cricket players and once as football players. Subtract them once:
$33 - 7 = 26$ students play at least one sport.

Now pick a student at random. The chance that they play at least one sport is
$\frac{26}{40} = 0.65$. In probabilities:
$\frac{18}{40} + \frac{15}{40} - \frac{7}{40} = 0.45 + 0.375 - 0.175 = 0.65$.
Same idea, same answer. Counting this way is the
[inclusion-exclusion principle](../02-COUNTING/2.8-Inclusion-Exclusion.md) from
Module 02; here we prove it for **any** probability, not only for counts.

## Picture

```text
   Ω
  +--------------------------------------------------+
  |     +-------------------+                        |
  |     |  A only     +-----+--------------+         |
  |     |             |A ∩ B|    B only    |         |
  |     +-------------+-----+              |         |
  |                   +--------------------+         |
  +--------------------------------------------------+

   P(A) + P(B) counts the middle piece A ∩ B twice,
   so subtract it once:   P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)
```

The union is made of three disjoint pieces, and the shared middle piece belongs to
both $A$ and $B$.

## Definition

The **addition rule** (also called the **inclusion-exclusion rule for two events**)
says that for **any** two events $A$ and $B$:

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
$$

where:

- $P(A \cup B)$ is the probability that $A$ happens, or $B$ happens, or both;
- $P(A \cap B)$ is the probability that both happen.

**In words:** the chance of "A or B" is the chance of A plus the chance of B, minus
the chance of both, which was counted twice.

If $A$ and $B$ are **disjoint**, then $A \cap B = \varnothing$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0$,
so the rule becomes Axiom 3 for two events: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

Two useful rules come with it. The chance of **neither** event is, by the complement
rule and De Morgan's law $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$:

$$
P(A^c \cap B^c) = 1 - P(A \cup B)
$$

**In words:** "neither A nor B" is the complement of "A or B".

The chance of **exactly one** of the two is the union minus the overlap:
$P(\text{exactly one}) = P(A \cup B) - P(A \cap B)$.

## Why It Is True

Cut $A \cup B$ into two disjoint pieces: all of $A$, and the part of $B$ outside $A$.
So $A \cup B = A \cup (B \setminus A)$, with $A \cap (B \setminus A) = \varnothing$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \cup B) &= P(A) + P(B \setminus A) && \text{(finite additivity, disjoint pieces)} \\
&= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) && \text{(difference rule from 3.3)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The difference rule $P(B \setminus A) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)$ was proved in
[3.3](3.3-Consequences-of-the-Axioms.md). That is the whole proof.

**Two bounds come for free.** Since $P(A \cap B) \ge 0$, the rule gives
$P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)$. Since $P(A \cup B) \le 1$, it gives a lower bound on
the overlap:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1
$$

**In words:** if two events are both very likely, they must overlap a lot. This is
called **Bonferroni's inequality**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Heart or Face Card

**Problem.** Draw one card from a shuffled deck of 52. What is the probability that
it is a heart or a face card (jack, queen or king)?

**Solution.**

1. Let $A$ = "heart" and $B$ = "face card". All 52 cards are equally likely.
2. There are 13 hearts, so $P(A) = \frac{13}{52}$.
3. There are 3 face cards in each of 4 suits, so 12 face cards: $P(B) = \frac{12}{52}$.
4. The cards that are both are the jack, queen and king of hearts:
   $P(A \cap B) = \frac{3}{52}$.
5. Apply the addition rule:

$$
P(A \cup B) = \frac{13}{52} + \frac{12}{52} - \frac{3}{52} = \frac{22}{52} = \frac{11}{26} \approx 0.4231
$$

**Answer.** $\frac{11}{26} \approx 0.423$.

**Check.** Count directly: 13 hearts plus the 9 face cards that are not hearts gives
$13 + 9 = 22$ cards. The simulation below also gives about $0.423$.

### Example 2: Links and Attachments in Email

**Problem.** In a company's email, 40% of messages contain a link, 25% contain an
attachment, and 10% contain both. A spam filter looks closely at any message with a
link or an attachment. What fraction of messages does it look at closely? What
fraction has neither? What fraction has exactly one of the two?

**Solution.**

1. Let $L$ = "has a link" and $T$ = "has an attachment": $P(L) = 0.40$,
   $P(T) = 0.25$, $P(L \cap T) = 0.10$.
2. Addition rule:

$$
P(L \cup T) = 0.40 + 0.25 - 0.10 = 0.55
$$

3. Neither:

$$
P(L^c \cap T^c) = 1 - P(L \cup T) = 1 - 0.55 = 0.45
$$

4. Exactly one: $P(L \cup T) - P(L \cap T) = 0.55 - 0.10 = 0.45$.

**Answer.** The filter looks closely at 55% of messages. 45% have neither, and 45%
have exactly one.

**Check.** The four disjoint groups are "both" $0.10$, "link only"
$0.40 - 0.10 = 0.30$, "attachment only" $0.25 - 0.10 = 0.15$, and "neither" $0.45$.
They add to $0.10 + 0.30 + 0.15 + 0.45 = 1$.

### Example 3: Using the Rule Backwards

**Problem.** In an exam, 70% of students pass mathematics and 60% pass physics.
(a) If 85% pass at least one subject, what fraction pass both? (b) Without knowing
the 85%, what is the smallest possible fraction that passes both?

**Solution.**

1. Let $M$ = "passes mathematics" and $F$ = "passes physics". Solve the addition
   rule for the overlap:

$$
P(M \cap F) = P(M) + P(F) - P(M \cup F) = 0.70 + 0.60 - 0.85 = 0.45
$$

2. For (b), use Bonferroni's inequality:

$$
P(M \cap F) \ge P(M) + P(F) - 1 = 0.70 + 0.60 - 1 = 0.30
$$

**Answer.** (a) 45% pass both. (b) At least 30% must pass both, whatever the data.

**Check.** In (b) the smallest overlap happens when $P(M \cup F) = 1$: everyone
passes at least one subject, and $0.70 + 0.60 - 1 = 0.30$.

## Simulate It

We draw a million cards and check Example 1, including the wrong answer that forgets
to subtract the overlap.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Draw one card: number 0..51. Suit = card // 13, rank = card % 13.
card = rng.integers(0, 52, size=n_trials)
is_heart = (card // 13) == 0
is_face = (card % 13) >= 10            # ranks 10, 11, 12 = jack, queen, king

p_a, p_b = is_heart.mean(), is_face.mean()
p_both = (is_heart & is_face).mean()
p_union = (is_heart | is_face).mean()

print(f"P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B) = {p_a + p_b - p_both:.4f}")
print(f"P(A or B), counted       = {p_union:.4f}")
print(f"P(A) + P(B) (wrong)      = {p_a + p_b:.4f}")
print(f"Exact 22/52              = {22 / 52:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B) = 0.4229
P(A or B), counted       = 0.4229
P(A) + P(B) (wrong)      = 0.4807
Exact 22/52              = 0.4231
```

The first two lines agree exactly: the addition rule is true for frequencies too.
Forgetting the overlap gives about $0.48 = \frac{25}{52}$, which is too big.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the overlap.** $P(A) + P(B)$ is the answer only for disjoint events.
  A quick alarm: if $P(A) + P(B) \gt 1$, the events must overlap.
- **Assuming events are disjoint.** "Heart" and "face card" look like different
  categories, but they share 3 cards. Always ask: can both happen at once?
- **Multiplying to get the overlap.** $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B)$ is true only for
  **independent** events ([4.7 Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)).
  The addition rule itself does not need independence.
- **Mixing up "at least one" and "exactly one".** "A or B" includes "both".
  "Exactly one" leaves "both" out: subtract $P(A \cap B)$ once more.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a fraud system flags a payment if rule A or rule B
  fires. The addition rule gives the fraction of payments flagged, from each rule's
  rate and their overlap.
- **Computer science:** a search engine returns a page if the query matches the
  title or the body. Caches: a request is served fast if the data is in cache 1 or
  cache 2.
- **Medicine:** the chance that a patient has symptom A or symptom B, from survey
  rates of each and of both.
- **Everyday life:** "Will I be late because of traffic or because of rain?" You
  cannot just add the two chances, because rain and heavy traffic often come
  together.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.4$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$. Find $P(A \cup B)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.7$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Roll a fair die. What is the probability that the result is even or
greater than 3?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Even is $\lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$ and greater than 3 is $\lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$. Both is
$\lbrace 4, 6 \rbrace$:

$$P = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} - \frac{2}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$$

Check: the union is $\lbrace 2, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$, which has 4 outcomes.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $P(A) = 0.6$ and $P(B) = 0.7$, and nothing else is known. What are the
smallest and largest possible values of $P(A \cap B)$? Of $P(A \cup B)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Overlap: by Bonferroni, $P(A \cap B) \ge 0.6 + 0.7 - 1 = 0.3$. By monotonicity,
$P(A \cap B) \le \min(0.6, 0.7) = 0.6$. So $0.3 \le P(A \cap B) \le 0.6$.

Union: $P(A \cup B) = 1.3 - P(A \cap B)$, so it goes from $1.3 - 0.6 = 0.7$ up to
$1.3 - 0.3 = 1$. So $0.7 \le P(A \cup B) \le 1$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In an app, 30% of users turn on two-factor login, 50% use a password
manager, and 20% do both. What fraction does neither? What fraction does exactly
one?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{at least one}) = 0.30 + 0.50 - 0.20 = 0.60$$

$$P(\text{neither}) = 1 - 0.60 = 0.40$$

$$P(\text{exactly one}) = 0.60 - 0.20 = 0.40$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for any events $A$ and $B$,
$P(\text{exactly one of } A, B) = P(A) + P(B) - 2 P(A \cap B)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"Exactly one" is $(A \setminus B) \cup (B \setminus A)$, and these two pieces are
disjoint. By finite additivity and the difference rule from 3.3:

$$P(\text{exactly one}) = P(A \setminus B) + P(B \setminus A) = (P(A) - P(A \cap B)) + (P(B) - P(A \cap B)) = P(A) + P(B) - 2 P(A \cap B)$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Roll two fair dice. Let $A$ = "the first die shows 6" and
$B$ = "the sum is at least 10". Find $P(A \cup B)$ exactly, then check it with a
simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(A) = \frac{6}{36}$. The sum is at least 10 for $(4,6), (5,5), (6,4), (5,6), (6,5), (6,6)$,
so $P(B) = \frac{6}{36}$. Both happen for $(6,4), (6,5), (6,6)$, so
$P(A \cap B) = \frac{3}{36}$:

$$P(A \cup B) = \frac{6}{36} + \frac{6}{36} - \frac{3}{36} = \frac{9}{36} = 0.25$$

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
first_is_six = die1 == 6
sum_at_least_10 = die1 + die2 >= 10
estimate = np.mean(first_is_six | sum_at_least_10)
print(f"Simulation: {estimate:.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {9 / 36:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.2492
Exact:      0.2500
```

</details>

## Summary

- For **any** two events, $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
- For disjoint events the overlap is 0, and the rule is Axiom 3.
- "Neither" is $1 - P(A \cup B)$; "exactly one" is $P(A \cup B) - P(A \cap B)$.
- Bounds: $P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)$, and $P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)}
$$
