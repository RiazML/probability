# 3.6 Union Bound

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 03: Axioms of Probability](README.md) / 3.6 Union Bound

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [3.5 Inclusion-Exclusion for Probability](3.5-Inclusion-Exclusion-for-Probability.md)

> **Big idea:** The chance that at least one of several events happens is never more than the sum of their chances, and this bound needs no information at all about how the events overlap.

---

## Why It Matters

[Inclusion-exclusion](3.5-Inclusion-Exclusion-for-Probability.md) is exact, but it
needs the probability of every intersection, which we rarely know. The **union
bound** needs only the single probabilities. It is one of the most used tools in
computer science and machine learning: to show that "everything goes right" with
high probability, you bound the chance that "something goes wrong" by adding up the
chances of each separate failure.

## Intuition

A machine has 100 parts. Each part fails today with probability 0.001. What is the
chance that at least one part fails?

If the failures never happened together, the answer would be
$100 \times 0.001 = 0.1$. If they sometimes happen together (a power surge can break
several parts at once), the overlaps make the chance of "at least one" **smaller**,
because a day with two failures is still only one bad day. So whatever the
connection between the parts, the chance is at most 0.1. That is the union bound.
In return, the machine works perfectly today with probability at least
$1 - 0.1 = 0.9$.

## Picture

```text
      +--------+
      |   A1   |                 +--------+
      |    +---+----+            |   A3   |
      +----+---+    |            +--------+
           |   A2   |
           +--------+

   P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ A3)  <=  P(A1) + P(A2) + P(A3)

   The right side counts the overlap of A1 and A2 twice.
   The left side counts it once. So the right side is never smaller.
```

Adding the sizes of overlapping pieces can only over-count the size of their union.

## Definition

**Boole's inequality**, usually called the **union bound**: for any events
$A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$,

$$
P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)
$$

The same holds for an infinite sequence $A_1, A_2, \dots$, with infinite sums.

**In words:** the chance that at least one of the events happens is at most the sum
of their separate chances.

The events do **not** need to be disjoint or independent. Equality holds when the
events do not overlap (more exactly, when every $P(A_i \cap A_j) = 0$).

Taking complements gives the form most often used in practice. Think of each $A_i$
as a bad event, such as "part number $i$ fails":

$$
P(\text{no bad event happens}) = P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i^c \right) \ge 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)
$$

**In words:** everything goes right with probability at least 1 minus the sum of
the failure chances.

## Why It Is True

**Short proof for two events, then induction.** The [addition rule](3.4-Addition-Rule.md)
gives $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \le P(A) + P(B)$, because
$P(A \cap B) \ge 0$. For more events, group them:
$P(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n) \le P(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{n-1}) + P(A_n)$, and repeat.

**Proof that also works for infinitely many events.** Make the events disjoint by
removing from each one everything that came before it:

$$
B_1 = A_1, \qquad B_i = A_i \setminus (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1}) \ \text{ for } i \ge 2
$$

Each $B_i$ holds the outcomes whose **first** event is $A_i$. So the $B_i$ are
pairwise disjoint, they have the same union as the $A_i$, and $B_i \subseteq A_i$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left( \bigcup_{i} A_i \right) &= P\left( \bigcup_{i} B_i \right) && \text{(same union)} \\
&= \sum_{i} P(B_i) && \text{(Axiom 3, the } B_i \text{ are disjoint)} \\
&\le \sum_{i} P(A_i) && \text{(monotonicity: } B_i \subseteq A_i \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The complement form.** By De Morgan's law, "no $A_i$ happens" is the complement of
"some $A_i$ happens":

$$
P\left( \bigcap_{i} A_i^c \right) = 1 - P\left( \bigcup_{i} A_i \right) \ge 1 - \sum_{i} P(A_i)
$$

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Shared Birthdays

**Problem.** Assume each person's birthday is equally likely to be any of 365 days,
and different people's birthdays are unrelated. Use the union bound to bound the
probability that some two people share a birthday, for 5 people and for 23 people.
Compare with the exact values $0.0271$ and $0.5073$ from the
[birthday problem](../02-COUNTING/2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md).

**Solution.**

1. For each pair of people $(i, j)$, let $A_{ij}$ = "persons $i$ and $j$ share a
   birthday". Whatever day person $i$ has, person $j$ matches it with probability
   $\frac{1}{365}$, so $P(A_{ij}) = \frac{1}{365}$.
2. "Some two people share" is the union of all $A_{ij}$. With $n$ people there are
   $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs.
3. For $n = 5$: $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs, so:

$$
P(\text{shared birthday}) \le \frac{10}{365} \approx 0.0274
$$

4. For $n = 23$: $\binom{23}{2} = \frac{23 \cdot 22}{2} = 253$ pairs, so:

$$
P(\text{shared birthday}) \le \frac{253}{365} \approx 0.693
$$

**Answer.** At most $0.0274$ for 5 people (exact $0.0271$), and at most $0.693$ for 23
people (exact $0.507$).

**Check.** The bound is very close when the total is small, because two pair events
rarely happen together. For 23 people, overlaps are common (three people on one day,
or two separate matches), so the bound over-counts more. The bound is always above
the exact value, as it must be.

### Example 2: Hash Collisions

**Problem.** A program stores 10,000 keys using a 32-bit hash function. Model the hash
of each key as a uniformly random value among $2^{32} = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296$ values,
unrelated for different keys. Bound the probability that two different keys get the
same hash (a **collision**).

**Solution.**

1. For each pair of keys, a collision has probability $\frac{1}{2^{32}}$, by the same
   reason as in Example 1.
2. The number of pairs is:

$$
\binom{10000}{2} = \frac{10000 \cdot 9999}{2} = 49{,}995{,}000
$$

3. Union bound:

$$
P(\text{some collision}) \le \frac{49{,}995{,}000}{4{,}294{,}967{,}296} \approx 0.0116
$$

**Answer.** At most about $0.0116$, a bit more than 1%.

**Check.** The exact value under this model is about $0.0116$ too (to three
significant digits, $0.01157$ against the bound $0.01164$). In general, $n$ keys in
$m$ slots give $P(\text{collision}) \le \frac{n(n-1)}{2m} \lt \frac{n^2}{2m}$, so
collisions become likely once $n$ is about $\sqrt{m}$. For 32-bit hashes that is
about 65,536 keys.

## Simulate It

We simulate birthdays for 5 and 23 people and compare the exact value with the union
bound.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

for n_people in [5, 23]:
    # Birthdays uniform over 365 days; look for any shared day.
    days = np.sort(rng.integers(0, 365, size=(n_trials, n_people)), axis=1)
    shared = np.any(days[:, 1:] == days[:, :-1], axis=1)
    exact = 1 - np.prod((365 - np.arange(n_people)) / 365)
    bound = comb(n_people, 2) / 365        # union bound over all pairs
    print(f"n = {n_people:2d}: simulation {shared.mean():.4f}, "
          f"exact {exact:.4f}, union bound {bound:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =  5: simulation 0.0270, exact 0.0271, union bound 0.0274
n = 23: simulation 0.5077, exact 0.5073, union bound 0.6932
```

The union bound is tight for small probabilities and loose for large ones, but it is
never below the truth.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the bound as the answer.** The union bound gives "at most", not "equal".
  For 23 people it says $0.693$ while the truth is $0.507$.
- **Using it when the sum is above 1.** If $\sum P(A_i) \ge 1$, the bound says
  nothing new, since every probability is at most 1. Then you need inclusion-exclusion
  or a better model.
- **Thinking it needs independence.** It needs nothing: no independence, no
  disjointness. That is its strength. The price is that it can be very loose when
  the events overlap a lot. In the extreme case where all $A_i$ are the same event
  $A$, the bound gives $n \thinspace P(A)$ while the truth is $P(A)$.
- **Counting the events wrong.** With $n$ people there are $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$
  pairs, not $n^2$ and not $n$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** in learning theory, if each of $H$ possible models
  could fool you with probability at most $\delta$, then **some** model fools you
  with probability at most $H \delta$. This is how generalization bounds are built
  (see [16.5 Union Bound Revisited](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.5-Union-Bound-Revisited.md)).
- **Computer science:** "with high probability" proofs for randomized algorithms:
  if each of $n$ steps fails with probability at most $\frac{1}{n^2}$, then some step
  fails with probability at most $\frac{1}{n}$. Hashing and Bloom filters use the same idea.
- **Statistics:** the **Bonferroni correction**. If you run 20 tests and want the chance
  of any false alarm to be at most $0.05$, run each test at level $\frac{0.05}{20} = 0.0025$.
- **Engineering:** a lower bound on the chance that a system with many parts works,
  even when failures are related.
- **Everyday life:** many small risks add up. Ten habits that each carry a 1% risk
  can carry up to a 10% risk together (see
  [22.12 Compounding Small Risks](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A factory has 50 sensors. Each one gives a false alarm on a given day
with probability $0.002$. Bound the probability of at least one false alarm today.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{at least one false alarm}) \le 50 \times 0.002 = 0.1$$

This holds whether or not the sensors' alarms are related.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $P(A) = 0.3$, $P(B) = 0.4$, $P(C) = 0.5$. What does the union bound say
about $P(A \cup B \cup C)$? What can you say instead?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The union bound gives $0.3 + 0.4 + 0.5 = 1.2$, which is useless because every
probability is at most 1. What we do know: $C \subseteq A \cup B \cup C$, so by
monotonicity $0.5 \le P(A \cup B \cup C) \le 1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A data scientist runs 10 tests. She wants the chance of at least one
false alarm, over all 10 tests, to be at most $0.05$. What false-alarm chance should
each single test have?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If each test has false-alarm chance $\alpha$, the union bound gives
$P(\text{any false alarm}) \le 10 \alpha$. Setting $10 \alpha = 0.05$:

$$\alpha = \frac{0.05}{10} = 0.005$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A chip has $10^6$ transistors, each defective with probability $10^{-8}$.
Give a lower bound on the probability that the chip has no defect. How small must the
defect rate per transistor be to guarantee at least $0.999$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{no defect}) \ge 1 - 10^6 \cdot 10^{-8} = 1 - 0.01 = 0.99$$

For $0.999$ we need $10^6 \cdot q \le 0.001$, so $q \le 10^{-9}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for finitely many events, the union bound is an equality if
and only if $P(A_i \cap A_j) = 0$ for every pair $i \ne j$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the disjoint pieces $B_i = A_i \setminus (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1})$ from the
proof. Then $P(\bigcup A_i) = \sum P(B_i)$, and by the difference rule
$P(A_i) - P(B_i) = P(A_i \cap (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1}))$. So:

$$\sum_i P(A_i) - P\left( \bigcup_i A_i \right) = \sum_i P(A_i \cap (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1}))$$

Every term on the right is $\ge 0$, so equality holds exactly when every term is 0.

If all pairs have probability 0: by the union bound,
$P(A_i \cap (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1})) \le \sum_{j \lt i} P(A_i \cap A_j) = 0$, so
every term is 0.

If every term is 0: for $j \lt i$, $A_i \cap A_j \subseteq A_i \cap (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1})$,
so by monotonicity $P(A_i \cap A_j) = 0$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Hash 100 keys into 100,000 buckets, uniformly at random. Simulate the
chance of a collision and compare it with the exact value and the union bound.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The union bound is $\binom{100}{2} / 100000 = \frac{4950}{100000} = 0.0495$.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_keys, n_buckets, n_trials = 100, 100_000, 200_000
hashes = np.sort(rng.integers(0, n_buckets, size=(n_trials, n_keys)), axis=1)
collision = np.any(hashes[:, 1:] == hashes[:, :-1], axis=1)
exact = 1 - np.prod((n_buckets - np.arange(n_keys)) / n_buckets)
print(f"Simulation:  {collision.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:       {exact:.4f}")
print(f"Union bound: {comb(n_keys, 2) / n_buckets:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation:  0.0486
Exact:       0.0483
Union bound: 0.0495
```

</details>

## Summary

- Union bound: $P(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n) \le P(A_1) + \dots + P(A_n)$, for any events.
- It needs no independence and no knowledge of overlaps.
- It is tight when the events rarely happen together, and loose (or useless, above 1)
  when they overlap a lot.
- Complement form: everything goes right with probability at least
  $1 - \sum P(\text{failure}_i)$.
- It is the first step of inclusion-exclusion, and a key tool in algorithms and
  learning theory.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)}
$$
