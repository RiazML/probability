# 11.7 Linear Transformations

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 11: Covariance and Correlation](README.md) / 11.7 Linear Transformations

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [11.6 Covariance Matrix is PSD](11.6-Covariance-Matrix-is-PSD.md)

> **Big idea:** If you mix the parts of a random vector with a fixed matrix and add a fixed shift, the new mean is the old mean pushed through the same map, and the new covariance matrix is A times Σ times A-transpose.

---

## Why It Matters

Data is transformed all the time: we change units, add features together, build
portfolios, rotate data for PCA, and turn plain random numbers into correlated
ones for simulations. Each new variable is a weighted sum of the old ones, plus a
constant. You could find every new variance and covariance one by one with
[11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md), but one matrix rule does them all at once:
$\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}) = A \Sigma A^{\top}$. It is the
key step of the [Kalman filter](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/README.md) and of
everything in [12.5 Affine Transformations](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.5-Affine-Transformations.md).

## Intuition

A school has two exam scores for each student: math $X_1$ and English $X_2$. The
math scores have variance $4$, the English scores variance $9$, and their
covariance is $2$. For a report, the school makes two new numbers:

- the total, $Y_1 = X_1 + X_2$, and
- the gap, $Y_2 = X_1 - X_2$ (how much better at math than at English).

What are the variances of the total and the gap, and do they move together? Each
new number is a recipe: "1 of math plus 1 of English", "1 of math minus 1 of
English". Write the recipes as the rows of a table of weights, called a matrix $A$.
The rule of this topic takes $A$ and the old covariance matrix and returns the whole
new covariance matrix in one multiplication. (The answer, in Example 1: total
variance $17$, gap variance $9$, and covariance $-5$.)

## Picture

```text
     Z: round cloud, Cov = I            X = A Z: tilted cloud, Cov = A Aᵀ

        |     .  .                                   |          . .
        |  .  . .  . .                               |       . . . .
        | . .. . . .. .       ----- A ----->         |     . .. . .
   -----+-.-.-.-.-.-.-.----                     -----+---. . . .------
        | . .. . . .. .                              | . .. . .
        |  .  . .  . .                               |. . . .
        |     .  .                                   | . .
```

A matrix stretches and turns the cloud. The mean moves to $A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$,
and the shape of the cloud is described by $A \Sigma A^{\top}$.

## Definition

### Two More Matrix Tools

**A matrix times a vector** is a list of weighted sums: entry $i$ of $A\mathbf{x}$ is
row $i$ of $A$ times $\mathbf{x}$. For example:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \cr x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + x_2 \cr x_1 - x_2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

So "total and gap" from the Intuition is just $A\mathbf{x}$ with this $A$.

**The transpose of a product** reverses the order:
$(A\mathbf{v})^{\top} = \mathbf{v}^{\top} A^{\top}$, and in general
$(AB)^{\top} = B^{\top} A^{\top}$. Check it on the example: $A\mathbf{x}$ above, laid
on its side, is the row $(x_1 + x_2, \thinspace x_1 - x_2)$, and that is exactly
$\mathbf{x}^{\top} A^{\top}$ (this $A$ is symmetric, so $A^{\top} = A$).

### The Rule

Let $\mathbf{X}$ be a random vector with $n$ entries, mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and
covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Let $A$ be a fixed $m \times n$ matrix and $\mathbf{b}$
a fixed vector with $m$ entries. Define the new random vector:

$$
\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}
$$

This is called a **linear** (strictly, **affine**) **transformation**: each entry of
$\mathbf{Y}$ is a weighted sum of the entries of $\mathbf{X}$ plus a constant. Then:

$$
E[\mathbf{Y}] = A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) = A \Sigma A^{\top}
$$

where $A\Sigma A^{\top}$ is an $m \times m$ matrix.

**In words:** the mean goes through the map like an ordinary point. The covariance
gets $A$ on the left and $A^{\top}$ on the right, and the shift $\mathbf{b}$ plays no
part in it.

Three special cases you already know:

| Case | $A$ | Rule gives |
|---|---|---|
| One variable | the number $a$ | $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$ |
| One weighted sum | the row $\mathbf{a}^{\top}$ | $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$ ([11.6](11.6-Covariance-Matrix-is-PSD.md)) |
| Sum of two | the row $(1, 1)$ | $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = \Sigma_{11} + 2\Sigma_{12} + \Sigma_{22}$ ([11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)) |

## Why It Is True

**The mean.** Entry $i$ of $\mathbf{Y}$ is $Y_i = \sum_{j} A_{ij} X_j + b_i$. By
[linearity of expectation](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md),
$E[Y_i] = \sum_{j} A_{ij} \mu_j + b_i$, which is entry $i$ of $A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$.

**The covariance.** Start from the definition in [11.5](11.5-Random-Vectors-and-Covariance-Matrix.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{Y} - E[\mathbf{Y}] &= (A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}) - (A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}) = A(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(the shift cancels)} \\
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) &= E\big[(\mathbf{Y} - E[\mathbf{Y}])(\mathbf{Y} - E[\mathbf{Y}])^{\top}\big] && \text{(definition)} \\
&= E\big[A(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \thinspace (A(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu}))^{\top}\big] && \text{(first line)} \\
&= E\big[A(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top} A^{\top}\big] && \text{(transpose of a product)} \\
&= A \thinspace E\big[(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\big] \thinspace A^{\top} && \text{(constants come out)} \\
&= A \Sigma A^{\top} && \text{(definition of } \Sigma \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Why can the constant matrices come out of the expectation? Each entry of
$A M A^{\top}$ is a fixed weighted sum of the entries of $M$, and expectation
passes through weighted sums by linearity.

**A check with sums.** Entry $(i, k)$ of $A\Sigma A^{\top}$ is
$\sum_{j} \sum_{l} A_{ij} A_{kl} \Sigma_{jl}$. By bilinearity from
[11.1](11.1-Covariance.md), that is exactly
$\operatorname{Cov}\big(\sum_{j} A_{ij} X_j, \ \sum_{l} A_{kl} X_l\big) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y_i, Y_k)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Total and Gap of Two Exam Scores

**Problem.** Math and English scores $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2)^{\top}$ have the mean
and covariance below. Find the mean and covariance matrix of the total
$Y_1 = X_1 + X_2$ and the gap $Y_2 = X_1 - X_2$.

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cr 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \cr 2 & 9 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

(The scores are measured from a class baseline, so the means are small.)

**Solution.**

1. Mean: $A\boldsymbol{\mu}$ has entries $1 + 2 = 3$ and $1 - 2 = -1$.
2. First multiply $A\Sigma$. Row 1 of $A$ is $(1, 1)$: it adds the rows of $\Sigma$, giving $(6, 11)$. Row 2 is $(1, -1)$: it subtracts them, giving $(2, -7)$.
3. Then multiply by $A^{\top}$ on the right. Since $A^{\top} = A$ here, column 1 adds the two entries of each row and column 2 subtracts them:

$$
A\Sigma A^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 11 \cr 2 & -7 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 + 11 & 6 - 11 \cr 2 - 7 & 2 + 7 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 17 & -5 \cr -5 & 9 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Answer.** $E[\mathbf{Y}] = (3, -1)^{\top}$. The total has variance $17$, the gap
has variance $9$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(\text{total}, \text{gap}) = -5$.

**Check.** One entry at a time, with the older formulas:
$\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = 4 + 9 + 2 \cdot 2 = 17$,
$\operatorname{Var}(X_1 - X_2) = 4 + 9 - 2 \cdot 2 = 9$, and
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1 + X_2, X_1 - X_2) = \operatorname{Var}(X_1) - \operatorname{Var}(X_2) = 4 - 9 = -5$
(from [11.1](11.1-Covariance.md), Practice 4). All three agree.

### Example 2: Making Correlated Random Numbers

**Problem.** A simulation needs two random numbers, each with variance $1$, with
correlation $0.6$. The computer gives independent $Z_1, Z_2$ with mean $0$ and
variance $1$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}) = I$, the **identity matrix** (ones
on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere). Show that $\mathbf{X} = L\mathbf{Z}$ works, with:

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \cr 0.6 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. By the rule, $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}) = L I L^{\top} = L L^{\top}$.
2. Multiply. Entry $(1, 1)$: $1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 1$. Entry $(1, 2)$: $1 \cdot 0.6 + 0 \cdot 0.8 = 0.6$. Entry $(2, 2)$: $0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$.

$$
L L^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \cr 0.6 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \cr 0 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \cr 0.6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Answer.** Each $X_i$ has variance $1$ and the covariance (and correlation) is
$0.6$. In words: $X_1 = Z_1$ and $X_2 = 0.6 Z_1 + 0.8 Z_2$.

**Check.** This is the trick used in the simulation of [11.2](11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md),
with $0.8 = \sqrt{1 - 0.6^2}$. For any target $\Sigma$, the matrix $L$ with
$L L^{\top} = \Sigma$ is found by the **Cholesky** method, the topic of
[12.4](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md).

### Example 3: Turning to Remove the Correlation

**Problem.** Two sensors have $\Sigma_{11} = \Sigma_{22} = 2$ and
$\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21} = 1$. Use the new coordinates
$Y_1 = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $Y_2 = \frac{X_1 - X_2}{\sqrt{2}}$ (a turn of
the axes by 45 degrees). Find $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y})$.

**Solution.**

1. Here $A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} B$ with $B$ the matrix of rows $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$. Numbers come out of the product twice, once from $A$ and once from $A^{\top}$, so $A\Sigma A^{\top} = \frac{1}{2} B \Sigma B^{\top}$.
2. Row sums and differences as in Example 1: $B\Sigma$ has rows $(3, 3)$ and $(1, -1)$.
3. Then $B\Sigma B^{\top}$ has entries $3 + 3 = 6$, $3 - 3 = 0$, $1 - 1 = 0$ and $1 + 1 = 2$.

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \cr 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \cr 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Answer.** In the turned coordinates the two variables are **uncorrelated**, with
variance $3$ along the diagonal direction and $1$ across it.

**Check.** The total variance is the same before and after the turn:
$2 + 2 = 3 + 1 = 4$, because a turn does not stretch the cloud. Finding the turn
that removes all correlations is what PCA does; the directions are the
eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ ([12.2 Geometry and Contours](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md)).

## Simulate It

We build samples with the mean and covariance of Example 1 (using the trick of
Example 2), apply $A$, and compare the sample mean and covariance of $\mathbf{Y}$ with
$A\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $A\Sigma A^{\top}$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

mu = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
sigma = np.array([[4.0, 2.0], [2.0, 9.0]])
A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -1.0]])

# X = mu + L Z has covariance L L^T = sigma (Example 2's trick)
L = np.linalg.cholesky(sigma)
z = rng.standard_normal((n_trials, 2))
x = mu + z @ L.T
y = x @ A.T                       # each row: (X1 + X2, X1 - X2)

print("mean of Y:", np.round(y.mean(axis=0), 3), "  exact:", A @ mu)
print("Cov(Y) simulated:")
print(np.round(np.cov(y, rowvar=False, bias=True), 3))
print("A Sigma A^T exact:")
print(A @ sigma @ A.T)
```

Output:

```text
mean of Y: [ 3.003 -1.003]   exact: [ 3. -1.]
Cov(Y) simulated:
[[16.987 -5.021]
 [-5.021  9.015]]
A Sigma A^T exact:
[[17. -5.]
 [-5.  9.]]
```

The sample mean and covariance of $\mathbf{Y}$ match $(3, -1)$ and the matrix with
$17$, $-5$, $-5$, $9$ to within about $0.03$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the $A^{\top}$.** $\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{X})$ is $A\Sigma A^{\top}$,
  not $A\Sigma$ and not $A^2 \Sigma$. The matrix appears on **both** sides, just as
  $a$ appears twice in $a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- **Writing the product in the wrong order.** $A^{\top}\Sigma A$ is a different
  matrix, and often the sizes do not even fit. Check sizes: $(m \times n)(n \times n)(n \times m)$
  gives $m \times m$.
- **Letting the shift change the covariance.** Adding $\mathbf{b}$ moves the cloud
  but does not change its shape. It changes the mean only.
- **Using the rule with a random matrix.** The rule needs $A$ and $\mathbf{b}$ to be
  fixed numbers. If the weights are random themselves, you need the tools of
  [Module 14](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/README.md).
- **Concluding independence from a diagonal result.** Example 3 makes $Y_1$ and $Y_2$
  uncorrelated, which in general is weaker than independent
  ([11.4](11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md)). For Gaussian vectors it is
  enough ([12.8](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** PCA and **whitening** turn data with matrices so that
  the new features are uncorrelated with variance $1$. A neural network layer starts
  with $A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, and good weight starting values are chosen so that
  $A\Sigma A^{\top}$ neither explodes nor shrinks from layer to layer.
- **Robotics and tracking:** the prediction step of a Kalman filter moves the
  uncertainty of a robot's position with $F P F^{\top}$, which is this rule with
  $A = F$ and $\Sigma = P$.
- **Simulation:** correlated random numbers are made as $\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$
  (Example 2), in Monte Carlo methods ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)).
- **Finance:** a portfolio with weights $\mathbf{w}$ has return $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}$
  and risk $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{w}$. Several portfolios at once form the
  rows of a matrix $W$, with covariance $W\Sigma W^{\top}$.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X_1$ and $X_2$ have means $1$ and $-1$, variances $1$ and $4$, and
covariance $0$. Find the mean and variance of $Y = 3X_1 - X_2 + 2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $A$ is the row $(3, -1)$ and $b = 2$:

$$E[Y] = 3 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot (-1) + 2 = 6, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = 3^2 \cdot 1 + (-1)^2 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 \cdot (-1) \cdot 0 = 13$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $\mathbf{X}$ has 2 entries and $A$ is a $3 \times 2$ matrix. How many
entries does $\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X}$ have, and what is the size of
$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y})$? Can $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y})$ be positive
definite?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\mathbf{Y}$ has 3 entries, and $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) = A\Sigma A^{\top}$ is
$(3 \times 2)(2 \times 2)(2 \times 3) = 3 \times 3$. It cannot be positive definite:
three numbers built from only two random inputs always obey a linear rule, so
some direction has variance $0$ (see [11.6](11.6-Covariance-Matrix-is-PSD.md)).

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Use the rule $\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{X}) = A\Sigma A^{\top}$ to
prove $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $A = \mathbf{a}^{\top}$, a $1 \times n$ matrix. Then $A\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}$
is a single random variable, and its $1 \times 1$ covariance matrix is its variance.
Also $A^{\top} = (\mathbf{a}^{\top})^{\top} = \mathbf{a}$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = A\Sigma A^{\top} = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $\Sigma_{11} = \Sigma_{22} = 1$ and $\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21} = 0.5$.
Find the covariance matrix of $Y_1 = X_1 + X_2$ and $Y_2 = X_1 - X_2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $A$ of rows $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, the rows of $A\Sigma$ are $(1.5, 1.5)$ and
$(0.5, -0.5)$. Multiplying by $A^{\top}$ adds and subtracts the entries of each row:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y_1) = 1.5 + 1.5 = 3, \quad \operatorname{Var}(Y_2) = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(Y_1, Y_2) = 1.5 - 1.5 = 0$$

The sum and difference are uncorrelated because the two variances are equal.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For random vectors $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, define the
**cross-covariance** matrix $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}) = E\big[(\mathbf{U} - E[\mathbf{U}])(\mathbf{V} - E[\mathbf{V}])^{\top}\big]$,
whose entry $(i, j)$ is $\operatorname{Cov}(U_i, V_j)$. Prove that
$\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{X}, B\mathbf{X}) = A\Sigma B^{\top}$. Use it to show that
$A\mathbf{X}$ and $B\mathbf{X}$ are uncorrelated (every entry is $0$) when
$A\Sigma B^{\top}$ is the zero matrix.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The deviations are $A(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$ and $B(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$.
Use the transpose of a product, then take the constant matrices out of the
expectation:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{X}, B\mathbf{X}) = E\big[A(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}B^{\top}\big] = A \thinspace E\big[(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\big] \thinspace B^{\top} = A\Sigma B^{\top}$$

Entry $(i, j)$ of this matrix is $\operatorname{Cov}\big((A\mathbf{X})_i, (B\mathbf{X})_j\big)$,
so if $A\Sigma B^{\top}$ is all zeros, every entry of $A\mathbf{X}$ is uncorrelated
with every entry of $B\mathbf{X}$. For example, with $A = (1, 1)$ and $B = (1, -1)$ we
get $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{22}$, the result of 11.1, Practice 4.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 3 by simulation: draw one million samples with
$\Sigma_{11} = \Sigma_{22} = 2$ and $\Sigma_{12} = 1$, turn them with $A$, and print
the sample covariance of $\mathbf{Y}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
sigma = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
x = rng.standard_normal((n_trials, 2)) @ np.linalg.cholesky(sigma).T
A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -1.0]]) / np.sqrt(2)
y = x @ A.T
print(np.round(np.cov(y, rowvar=False, bias=True), 3))
print("exact: [[3, 0], [0, 1]]")
```

Output:

```text
[[ 2.995 -0.003]
 [-0.003  1.001]]
exact: [[3, 0], [0, 1]]
```

</details>

## Summary

- A linear transformation $\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}$ makes each new
  variable a weighted sum of the old ones plus a constant.
- The mean goes through the map: $E[\mathbf{Y}] = A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$.
- The covariance gets the matrix on both sides: $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) = A\Sigma A^{\top}$;
  the shift does not matter.
- Special cases are $a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$, $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$, and
  the variance of a sum.
- Matrices can create correlation ($L\mathbf{Z}$) or remove it (a turn of the axes,
  as in PCA).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b} \implies E[\mathbf{Y}] = A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) = A\Sigma A^{\top}}
$$
