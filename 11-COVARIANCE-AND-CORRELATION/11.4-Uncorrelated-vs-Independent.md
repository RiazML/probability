# 11.4 Uncorrelated vs Independent

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 11: Covariance and Correlation](README.md) / 11.4 Uncorrelated vs Independent

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [11.3 Variance of a Sum](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)

> **Big idea:** Independent variables always have covariance zero, but covariance zero only rules out a straight-line trend, so uncorrelated variables can still be strongly dependent.

---

## Why It Matters

"The correlation is zero, so the two things are unrelated" is one of the most
common mistakes in data analysis. Covariance only looks for a **linear** trend.
A curved relation, even a perfect one, can hide behind a correlation of zero. Knowing
exactly when "uncorrelated" is enough (adding variances) and when it is not
(claiming independence) protects you from wrong conclusions, and it prepares you
for the special case of the [multivariate Gaussian](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md),
where the two ideas do agree.

## Intuition

An office keeps its thermostat near 22 degrees. Let $X$ be how far the room is from
22 degrees on a given day: sometimes $-3$, sometimes $+3$, on average $0$. Let $Y$
be the number of complaints. People complain when it is too cold **and** when it is
too hot, so complaints follow a U shape: few near $X = 0$, many at both ends.

Now think about the covariance. On a hot day, $X$ is above its mean and $Y$ is
above its mean: positive product. On an equally cold day, $X$ is below its mean but
$Y$ is still above: negative product of the same size. The hot days and the cold
days cancel. The covariance is $0$.

Yet $X$ and $Y$ are **not** independent: if you know $X = 3$, you know
there will be many complaints. Covariance asks only "does $Y$ tend to go up when
$X$ goes up?". Here the answer is "no, it goes up on both sides", and covariance
cannot see the U.

## Picture

```text
          Y
          ^
      1.0 +.                                     .
          | .                                   .
          |  ..                               ..
          |    ..                           ..
          |      ...                     ...
          |         ....             ....
      0.0 +-------------'''''''''''''------------> X
         -1                  0                   1

          Y = X^2 :  left half has a downward trend, right half upward.
          They cancel, so Cov(X, Y) = 0, but Y is fixed once X is known.
```

The points lie exactly on a curve, so $Y$ depends completely on $X$, but the
best straight line through them is flat.

## Definition

Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ (with finite variances) are **uncorrelated** if:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0, \quad \text{or equally} \quad E[XY] = E[X] \thinspace E[Y]
$$

They are **independent**, written $X \perp Y$, if their joint distribution is the
product of the marginals (see [10.5](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)):

$$
p(x, y) = p_X(x) \thinspace p_Y(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y
$$

(or $f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y)$ for densities).

**In words:** uncorrelated means "the product has the right average". Independent
means "every probability factors": knowing $X$ tells you nothing at all about $Y$.

The relation between them:

$$
X \perp Y \implies \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0, \qquad \text{but} \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 \ \text{does NOT imply} \ X \perp Y
$$

**In words:** independence is the strong condition and uncorrelated is the weak
one. Every independent pair is uncorrelated, not the other way around.

## Why It Is True

**Independent implies uncorrelated.** We proved this in
[11.1](11.1-Covariance.md): independence gives $E[XY] = E[X] E[Y]$, so the
covariance is $0$.

**Uncorrelated does not imply independent.** One counterexample is enough. Let $X$
be $-1$, $0$ or $1$, each with probability $\frac{1}{3}$, and let $Y = X^2$.

1. $E[X] = \frac{-1 + 0 + 1}{3} = 0$.
2. $XY = X^3$, which is $-1$, $0$ or $1$, so $E[XY] = 0$.
3. So $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot E[Y] = 0$: uncorrelated.
4. But $P(X = 0, Y = 0) = P(X = 0) = \frac{1}{3}$, while $P(X = 0) P(Y = 0) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{9}$. The joint PMF does not factor: dependent.

**Why the gap exists.** If $X \perp Y$, then any function of $X$ is independent of
any function of $Y$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(g(X), h(Y)) = 0$ for **every** pair of
functions $g$ and $h$. Being uncorrelated checks only one pair: $g(x) = x$ and
$h(y) = y$. In the example above, the pair $g(x) = x^2$, $h(y) = y$ fails:
$\operatorname{Cov}(X^2, Y) = \operatorname{Var}(Y) = E[X^4] - (E[X^2])^2 = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{4}{9} = \frac{2}{9} \ne 0$.

**A case where the two ideas agree: indicators.** Let $I_A$ and $I_B$ be indicators.
From [11.1](11.1-Covariance.md), Practice 5:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B) = P(A \cap B) - P(A) \thinspace P(B)
$$

So $\operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B) = 0$ means exactly $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$: the
events $A$ and $B$ are independent. Then $A$ and $B^c$, $A^c$ and $B$, and $A^c$ and
$B^c$ are independent too (see [4.7 Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)).
Those four facts are the four cells of the joint PMF of $(I_A, I_B)$, so every cell
factors and $I_A \perp I_B$.

**In words:** for yes/no variables, zero covariance is the same as independence.
The other famous case is a pair that is **jointly Gaussian**, shown in
[12.8 Uncorrelated Implies Independent](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md).

| Situation | Uncorrelated implies independent? |
|---|---|
| General random variables | No |
| Both variables take only two values (indicators) | Yes |
| $(X, Y)$ jointly Gaussian | Yes (Module 12) |
| Each of $X$ and $Y$ Gaussian on its own, but not jointly | No |

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Parabola on a Continuous Scale

**Problem.** Let $X \sim \text{Unif}(-1, 1)$, uniform between $a = -1$ and $b = 1$,
so its density is $\frac{1}{2}$ on $[-1, 1]$. Let $Y = X^2$. Show that $X$ and $Y$
are uncorrelated but not independent.

**Solution.**

1. By symmetry $E[X] = 0$.
2. $E[XY] = E[X^3]$. The function $x^3$ is odd, so its integral over $[-1, 1]$ is $0$:

$$
E[X^3] = \int_{-1}^{1} x^3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{4} \right) = 0
$$

3. So $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y] = 0 - 0 = 0$. Uncorrelated.
4. For dependence, find two events that do not factor. Take $X \gt \frac{1}{2}$ and $Y \lt \frac{1}{4}$. They cannot happen together, because $X \gt \frac{1}{2}$ forces $Y = X^2 \gt \frac{1}{4}$. So the joint probability is $0$.
5. But each one alone has positive probability:

$$
P\left(X \gt \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1 - 0.5}{2} = \frac{1}{4}, \qquad P\left(Y \lt \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = P\left(-\tfrac{1}{2} \lt X \lt \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}
$$

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, but
$P(X \gt \frac{1}{2}, Y \lt \frac{1}{4}) = 0 \ne \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8}$.
So $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. In fact, $Y$ is completely determined by $X$.

**Check.** Look at $\lvert X \rvert$ instead of $X$. On $[0, 1]$ the curve
$y = x^2$ always rises, so $\lvert X \rvert$ and $Y$ should have a strong
**positive** correlation. The simulation below finds about $0.97$.

### Example 2: A Useful Feature With Zero Correlation

**Problem.** A server's temperature, measured from its safe value, is
$X \sim \text{Unif}(-1, 1)$. An alarm $L = 1$ goes off when $\lvert X \rvert \gt \frac{1}{2}$
(too cold **or** too hot), and $L = 0$ otherwise. A data scientist keeps only
features with nonzero correlation to the label. Will $X$ survive?

**Solution.**

1. $P(L = 1) = P(\lvert X \rvert \gt \frac{1}{2}) = \frac{1}{2}$, so $E[L] = \frac{1}{2}$, and $E[X] = 0$.
2. $XL$ equals $X$ when $\lvert X \rvert \gt \frac{1}{2}$ and $0$ otherwise. The two parts $(-1, -\frac{1}{2})$ and $(\frac{1}{2}, 1)$ are mirror images, so they cancel:

$$
E[XL] = \int_{-1}^{-1/2} x \cdot \frac{1}{2} \thinspace dx + \int_{1/2}^{1} x \cdot \frac{1}{2} \thinspace dx = -\frac{3}{16} + \frac{3}{16} = 0
$$

3. So $\operatorname{Cov}(X, L) = 0 - 0 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 0$.

**Answer.** The correlation is exactly $0$, so the filter throws $X$ away. Yet $X$
predicts the alarm **perfectly**: $L$ is a function of $X$. A correlation filter
only finds linear signal. The fix is to also try transformed features, such as
$\lvert X \rvert$ or $X^2$, which have strong correlation with $L$.

**Check.** Each integral: $\int_{1/2}^{1} \frac{x}{2} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{4}\left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{3}{16}$, and the left one is its negative.

## Simulate It

We check Example 1: the correlation of $X$ and $X^2$ is about $0$, the correlation
of $\lvert X \rvert$ and $X^2$ is large, and the joint probability does not factor.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=n_trials)
y = x**2

print(f"corr(X, Y)   = {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]:.4f}   (exact 0)")
print(f"corr(|X|, Y) = {np.corrcoef(np.abs(x), y)[0, 1]:.4f}   "
      f"(exact {np.sqrt(540) / 24:.4f})")

# Independence would need P(A and B) = P(A) P(B).
a = x > 0.5
b = y < 0.25
print(f"P(A and B) = {np.mean(a & b):.4f}   (exact 0)")
print(f"P(A) P(B)  = {a.mean() * b.mean():.4f}   (exact 0.1250)")
```

Output:

```text
corr(X, Y)   = 0.0008   (exact 0)
corr(|X|, Y) = 0.9682   (exact 0.9682)
P(A and B) = 0.0000   (exact 0)
P(A) P(B)  = 0.1250   (exact 0.1250)
```

The exact value $\frac{\sqrt{540}}{24} \approx 0.968$ comes from
$\operatorname{Cov}(U, U^2) = \frac{1}{12}$, $\operatorname{Var}(U) = \frac{1}{12}$
and $\operatorname{Var}(U^2) = \frac{4}{45}$ for $U = \lvert X \rvert \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$.

## Common Mistakes

- **"The correlation is zero, so there is no relation."** Zero correlation means
  no **linear** trend. Always plot the data: a U shape, a circle or a V can all
  have correlation $0$.
- **Using "uncorrelated" to justify independence.** In a proof, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$
  does not let you write $p(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y)$, or $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$
  for events built from $X$ and $Y$.
- **Thinking you need independence to add variances.** You do not. The formula
  $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$ from
  [11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md) needs only $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$.
- **"Each variable is normal, so uncorrelated means independent."** This is true
  only when the pair is **jointly** Gaussian. Two normal variables can be
  uncorrelated and dependent (see the Challenge section of the
  [module practice set](11.8-Practice-Problems.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** feature selection by correlation misses curved
  relations (Example 2). Tools such as mutual information or tree models look for
  any kind of dependence.
- **Computer science:** a random number generator can pass a correlation test and
  still have strong patterns. Good tests check many functions of the output, not
  only the numbers themselves.
- **Signal processing:** methods such as independent component analysis need true
  independence, which is stronger than the "uncorrelated" that PCA gives.
- **Finance:** two assets can be uncorrelated in normal times and still crash
  together in a crisis, a dependence that covariance misses.
- **Everyday life:** "no correlation between dose and effect" can hide a dose that
  is best in the middle, with too little and too much both harmful.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** True or false? (a) If $X \perp Y$ then $\rho(X, Y) = 0$. (b) If
$\rho(X, Y) = 0$ then $X \perp Y$. (c) If $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ then
$\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) True: independence gives covariance $0$. (b) False: $Y = X^2$ with $X$ uniform
on $[-1, 1]$ is a counterexample. (c) True: the covariance term in the variance of
a sum is $0$, and independence is not needed.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ is equally likely to be $-2$, $-1$, $1$ or $2$, and $Y = X^2$.
Show that $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, and that $X$ and $Y$ are dependent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[X] = 0$ and $E[XY] = E[X^3] = \frac{-8 - 1 + 1 + 8}{4} = 0$, so the covariance
is $0$. For dependence, $Y = 4$ forces $X = \pm 2$:

$$P(X = 1, Y = 4) = 0 \ne P(X = 1) P(Y = 4) = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8}$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $P(A) = 0.3$, $P(B) = 0.5$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B) = 0$.
Find $P(A \cap B)$ and $P(A^c \cap B)$. Are $I_A$ and $I_B$ independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Zero covariance of indicators means $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B) = 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15$.
Then:

$$P(A^c \cap B) = P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.5 - 0.15 = 0.35 = 0.7 \times 0.5 = P(A^c) P(B)$$

Yes. For indicators, uncorrelated is the same as independent, and every cell of the
joint table factors.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A point is chosen uniformly on the unit circle: $\Theta \sim \text{Unif}(0, 2\pi)$,
$X = \cos \Theta$, $Y = \sin \Theta$. Show $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$. Are $X$
and $Y$ independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Over a full turn, $\cos$ and $\sin$ average to $0$, so $E[X] = E[Y] = 0$. Also
$XY = \cos \Theta \sin \Theta = \frac{1}{2} \sin 2\Theta$, which also averages to $0$
over a full turn (two full turns of $2\Theta$):

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E\left[\tfrac{1}{2} \sin 2\Theta\right] - 0 \cdot 0 = 0$$

They are **not** independent, because $X^2 + Y^2 = 1$. For example $X \gt 0.9$
and $Y \gt 0.9$ cannot both hold (that would give $X^2 + Y^2 \gt 1.62$), so their
joint probability is $0$, while each alone has probability
$\frac{\operatorname{arccos}(0.9)}{\pi} \approx 0.144 \gt 0$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $X$ takes only two values $x_1 \ne x_2$ and $Y$ takes only two
values $y_1 \ne y_2$. Prove that if $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ then $X \perp Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $A = \lbrace X = x_2 \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace Y = y_2 \rbrace$. Then
$X = x_1 + (x_2 - x_1) I_A$ and $Y = y_1 + (y_2 - y_1) I_B$. Shifts do not change a
covariance and factors come out:

$$0 = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = (x_2 - x_1)(y_2 - y_1) \operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B)$$

The factors $x_2 - x_1$ and $y_2 - y_1$ are not $0$, so
$\operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B) = 0$. For indicators that means $I_A \perp I_B$. Since
$X$ is a function of $I_A$ and $Y$ is a function of $I_B$, $X \perp Y$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Practice 4 with one million points. Let
$A = \lbrace X \gt 0.9 \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace Y \gt 0.9 \rbrace$. Estimate
$\rho(X, Y)$, $P(A \cap B)$ and $P(A) P(B)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, size=n_trials)
x, y = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
a, b = x > 0.9, y > 0.9
p_each = np.arccos(0.9) / np.pi
print(f"rho(X, Y):    {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]:.4f}  (exact 0)")
print(f"P(A and B):   {np.mean(a & b):.4f}  (exact 0)")
print(f"P(A) P(B):    {a.mean() * b.mean():.4f}  (exact {p_each**2:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
rho(X, Y):    -0.0004  (exact 0)
P(A and B):   0.0000  (exact 0)
P(A) P(B):    0.0206  (exact 0.0206)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Uncorrelated means $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$; independent means the joint
  distribution factors.
- Independent always implies uncorrelated. Uncorrelated does **not** imply
  independent: $Y = X^2$ is the classic counterexample.
- Covariance sees only straight-line trends; curved relations can have
  correlation $0$.
- For indicators (and for jointly Gaussian pairs) the two ideas agree.
- To add variances, uncorrelated is enough.

The one fact to remember:

$$
\boxed{X \perp Y \implies \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0, \quad \text{but not the other way around}}
$$
