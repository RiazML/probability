# 11.6 Covariance Matrix is PSD

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 11: Covariance and Correlation](README.md) / 11.6 Covariance Matrix is PSD

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [11.5 Random Vectors and Covariance Matrix](11.5-Random-Vectors-and-Covariance-Matrix.md)

> **Big idea:** Every weighted sum of the variables has a variance that the covariance matrix computes, and no variance can be negative, so every covariance matrix is positive semi-definite.

---

## Why It Matters

Not every symmetric table of numbers can be a covariance matrix. If you type in a
table that "looks fine" and use it to simulate data, price a portfolio or train a
Gaussian model, the program may crash, or, worse, it may report a **negative**
risk. The test that separates real covariance matrices from fake ones is called
**positive semi-definite**. This topic shows what it means, why it is always true,
and how to check it for a $2 \times 2$ matrix by hand.

## Intuition

An analyst studies three stocks. She believes each pair moves in opposite
directions, with correlation $-0.6$ for every pair. Each stock has variance $1$.
Every single number looks legal: the variances are positive, and each correlation
is between $-1$ and $1$.

Now buy one share of each stock. The variance of the total, by the formula of
[11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md), is the sum of all 9 boxes of the table: three
variances and six covariances:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2 + X_3) = 3 \times 1 + 6 \times (-0.6) = 3 - 3.6 = -0.6
$$

A negative variance is impossible. So her table cannot be the covariance matrix of
any three random variables. Here is the reason in words: if stock 1 goes up, both 2
and 3 tend to go down, but then 2 and 3 move **together**, which disagrees with
their own correlation of $-0.6$. Three things cannot all be strongly "opposite" to
each other.

The rule hiding here: for **every** choice of amounts $a_1, a_2, a_3$, the
combination $a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2 + a_3 X_3$ is a random variable, and its variance
must be $0$ or more. That is exactly what positive semi-definite means.

## Picture

```text
     A real covariance matrix                 A singular one (det = 0)

      x2 |       . .  .                         x2 |            .
         |    .  . .. . .                          |          .
         |  . .. . .. .  .                         |        .
         | . . .. . .  .                           |      .
         |   .  .  .                               |    .
         +-------------------- x1                  +-------------------- x1

  every direction a has spread               along one direction the
  Var(aᵀX) = aᵀΣa > 0                        spread is 0: aᵀΣa = 0
```

Project the cloud onto any direction $\mathbf{a}$: the spread you see is
$\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$. It can shrink to $0$ (all points on a line),
but it can never be negative.

## Definition

### One More Matrix Tool: the Quadratic Form

For a square matrix $M$ and a vector $\mathbf{a}$, the number
$\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a}$ is called a **quadratic form**: a row, times a
matrix, times a column, gives one number. For a symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix it
works out to:

$$
\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} m_{11} & m_{12} \cr m_{12} & m_{22} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \cr a_2 \end{pmatrix} = a_1^2 m_{11} + 2 a_1 a_2 m_{12} + a_2^2 m_{22}
$$

For example, with $m_{11} = 4$, $m_{12} = 3$, $m_{22} = 9$ and $\mathbf{a} = (1, -1)^{\top}$:
$1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 9 = 4 - 6 + 9 = 7$. In general,
$\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a} = \sum_{i} \sum_{j} a_i a_j m_{ij}$.

### Positive Semi-Definite

A symmetric matrix $M$ is **positive semi-definite** (PSD) if:

$$
\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a} \ge 0 \quad \text{for every vector } \mathbf{a}
$$

It is **positive definite** (PD) if $\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a} \gt 0$ for every
$\mathbf{a}$ that is not all zeros.

**In words:** whatever numbers you put in $\mathbf{a}$, the quadratic form never
comes out negative.

### The Theorem

For a random vector $\mathbf{X}$ with covariance matrix $\Sigma$ and any constant
vector $\mathbf{a}$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} \ge 0
$$

where $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X} = a_1 X_1 + \dots + a_n X_n$ is one random number,
a weighted sum of the variables.

**In words:** the variance of any weighted sum of the variables is the quadratic
form of $\Sigma$, so every covariance matrix is symmetric and positive
semi-definite.

The reverse is also true: every symmetric PSD matrix is the covariance matrix of
some random vector. [12.4 Standard Normal Vectors and Cholesky](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)
shows how to build one.

**The $2 \times 2$ test.** A symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix is PSD exactly when:

$$
m_{11} \ge 0, \qquad m_{22} \ge 0, \qquad m_{11} m_{22} - m_{12}^2 \ge 0
$$

The last number, $m_{11} m_{22} - m_{12}^2$, is the **determinant** of the matrix.
For a covariance matrix the last condition says
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)^2 \le \operatorname{Var}(X_1) \operatorname{Var}(X_2)$,
which is $\rho^2 \le 1$ from [11.2](11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) again.

For bigger matrices, pairwise checks are **not** enough (the Intuition shows why).
The standard test is that all **eigenvalues** of the matrix are $0$ or more; you
meet eigenvalues in [12.2 Geometry and Contours](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md).
In code, `np.linalg.eigvalsh(M)` gives them.

## Why It Is True

**Proof of $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$.**
Write the weighted sum out and use bilinearity from [11.1](11.1-Covariance.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) &= \operatorname{Cov}\Big(\sum_{i} a_i X_i, \ \sum_{j} a_j X_j\Big) && \text{(variance is a covariance)} \\
&= \sum_{i} \sum_{j} a_i a_j \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) && \text{(bilinearity)} \\
&= \sum_{i} \sum_{j} a_i a_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{(definition of } \Sigma \text{)} \\
&= \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} && \text{(the quadratic form written out)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A variance is the average of a square, so it is never negative. Therefore
$\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} \ge 0$ for every $\mathbf{a}$, and $\Sigma$ is PSD.
Symmetry comes from $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_j, X_i)$.

The same proof in matrix language: $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}$ has mean
$\mathbf{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}$, so its deviation is the number
$D = \mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$. A number equals its own
transpose, so $D^2 = \mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{a}$.
Take expectations; the constant $\mathbf{a}$ comes out on both sides, and the middle
becomes $\Sigma$.

**Proof of the $2 \times 2$ test.** If the matrix is PSD, take $\mathbf{a} = (1, 0)^{\top}$
to get $m_{11} \ge 0$, and $\mathbf{a} = (0, 1)^{\top}$ to get $m_{22} \ge 0$. For the
determinant, look at the case $m_{11} \gt 0$ and complete the square:

$$
a_1^2 m_{11} + 2 a_1 a_2 m_{12} + a_2^2 m_{22} = m_{11} \Big(a_1 + \frac{m_{12}}{m_{11}} a_2\Big)^2 + \frac{m_{11} m_{22} - m_{12}^2}{m_{11}} \thinspace a_2^2
$$

The first term is never negative and can be made $0$ by choosing
$a_1 = -\frac{m_{12}}{m_{11}} a_2$. So the whole thing is $\ge 0$ for every
$\mathbf{a}$ exactly when $m_{11} m_{22} - m_{12}^2 \ge 0$. (If $m_{11} = 0$, the
form is $2 a_1 a_2 m_{12} + a_2^2 m_{22}$, which goes negative for a large $a_1$ of
the right sign unless $m_{12} = 0$; the determinant test $-m_{12}^2 \ge 0$ says the
same.)

**When is the matrix singular?** If $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} = 0$ for some
$\mathbf{a}$ that is not all zeros, then $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = 0$,
so $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}$ is a constant. The variables obey an exact linear
equation, and the cloud of points is flat, as in the right half of the picture.
Such a $\Sigma$ is PSD but not PD, and its determinant is $0$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Valid Matrix and Two Portfolios

**Problem.** Check that the matrix below is a valid covariance matrix. Then find
$\operatorname{Var}(X_1 - X_2)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + 2X_2)$ using $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$.

$$
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \cr 3 & 9 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. It is symmetric. Diagonal: $4 \ge 0$ and $9 \ge 0$. Determinant: $4 \times 9 - 3^2 = 36 - 9 = 27 \ge 0$. So it is PSD (in fact PD, since $27 \gt 0$).
2. For $X_1 - X_2$, use $\mathbf{a} = (1, -1)^{\top}$, the form is $1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 9 = 4 - 6 + 9 = 7$.
3. For $X_1 + 2X_2$, use $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)^{\top}$, the form is $1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 9 = 4 + 12 + 36 = 52$.

**Answer.** Valid. $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 - X_2) = 7$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + 2X_2) = 52$.

**Check.** The formula of [11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md) gives the same:
$\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + 2X_2) = 4 + 4 \cdot 9 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 4 + 36 + 12 = 52$.
The quadratic form is just that formula in matrix clothes.

### Example 2: A Fake Matrix

**Problem.** A colleague proposes the matrix below as a covariance matrix. Is it
valid?

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \cr 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. It is symmetric and the diagonal is positive, so it passes the first checks.
2. Determinant: $1 \times 1 - 2^2 = 1 - 4 = -3 \lt 0$. It fails.
3. Find a direction that shows the problem. Try $\mathbf{a} = (1, -1)^{\top}$:

$$
\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a} = 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2
$$

**Answer.** Not valid. If it were a covariance matrix, $X_1 - X_2$ would have
variance $-2$.

**Check.** The implied correlation is $\frac{2}{\sqrt{1} \sqrt{1}} = 2 \gt 1$, which
[11.2](11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) already forbids.

### Example 3: Three Stocks With Equal Correlation

**Problem.** Three variables each have variance $1$, and every pair has the same
correlation $\rho$. For which $\rho$ can this be a covariance matrix? Check the
Intuition's value $\rho = -0.6$.

**Solution.**

1. Each $2 \times 2$ piece has determinant $1 - \rho^2$, which is fine for any $\rho$ between $-1$ and $1$. So we need a test that uses all three at once.
2. Take $\mathbf{a} = (1, 1, 1)^{\top}$: $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$ is the sum of all 9 entries, which is $3 + 6\rho$.
3. PSD needs $3 + 6\rho \ge 0$, so $\rho \ge -\frac{1}{2}$.
4. With $\rho = -0.6$: $3 + 6 \times (-0.6) = 3 - 3.6 = -0.6 \lt 0$. Not valid.

**Answer.** We need $\rho \ge -0.5$ (and the full eigenvalue test confirms that
every $\rho$ from $-0.5$ to $1$ works). The value $-0.6$ is impossible, even though
each pair alone looks fine.

**Check.** The simulation below computes the eigenvalues: with $\rho = -0.6$ the
smallest one is negative.

### Example 4: A Hidden Linear Rule

**Problem.** $X_1$ has variance $1$ and $X_2 = 2X_1 + 3$. Find $\Sigma$, its
determinant, and a direction $\mathbf{a}$ with $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} = 0$.

**Solution.**

1. $\operatorname{Var}(X_2) = 4 \operatorname{Var}(X_1) = 4$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 2 \operatorname{Var}(X_1) = 2$.
2. So $\Sigma_{11} = 1$, $\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21} = 2$, $\Sigma_{22} = 4$, and the determinant is $1 \times 4 - 2^2 = 0$.
3. Try $\mathbf{a} = (2, -1)^{\top}$, the form is $4 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 4 = 4 - 8 + 4 = 0$.

**Answer.** $\Sigma$ is PSD but singular. The zero direction says
$2X_1 - X_2$ has variance $0$; indeed $2X_1 - X_2 = -3$ always.

## Simulate It

We check $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$
on samples with the matrix of Example 1, then compute the smallest eigenvalue of
the equal-correlation matrix from Example 3. The code writes `a'` for the
transpose.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

sigma = np.array([[4.0, 3.0], [3.0, 9.0]])
x = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], sigma, size=n_trials)

for a in [np.array([1, -1]), np.array([1, 2])]:
    combo = x @ a                       # a1*X1 + a2*X2 for every sample
    print(f"a = {a}: Var(a'X) simulation {combo.var():6.3f}, "
          f"a' Sigma a = {a @ sigma @ a:.0f}")

# Equal correlations among 3 variables: valid only if rho >= -0.5
for rho in [-0.4, -0.5, -0.6]:
    m = np.full((3, 3), rho) + (1 - rho) * np.eye(3)
    smallest = round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(m).min(), 2) + 0.0
    print(f"rho = {rho}: smallest eigenvalue {smallest:.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
a = [ 1 -1]: Var(a'X) simulation  7.005, a' Sigma a = 7
a = [1 2]: Var(a'X) simulation 51.872, a' Sigma a = 52
rho = -0.4: smallest eigenvalue 0.20
rho = -0.5: smallest eigenvalue 0.00
rho = -0.6: smallest eigenvalue -0.20
```

The simulated variances match $7$ and $52$. For equal correlations the smallest
eigenvalue is $1 + 2\rho$: positive at $-0.4$, zero at $-0.5$ (singular), and
negative at $-0.6$ (not a covariance matrix).

## Common Mistakes

- **Checking only pairs.** Every $2 \times 2$ piece can pass while the whole
  matrix fails (Example 3). For three or more variables, check all eigenvalues.
- **Thinking "positive semi-definite" means "all entries positive".** Negative
  entries are fine: the one-hot matrix in [11.5](11.5-Random-Vectors-and-Covariance-Matrix.md)
  has negative covariances and is PSD. And a matrix with all entries positive can
  fail (Example 2).
- **Forgetting symmetry.** PSD is defined for symmetric matrices. A table with
  $\Sigma_{12} \ne \Sigma_{21}$ is never a covariance matrix.
- **Assuming every covariance matrix can be inverted.** A singular $\Sigma$
  (determinant $0$) is a valid covariance matrix. It just means the variables obey
  a linear rule (Example 4). Formulas with $\Sigma^{-1}$ need PD.
- **Trusting rounded matrices.** A correlation matrix copied with 2 decimals, or
  built by hand from several sources, can come out slightly non-PSD. Check the
  eigenvalues before you use it.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a Gaussian process or a kernel method needs its
  kernel matrix to be PSD, for the same reason: it is a covariance matrix.
- **Simulation:** to draw correlated random numbers, programs factor $\Sigma$
  ([12.4 Cholesky](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)).
  The factoring fails if the matrix is not PSD.
- **Finance:** risk managers combine correlation estimates from many sources and
  must repair the result if it is not PSD, or the model will report negative risk
  for some portfolio ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Optimization:** the portfolio variance $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$ is a
  bowl-shaped function of $\mathbf{a}$ because $\Sigma$ is PSD, so the safest
  portfolio can be found quickly.
- **Data science:** PCA ranks directions by the spread
  $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$; a zero tells you that some feature is a
  linear combination of the others.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Can the matrix with $m_{11} = 9$, $m_{12} = m_{21} = -4$, $m_{22} = 1$
be a covariance matrix? If not, find a vector $\mathbf{a}$ with a negative
quadratic form.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The determinant is $9 \times 1 - (-4)^2 = 9 - 16 = -7 \lt 0$, so no. Try
$\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)^{\top}$:

$$\mathbf{a}^{\top} M \mathbf{a} = 1 \cdot 9 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot (-4) + 4 \cdot 1 = 9 - 16 + 4 = -3 \lt 0$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For which numbers $c$ is the matrix with $m_{11} = 1$,
$m_{12} = m_{21} = c$, $m_{22} = 4$ a covariance matrix?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The diagonal is positive, so we need the determinant $1 \times 4 - c^2 \ge 0$:

$$c^2 \le 4 \iff -2 \le c \le 2$$

This matches $\lvert \rho \rvert = \frac{\lvert c \rvert}{1 \times 2} \le 1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A random vector has $\Sigma_{11} = 2$, $\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21} = 1$
and $\Sigma_{22} = 3$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 - 2X_2)$ as a quadratic form,
and check it with the formula from 11.3.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $\mathbf{a} = (1, -2)^{\top}$:

$$\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-2) \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 3 = 2 - 4 + 12 = 10$$

Check: $\operatorname{Var}(X_1) + 4\operatorname{Var}(X_2) - 2 \cdot 2 \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 2 + 12 - 4 = 10$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Prove that every diagonal entry of a PSD matrix is $0$ or more.
What does this say about a covariance matrix?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $\mathbf{e}_i$ be the vector with $1$ in position $i$ and $0$ elsewhere. The
quadratic form picks out one entry:

$$\mathbf{e}_i^{\top} M \mathbf{e}_i = m_{ii} \ge 0$$

For a covariance matrix, $\mathbf{e}_i^{\top}\mathbf{X} = X_i$, so this is just
$\operatorname{Var}(X_i) \ge 0$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $n$ random variables each have variance $1$, and every pair has
correlation $\rho$. Prove that $\rho \ge -\frac{1}{n - 1}$. What does this say when
$n$ is large?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $\mathbf{a} = (1, 1, \dots, 1)^{\top}$. Then $\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$
is the sum of all entries: $n$ ones on the diagonal and $n(n - 1)$ entries equal to
$\rho$ off it. It is the variance of $X_1 + \dots + X_n$, so:

$$0 \le n + n(n - 1)\rho \implies \rho \ge -\frac{n}{n(n - 1)} = -\frac{1}{n - 1}$$

For $n = 3$ this gives $-0.5$ (Example 3). For large $n$ the bound tends to $0$:
many variables cannot all be negatively correlated with each other by much.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Use numpy to find the smallest eigenvalue of the
equal-correlation matrix for $n = 4$ with $\rho = -0.3$ and $\rho = -0.4$. Compare
with Practice 5.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Practice 5 gives the bound $-\frac{1}{3} \approx -0.333$, so $-0.3$ should pass
and $-0.4$ should fail. (The smallest eigenvalue is $1 + 3\rho$.)

```python
import numpy as np

n = 4
for rho in [-0.3, -0.4]:
    m = np.full((n, n), rho) + (1 - rho) * np.eye(n)
    smallest = np.linalg.eigvalsh(m).min()
    print(f"rho = {rho}: smallest eigenvalue {smallest:.2f}, "
          f"exact {1 + (n - 1) * rho:.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
rho = -0.3: smallest eigenvalue 0.10, exact 0.10
rho = -0.4: smallest eigenvalue -0.20, exact -0.20
```

</details>

## Summary

- For any weights $\mathbf{a}$, $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a}$.
- Because a variance is never negative, every covariance matrix is symmetric and
  positive semi-definite.
- A $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix is PSD exactly when its diagonal entries and its
  determinant are $0$ or more; this is $\lvert \rho \rvert \le 1$ again.
- For more variables, pairwise checks are not enough; check the eigenvalues.
- A zero direction ($\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} = 0$) means the variables
  obey an exact linear rule.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} \ge 0 \quad \text{for every } \mathbf{a}}
$$
