# 11.1 Covariance

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 11: Covariance and Correlation](README.md) / 11.1 Covariance

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 10: Joint Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/README.md)

> **Big idea:** Covariance is the average of "how far X is from its mean" times "how far Y is from its mean", so it is positive when X and Y tend to move up and down together.

---

## Why It Matters

Variance tells you how much **one** random quantity spreads out. But real
problems have many quantities at once: temperature and ice cream sales, study
hours and exam marks, the prices of two stocks. **Covariance** is the first
number that measures how two random variables move together. It is the building
block of correlation, of the variance of a sum, and of the covariance matrix used
in finance, PCA and the multivariate Gaussian.

## Intuition

Think about a small shop that sells cold drinks. On a hot day the temperature is
above its usual value, and sales are also above their usual value. On a cold day
both are below usual. So the two numbers are usually on the **same side** of their
averages.

Now multiply the two "distances from average". On a hot day, (above) times
(above) gives a positive number. On a cold day, (below) times (below) is a
negative times a negative, which is also positive. Only an unusual day, such as
a hot day with low sales, gives a negative product. Average these products over
many days and you get a positive number. That average is the covariance.

For umbrellas the story flips. Rainy days have **low** temperature and **high**
umbrella sales. One distance is negative and the other is positive, so most
products are negative, and the covariance is negative. And if two numbers have
nothing to do with each other, such as the temperature in Dhaka and a coin flip
in Paris, the positive and negative products cancel. The covariance is about 0.

## Picture

```text
         Y
         ^       product < 0           :       product > 0
         |    (x below, y above)       :    (x above, y above)
         |                             :          .   .  .
         |                             :      .  . . .  .
   μ_Y --+-----------------------------+--------------------------
         |        .  . .  .            :
         |     .  . .   .              :
         |       product > 0           :       product < 0
         |    (x below, y below)       :    (x above, y below)
         +-----------------------------+------------------------> X
                                      μ_X
```

The two lines cross at the means. Most points sit in the two corners where the
product $(x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y)$ is positive, so the covariance is positive.

## Definition

Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on the same experiment, with means
$\mu_X = E[X]$ and $\mu_Y = E[Y]$. The **covariance** of $X$ and $Y$ is:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big]
$$

where:

- $X - \mu_X$ is how far $X$ lands from its mean (positive above, negative below),
- $Y - \mu_Y$ is the same for $Y$,
- the expectation averages their product over the **joint distribution** of
  $(X, Y)$ (see [10.1 Joint PMF and PDF](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md)).

**In words:** covariance is the average product of the two deviations from the
mean.

For a discrete pair with joint PMF $p(x, y)$, this average is a double sum:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \sum_{x} \sum_{y} (x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y) \thinspace p(x, y)
$$

For a continuous pair, replace the sums by integrals and $p(x, y)$ by the joint
density $f(x, y)$.

In practice we almost always use the **shortcut formula**:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] \thinspace E[Y]
$$

**In words:** the covariance is "the mean of the product" minus "the product of
the means".

How to read the sign:

| Sign | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \gt 0$ | large $X$ tends to come with large $Y$ | temperature and cold drink sales |
| $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \lt 0$ | large $X$ tends to come with small $Y$ | rain and overs played in a cricket match |
| $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ | no **linear** trend (see [11.4](11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md)) | two separate coin flips |

## Why It Is True

**The shortcut formula.** Expand the product and use
[linearity of expectation](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md).
The means $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$ are plain numbers, so they come out of the expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) &= E[XY - \mu_Y X - \mu_X Y + \mu_X \mu_Y] && \text{(multiply out)} \\
&= E[XY] - \mu_Y E[X] - \mu_X E[Y] + \mu_X \mu_Y && \text{(linearity)} \\
&= E[XY] - \mu_Y \mu_X - \mu_X \mu_Y + \mu_X \mu_Y && \text{(since } E[X] = \mu_X \text{ and } E[Y] = \mu_Y \text{)} \\
&= E[XY] - \mu_X \mu_Y && \text{(two terms cancel)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The main properties.** Each one follows from the definition in one or two lines.

1. **Variance is a special case:** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = E[(X - \mu_X)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
2. **Symmetry:** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X)$, because the
   product of two numbers does not depend on their order.
3. **Constants do not co-vary:** for a number $c$, the deviation of $c$ is
   $c - c = 0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, c) = 0$.
4. **Scaling and shifting:** $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + b, Y) = a \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.
   Here is why: $aX + b$ has mean $a\mu_X + b$, so its deviation is
   $a(X - \mu_X)$. The shift $b$ disappears, and the factor $a$ comes out of the
   expectation.
5. **Sums split:** $\operatorname{Cov}(X + Y, Z) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Z) + \operatorname{Cov}(Y, Z)$.
   Here is why: the deviation of $X + Y$ is $(X - \mu_X) + (Y - \mu_Y)$. Multiply
   by $(Z - \mu_Z)$ and use linearity.

Properties 2, 4 and 5 together say that covariance is **bilinear**: it works like
multiplying out brackets. You can expand any covariance of sums term by term:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}\Big(\sum_{i} a_i X_i, \ \sum_{j} b_j Y_j\Big) = \sum_{i} \sum_{j} a_i b_j \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y_j)
$$

**In words:** expand both sides like $(a_1 + a_2)(b_1 + b_2)$, and each pair of
terms gives one covariance.

A useful special case is $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + b, \thinspace cY + d) = ac \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

**Independent means zero covariance.** If $X$ and $Y$ are
[independent](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md),
then $p(x, y) = p_X(x) \thinspace p_Y(y)$, and the double sum splits into two
single sums:

$$
E[XY] = \sum_{x} \sum_{y} x y \thinspace p_X(x) \thinspace p_Y(y) = \Big(\sum_{x} x \thinspace p_X(x)\Big) \Big(\sum_{y} y \thinspace p_Y(y)\Big) = E[X] \thinspace E[Y]
$$

So $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y] = 0$. The continuous case is the
same with integrals. The opposite direction is **false**: covariance 0 does not
mean independent. That is the whole story of
[11.4 Uncorrelated vs Independent](11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: First Coin and Total Heads

**Problem.** Flip two fair coins. Let $X = 1$ if the first coin is heads (else
$0$), and let $Y$ be the total number of heads. Find $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

**Solution.**

1. List the 4 equally likely outcomes and the values of $X$, $Y$ and $XY$:

| Outcome | Probability | $X$ | $Y$ | $XY$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HH | $\frac{1}{4}$ | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| HT | $\frac{1}{4}$ | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| TH | $\frac{1}{4}$ | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| TT | $\frac{1}{4}$ | 0 | 0 | 0 |

2. Compute the three means:

$$
E[X] = \frac{1 + 1 + 0 + 0}{4} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad E[Y] = \frac{2 + 1 + 1 + 0}{4} = 1, \qquad E[XY] = \frac{2 + 1 + 0 + 0}{4} = \frac{3}{4}
$$

3. Use the shortcut formula:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] \thinspace E[Y] = \frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{4}
$$

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$. It is positive: when
the first coin is heads, the total tends to be higher.

**Check.** Use the properties instead. Write $Y = X + X_2$, where $X_2$ is the
indicator of heads on the second coin. Then
$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) + \operatorname{Cov}(X, X_2) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + 0$,
because the coins are independent. A fair coin indicator has variance
$\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$. The two methods agree.

### Example 2: Phone Users and Ad Clicks

**Problem.** A website records two indicators for each visitor: $X = 1$ if the
visitor uses a phone, and $Y = 1$ if the visitor clicks an ad. The joint PMF is:

```text
                 Y = 0     Y = 1    |  P(X = x)
              +---------+---------+ +----------
      X = 0   |  0.10   |  0.20   | |   0.30
      X = 1   |  0.30   |  0.40   | |   0.70
              +---------+---------+ +----------
   P(Y = y)      0.40      0.60     |   1.00
```

Find $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ with the definition, and check it with the shortcut.

**Solution.**

1. Read the means from the margins: $\mu_X = 0.70$ and $\mu_Y = 0.60$.
2. For each cell, multiply the two deviations, then multiply by the probability:

| Cell $(x, y)$ | $x - 0.7$ | $y - 0.6$ | product | $p(x, y)$ | product times $p(x, y)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $(0, 0)$ | $-0.7$ | $-0.6$ | $0.42$ | $0.10$ | $0.042$ |
| $(0, 1)$ | $-0.7$ | $0.4$ | $-0.28$ | $0.20$ | $-0.056$ |
| $(1, 0)$ | $0.3$ | $-0.6$ | $-0.18$ | $0.30$ | $-0.054$ |
| $(1, 1)$ | $0.3$ | $0.4$ | $0.12$ | $0.40$ | $0.048$ |

3. Add the last column:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0.042 - 0.056 - 0.054 + 0.048 = -0.020
$$

4. Shortcut check. $XY = 1$ only in cell $(1, 1)$, so $E[XY] = 0.40$. Then
   $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0.40 - 0.70 \times 0.60 = 0.40 - 0.42 = -0.02$.

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -0.02$. Phone users click a little
**less** often. Indeed, $P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = \frac{0.40}{0.70} \approx 0.571$,
while $P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = \frac{0.20}{0.30} \approx 0.667$.

**Check.** Is $-0.02$ "big" or "small"? Covariance alone cannot say, because its
size depends on units. The next topic,
[11.2 Correlation Coefficient](11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md), turns it into a
number between $-1$ and $1$.

### Example 3: Changing Units

**Problem.** For a city, the daily temperature $T$ (in degrees Celsius) and the
number of cold drinks sold $S$ have $\operatorname{Cov}(T, S) = 120$. A reporter
uses Fahrenheit instead: $F = 1.8 T + 32$. What is $\operatorname{Cov}(F, S)$?
What is the covariance if sales are counted in **hundreds** of drinks?

**Solution.**

1. Use the scaling rule $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + b, Y) = a \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ with $a = 1.8$ and $b = 32$:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(F, S) = 1.8 \times 120 = 216
$$

2. Sales in hundreds are $\frac{S}{100}$. Scale the second variable by $\frac{1}{100}$:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}\Big(T, \frac{S}{100}\Big) = \frac{1}{100} \times 120 = 1.2
$$

**Answer.** $216$ in Fahrenheit, and $1.2$ with sales in hundreds. The link between
weather and sales did not change at all, but the covariance went from $120$ to
$216$ to $1.2$. Covariance carries the **units** of $X$ times the units of $Y$.

## Simulate It

Roll two fair dice. Let $X$ be the first die and $Y$ the sum. As in Example 1,
$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Cov}(X, X_2) = \frac{35}{12} + 0$,
because one die has variance $\frac{35}{12}$
(see [6.5 Variance and Standard Deviation](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
total = die1 + die2

def cov(x, y):
    # average product of the deviations from the mean
    return np.mean((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean()))

print(f"Cov(die1, total): simulation {cov(die1, total):.4f}, "
      f"exact {35 / 12:.4f}")
print(f"Cov(die1, die2):  simulation {cov(die1, die2):.4f}, exact 0.0000")
shortcut = np.mean(die1 * total) - die1.mean() * total.mean()
print(f"Shortcut E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]:   {shortcut:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Cov(die1, total): simulation 2.9117, exact 2.9167
Cov(die1, die2):  simulation -0.0035, exact 0.0000
Shortcut E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]:   2.9117
```

The two independent dice have a covariance very close to 0. The first die and the
total have a covariance very close to $\frac{35}{12} \approx 2.9167$, and both
formulas give the same number.

> **Note:** `np.cov` divides by $n - 1$ instead of $n$ (a "sample covariance").
> With a million samples the difference is invisible. Here we divide by $n$ to
> match the definition.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using $E[XY]$ as the covariance.** $E[XY]$ alone is not the covariance. You
  must subtract $E[X] E[Y]$. In Example 2, $E[XY] = 0.40$ is positive, yet the
  covariance is negative.
- **Reading the size of the covariance as strength.** A covariance of $216$ is not
  "stronger" than $1.2$. Example 3 shows the same relation giving both numbers.
  Use the correlation to measure strength.
- **Thinking that covariance 0 means independent.** Independence gives
  covariance 0, but not the other way around. See
  [11.4](11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md).
- **Getting the scaling wrong.** Each variable brings its own factor, so
  $\operatorname{Cov}(2X, 2Y) = 4 \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$, not
  $2 \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$. Adding a constant changes nothing.
- **Reading covariance as cause.** Ice cream sales and drownings have positive
  covariance because both rise in summer. Neither causes the other. See
  [22.8 Correlation vs Causation](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** PCA starts from the covariances between all pairs
  of features, and finds the directions where the data varies most.
- **Computer science:** in monitoring dashboards, covariance shows which metrics
  move together, such as server load and response time.
- **Finance:** the risk of a portfolio depends on the covariances between the
  assets, not only on each asset's own variance
  ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Engineering:** sensor fusion and Kalman filters track how the errors of
  different sensors move together.
- **Everyday life:** "people who sleep more get better marks" is a claim that a
  covariance is positive. It is a starting point, not a proof of cause.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You know $E[X] = 2$, $E[Y] = 5$ and $E[XY] = 13$. Find $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y] = 13 - 2 \times 5 = 3$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Suppose $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 3$. Find
$\operatorname{Cov}(2X + 7, \thinspace 1 - Y)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, 5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $1 - Y = (-1) Y + 1$. The shifts $7$ and $1$ do not matter, and the factors
$2$ and $-1$ come out:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(2X + 7, \thinspace 1 - Y) = 2 \times (-1) \times \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -2 \times 3 = -6$$

A constant does not vary, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, 5) = 0$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X = 1$ if a morning is cloudy and $Y = 1$ if it rains in the
afternoon (each is $0$ otherwise). The joint PMF is $p(0, 0) = 0.3$,
$p(0, 1) = 0.1$, $p(1, 0) = 0.1$ and $p(1, 1) = 0.5$. Find $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The margins are $P(X = 1) = 0.1 + 0.5 = 0.6$ and $P(Y = 1) = 0.1 + 0.5 = 0.6$, so
$E[X] = E[Y] = 0.6$. The product $XY$ is $1$ only in cell $(1, 1)$, so
$E[XY] = 0.5$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0.5 - 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.5 - 0.36 = 0.14$$

The covariance is positive: a cloudy morning makes afternoon rain more likely.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that $\operatorname{Cov}(X + Y, \thinspace X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) - \operatorname{Var}(Y)$.
What does it say about the sum and the difference of two fair dice?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand with bilinearity, like $(a + b)(a - b)$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X + Y, X - Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) - \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) + \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X) - \operatorname{Cov}(Y, Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) - \operatorname{Var}(Y)$$

The middle terms cancel by symmetry. For two fair dice both variances are
$\frac{35}{12}$, so the sum and the difference have covariance 0. Yet they are
strongly linked: for example, a sum of $12$ forces a difference of $0$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $I_A$ and $I_B$ be the indicators of events $A$ and $B$. Prove
that $\operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B) = P(A \cap B) - P(A) P(B)$. Then show that, when
$P(B) \gt 0$, the covariance is positive exactly when $P(A \mid B) \gt P(A)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[I_A] = P(A)$ and $E[I_B] = P(B)$. The product $I_A I_B$ is $1$ exactly when both
events happen, so $I_A I_B = I_{A \cap B}$ and $E[I_A I_B] = P(A \cap B)$. The
shortcut formula gives the first claim. For the second, use
$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B)$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(I_A, I_B) = P(A \mid B) P(B) - P(A) P(B) = P(B) \big(P(A \mid B) - P(A)\big)$$

Since $P(B) \gt 0$, the sign is the sign of $P(A \mid B) - P(A)$. So "positive
covariance" means "knowing that $B$ happened makes $A$ more likely".

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate one million days from the table in Problem 3 and
estimate the covariance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
x_of_cell = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1])   # cells (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)
y_of_cell = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1])
cell = rng.choice(4, size=n_trials, p=[0.3, 0.1, 0.1, 0.5])
x, y = x_of_cell[cell], y_of_cell[cell]
estimate = np.mean(x * y) - x.mean() * y.mean()
print(f"Simulation: {estimate:.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {0.14:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.1398
Exact:      0.1400
```

</details>

## Summary

- Covariance measures how two random variables move together: positive if they
  rise together, negative if one rises when the other falls.
- Use the shortcut $E[XY] - E[X] E[Y]$ to compute it.
- Covariance is bilinear: constants vanish, factors come out, and sums split.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$, and independent variables
  have covariance 0 (but covariance 0 does not prove independence).
- Its size depends on units, so it does not measure strength by itself.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = E[XY] - E[X] \thinspace E[Y]}
$$
