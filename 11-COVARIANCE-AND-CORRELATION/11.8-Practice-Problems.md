## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy, uses 11.1)** $E[X] = 3$, $E[Y] = -1$ and $E[XY] = -1$. Find
$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(2X - 1, \thinspace 3 - Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the shortcut formula:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y] = -1 - 3 \cdot (-1) = -1 + 3 = 2$$

The constants $-1$ and $3$ do not matter, and the factors $2$ and $-1$ come out:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(2X - 1, \thinspace 3 - Y) = 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -2 \cdot 2 = -4$$

</details>

**W2. (Easy, uses 11.2)** $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 25$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 4$
and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -6$. Find $\rho(X, Y)$ and $\rho(-X, \thinspace 10Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\rho(X, Y) = \frac{-6}{\sqrt{25} \sqrt{4}} = \frac{-6}{5 \times 2} = -0.6$$

Scaling $Y$ by $10$ changes nothing. Flipping the sign of $X$ flips the sign of the
correlation, so $\rho(-X, 10Y) = 0.6$.

</details>

**W3. (Easy, uses 11.3)** $X$ and $Y$ are independent with $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 3$
and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 5$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y)$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(2X + Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Independent means covariance $0$, so only the squared factors matter:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = 3 + 5 = 8, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(2X + Y) = 4 \cdot 3 + 5 = 17$$

Note that the variances **add** in the difference too.

</details>

**W4. (Easy, uses 11.4)** Roll a fair die. Let $X$ be the result and
$Y = (X - 3.5)^2$. Show that $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$. Are $X$ and $Y$
independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $D = X - 3.5$. The values of $D$ are $\pm 0.5$, $\pm 1.5$, $\pm 2.5$, each with
probability $\frac{1}{6}$, so $E[D] = 0$ and $E[D^3] = 0$ (the values cancel in pairs).
Since a shift does not change a covariance:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(D, D^2) = E[D^3] - E[D] E[D^2] = 0 - 0 = 0$$

They are not independent: $Y = 6.25$ happens only when $X$ is $1$ or $6$, so
$P(X = 3, Y = 6.25) = 0$, while $P(X = 3) P(Y = 6.25) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{18}$.

</details>

**W5. (Easy, uses 11.5)** $X_1$ has standard deviation $2$, $X_2$ has standard
deviation $5$, and $\rho(X_1, X_2) = -0.3$. Write the covariance matrix of
$(X_1, X_2)^{\top}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The diagonal holds the variances, $2^2 = 4$ and $5^2 = 25$. The off-diagonal entry is
the covariance:

$$\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21} = \rho \thinspace \sigma_1 \sigma_2 = -0.3 \times 2 \times 5 = -3$$

So $\Sigma$ has rows $(4, -3)$ and $(-3, 25)$.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium, uses 11.1, 11.2)** An urn has 2 red and 2 blue balls. Draw 2 balls
without replacement. Let $X_k = 1$ if draw $k$ is red. Find
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)$ and $\rho(X_1, X_2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each draw on its own is red with probability $\frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$, so
$E[X_k] = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_k) = \frac{1}{4}$. Both draws are red
with probability $\frac{2}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$, so $E[X_1 X_2] = \frac{1}{6}$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{12} - \frac{3}{12} = -\frac{1}{12}$$

$$\rho(X_1, X_2) = \frac{-1/12}{\sqrt{1/4} \sqrt{1/4}} = \frac{-1/12}{1/4} = -\frac{1}{3}$$

A red first ball makes a red second ball less likely, so the correlation is
negative.

</details>

**C2. (Medium, uses 11.3)** Stock A has a yearly return with standard deviation
$0.2$, stock B has standard deviation $0.1$, and their correlation is $-0.5$. You put
a fraction $w$ of your money in A and $1 - w$ in B. (a) Find the variance of your
return for $w = 0.5$. (b) Which $w$ gives the smallest variance, and what is the
smallest standard deviation?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The covariance is $-0.5 \times 0.2 \times 0.1 = -0.01$. The return is
$wA + (1 - w)B$, so:

$$V(w) = 0.04 w^2 + 0.01 (1 - w)^2 - 0.02 \thinspace w(1 - w)$$

(a) At $w = 0.5$:

$$V(0.5) = 0.04 \cdot 0.25 + 0.01 \cdot 0.25 - 0.02 \cdot 0.25 = 0.01 + 0.0025 - 0.005 = 0.0075$$

so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.0075} \approx 0.0866$.

(b) Set the derivative to zero:

$$V'(w) = 0.08 w - 0.02 (1 - w) - 0.02 (1 - 2w) = 0.14 w - 0.04 = 0 \implies w = \frac{2}{7} \approx 0.286$$

$$V\left(\tfrac{2}{7}\right) = \frac{0.04 \cdot 4 + 0.01 \cdot 25 - 0.02 \cdot 10}{49} = \frac{0.21}{49} \approx 0.00429$$

The smallest standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.00429} \approx 0.0655$. This is less than
the $0.1$ of the safer stock alone: the negative correlation makes the mix safer
than either part.

</details>

**C3. (Medium, uses 11.4)** A point $(X, Y)$ is chosen uniformly in the disk
$x^2 + y^2 \le 1$. Explain why $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, and show that $X$ and
$Y$ are dependent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The disk is symmetric under $x \to -x$, so $E[X] = 0$. The same symmetry sends
$XY$ to $-XY$ and does not change the distribution, so $E[XY] = -E[XY]$, which
means $E[XY] = 0$. Therefore $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot E[Y] = 0$.

They are dependent: $X \gt 0.8$ and $Y \gt 0.8$ cannot both hold, since then
$X^2 + Y^2 \gt 0.64 + 0.64 = 1.28 \gt 1$. So
$P(X \gt 0.8, Y \gt 0.8) = 0$, while $P(X \gt 0.8) \gt 0$ and $P(Y \gt 0.8) \gt 0$
(each is a small cap of the disk), so their product is positive. Knowing that $X$
is near $1$ forces $Y$ to be near $0$.

</details>

**C4. (Medium, uses 11.5, 11.6)** A random vector has mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (1, 0, 2)^{\top}$
and covariance matrix:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \cr 1 & 3 & -1 \cr 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Find the mean and variance of $Y = X_1 - 2X_2 + X_3$, and find
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1 + X_2, \thinspace X_3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Mean: $E[Y] = 1 - 2 \cdot 0 + 2 = 3$. For the variance use $\mathbf{a} = (1, -2, 1)^{\top}$
and add $a_i a_j \Sigma_{ij}$ over all 9 boxes (diagonal once, each pair twice):

$$\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} = 1 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 1 + 2(1)(-2)(1) + 2(1)(1)(0) + 2(-2)(1)(-1) = 2 + 12 + 1 - 4 + 0 + 4 = 15$$

By bilinearity:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1 + X_2, X_3) = \Sigma_{13} + \Sigma_{23} = 0 + (-1) = -1$$

</details>

**C5. (Medium, uses 11.6)** Three variables have variance $1$ each, with
$\rho_{12} = 0.9$, $\rho_{13} = 0.9$ and $\rho_{23} = -0.9$. Is this possible?
Explain in words, then prove it with a vector $\mathbf{a}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In words: $X_2$ and $X_3$ both follow $X_1$ closely, so they should follow each other,
not move in opposite directions. Proof: take $\mathbf{a} = (1, -1, -1)^{\top}$. The
quadratic form is the variance of $X_1 - X_2 - X_3$:

$$\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{a} = 3 + 2\big[(1)(-1)(0.9) + (1)(-1)(0.9) + (-1)(-1)(-0.9)\big] = 3 + 2(-2.7) = -2.4$$

A variance cannot be negative, so the matrix is not PSD and these correlations are
impossible.

</details>

**C6. (Medium, uses 11.7)** The temperatures in two cities (in Celsius) have mean
$(20, 25)^{\top}$, variances $16$ and $25$, and covariance $12$. Convert both to
Fahrenheit with $F = 1.8 C + 32$. Find the new mean vector, covariance matrix and
correlation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $A = 1.8 I$ and $\mathbf{b} = (32, 32)^{\top}$. The mean becomes
$(1.8 \cdot 20 + 32, \thinspace 1.8 \cdot 25 + 32) = (68, 77)$. Since
$A\Sigma A^{\top} = 1.8^2 \Sigma = 3.24 \Sigma$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(F_1) = 3.24 \cdot 16 = 51.84, \quad \operatorname{Var}(F_2) = 3.24 \cdot 25 = 81, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(F_1, F_2) = 3.24 \cdot 12 = 38.88$$

The correlation does not change:

$$\rho = \frac{38.88}{\sqrt{51.84} \sqrt{81}} = \frac{38.88}{7.2 \times 9} = \frac{38.88}{64.8} = 0.6 = \frac{12}{4 \times 5}$$

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, uses 11.4)** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, a standard normal (mean $0$,
variance $1$). Let $S$ be $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each,
independent of $X$, and set $Y = SX$. Show that $Y$ is also standard normal, that
$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, and that $X$ and $Y$ are **not** independent. Why
does this not contradict the fact that uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are
independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**$Y$ is standard normal.** The normal density is symmetric, so $-X$ has the same
distribution as $X$. Whether $S = 1$ or $S = -1$, $Y$ is standard normal, so it is
standard normal overall.

**Uncorrelated.** $E[X] = E[Y] = 0$, and by independence of $S$ and $X$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[S X^2] = E[S] \thinspace E[X^2] = 0 \cdot 1 = 0$$

**Dependent.** Always $\lvert Y \rvert = \lvert X \rvert$. So
$P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 1, \lvert Y \rvert \lt 1) = 0$, but

$$P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 1) \thinspace P(\lvert Y \rvert \lt 1) \approx 0.3173 \times 0.6827 \approx 0.2166 \gt 0$$

**No contradiction.** Each of $X$ and $Y$ is normal, but the pair is not jointly
Gaussian: $X + Y$ equals $0$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ (whenever $S = -1$),
and no normal variable has a point with positive probability. The theorem in
[12.8](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md) needs
the pair to be jointly Gaussian.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, uses 11.2, 11.3)** A sensor reads $Y = X + N$, where the true signal $X$
has variance $\sigma^2$, the noise $N$ has variance $\tau^2$, and $X$ and $N$ are
independent. Prove that:

$$
\rho(X, Y) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}}
$$

What happens when the noise is very small, and when it is very large?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By bilinearity and independence, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) + \operatorname{Cov}(X, N) = \sigma^2 + 0$.
By [11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md), $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sigma^2 + \tau^2$. So:

$$\rho(X, Y) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}}$$

When $\tau \to 0$ the correlation tends to $1$: the reading is almost the signal.
When $\tau$ is much larger than $\sigma$ the correlation tends to $0$: the reading
is mostly noise. (Two fair coins, Example 1 of [11.2](11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md),
is the case $\sigma = \tau$, which gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$.)

</details>

**H3. (Hard, uses 11.3, proof)** An urn has $N$ balls, $K$ of them red. Draw $n$
balls without replacement, and let $X$ be the number of red balls. With
$p = \frac{K}{N}$, prove that:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = n p (1 - p) \frac{N - n}{N - 1}
$$

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $X = I_1 + \dots + I_n$, where $I_k = 1$ if draw $k$ is red. Each draw on its
own is red with probability $p$, so $\operatorname{Var}(I_k) = p(1 - p)$. For
$j \ne k$, both are red with probability $\frac{K}{N} \cdot \frac{K - 1}{N - 1}$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(I_j, I_k) = \frac{K(K - 1)}{N(N - 1)} - \frac{K^2}{N^2} = \frac{K \big(N(K - 1) - K(N - 1)\big)}{N^2 (N - 1)} = \frac{K(K - N)}{N^2(N - 1)} = -\frac{p(1 - p)}{N - 1}$$

There are $n$ variances and $n(n - 1)$ ordered pairs $j \ne k$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = n p(1 - p) - n(n - 1) \frac{p(1 - p)}{N - 1} = n p(1 - p) \left(1 - \frac{n - 1}{N - 1}\right) = n p(1 - p) \frac{N - n}{N - 1}$$

The factor $\frac{N - n}{N - 1}$ is less than $1$: drawing without replacement makes
the count less variable, because the draws are negatively correlated. Problem C1 is
the case $N = 4$, $K = 2$, $n = 2$: $2 \cdot \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$,
which matches $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} + 2 \cdot (-\frac{1}{12}) = \frac{1}{3}$.

</details>

**H4. (Hard, uses 11.6, 11.7, proof)** Let $\Sigma$ be a symmetric PSD $n \times n$
matrix and $A$ any $m \times n$ matrix. Prove that $A\Sigma A^{\top}$ is symmetric
and PSD. Explain why this had to be true.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Symmetric.** Use the transpose of a product, reversing the order:

$$(A\Sigma A^{\top})^{\top} = (A^{\top})^{\top} \Sigma^{\top} A^{\top} = A \Sigma A^{\top}$$

**PSD.** Take any vector $\mathbf{v}$ with $m$ entries and let $\mathbf{u} = A^{\top}\mathbf{v}$,
so $\mathbf{u}^{\top} = \mathbf{v}^{\top} A$. Since $\Sigma$ is PSD:

$$\mathbf{v}^{\top} (A\Sigma A^{\top}) \mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{v}^{\top} A) \Sigma (A^{\top}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{u} \ge 0$$

**Why it had to be true.** $A\Sigma A^{\top}$ is the covariance matrix of $A\mathbf{X}$
([11.7](11.7-Linear-Transformations.md)), and every covariance matrix is PSD
([11.6](11.6-Covariance-Matrix-is-PSD.md)). In fact
$\mathbf{v}^{\top}(A\Sigma A^{\top})\mathbf{v} = \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{v}^{\top}A\mathbf{X})$.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Project, uses 11.3)** **How far can diversification go?** Simulate $n$ stocks,
each with variance $1$ and every pair with correlation $\rho = 0.3$. For
$n = 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, 200$, estimate the variance of an equal-weight portfolio (the
average of the $n$ returns) and compare with the exact
$\frac{1}{n}\big(1 + (n - 1)\rho\big)$. What value does it approach?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use a common part shared by all stocks plus an own part for each, as in
[11.3](11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md), Simulate It.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, rho = 20_000, 0.3

for n in [1, 2, 5, 10, 50, 200]:
    common = rng.standard_normal((n_trials, 1))
    own = rng.standard_normal((n_trials, n))
    returns = np.sqrt(rho) * common + np.sqrt(1 - rho) * own
    portfolio = returns.mean(axis=1)
    exact = (1 + (n - 1) * rho) / n
    print(f"n = {n:3d}: simulation {portfolio.var():.4f}, "
          f"exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =   1: simulation 1.0060, exact 1.0000
n =   2: simulation 0.6481, exact 0.6500
n =   5: simulation 0.4400, exact 0.4400
n =  10: simulation 0.3667, exact 0.3700
n =  50: simulation 0.3138, exact 0.3140
n = 200: simulation 0.3008, exact 0.3035
```

The variance falls quickly at first, then levels off near $\rho = 0.3$. Adding
stocks removes their **own** risk but cannot remove the **shared** market risk:
$\frac{1}{n}(1 + (n - 1)\rho) \to \rho$ as $n \to \infty$.

</details>

**S2. (Project, uses 11.4)** Check Challenge H1 by simulation. Estimate
$\rho(X, Y)$, $\rho(\lvert X \rvert, \lvert Y \rvert)$, and compare
$P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 1, \lvert Y \rvert \lt 1)$ with the product of the two
probabilities.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $A = \lbrace \lvert X \rvert \gt 1 \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace \lvert Y \rvert \lt 1 \rbrace$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
x = rng.standard_normal(n_trials)
s = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=n_trials)
y = s * x

a, b = np.abs(x) > 1, np.abs(y) < 1
print(f"rho(X, Y)     = {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]:.4f}  (exact 0)")
rho_sizes = np.corrcoef(np.abs(x), np.abs(y))[0, 1]
print(f"rho(|X|, |Y|) = {rho_sizes:.4f}  (exact 1)")
print(f"P(A and B)    = {np.mean(a & b):.4f}  (exact 0)")
print(f"P(A) P(B)     = {a.mean() * b.mean():.4f}  (exact 0.2166)")
```

Output:

```text
rho(X, Y)     = 0.0020  (exact 0)
rho(|X|, |Y|) = 1.0000  (exact 1)
P(A and B)    = 0.0000  (exact 0)
P(A) P(B)     = 0.2166  (exact 0.2166)
```

$X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated, yet their sizes are perfectly correlated, and the
joint probability is $0$ instead of about $0.217$.

</details>
