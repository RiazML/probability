# 19.5 Rejection Sampling

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 19: Simulation and Monte Carlo](README.md) / 19.5 Rejection Sampling

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [19.4 Inverse Transform Sampling](19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md)

> **Big idea:** To sample from a target density $p$ that is hard to sample, draw candidates from an easy density $q$ that covers it ($p \le Mq$) and keep each candidate with probability $\frac{p(x)}{Mq(x)}$; the kept values have density exactly $p$, and the fraction kept is $\frac{1}{M}$.

---

## Why It Matters

Inverse transform sampling ([19.4](19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md)) needs a quantile function we can compute. Many densities have no usable inverse, especially in several dimensions, and sometimes we only know the density up to a constant (a Bayesian posterior, [4.6](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md)). Rejection sampling needs only the ability to evaluate the density. It is simple, exact, and it shows clearly why sampling in high dimensions is hard, which motivates MCMC ([19.7](19.7-MCMC/README.md)). You have already met a special case: the polar method in [13.8](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md).

## Intuition

Imagine you want a random point under a mountain-shaped curve, where each point under the curve has the same chance. Throw darts at a big rectangle that covers the whole mountain. Darts that land under the curve are kept. Darts that land above it are thrown away. The kept darts are spread evenly under the curve. Now look only at their horizontal positions: they follow the shape of the mountain. The mountain is high in the middle, so more darts land under it there, and the middle values appear more often. That is the target distribution.

The big rectangle can be replaced by any easy "roof" $Mq(x)$ that lies above the target everywhere. A roof that hugs the mountain wastes few darts. A roof far above the mountain wastes many. The fraction kept is the area under the mountain divided by the area under the roof, which is $\frac{1}{M}$ when the mountain has area 1.

## Picture

```text
  density
    |   roof: M q(x)
    +-----------------------------------------+
    |................#########................|
    |............#################............|
    |..........#####################..........|
    |........#########################........|
    |......#############################......|
    |....#################################....|
    |...###################################...|
    |..#####################################..|
    |.#######################################.|
    +-----------------------------------------+--- x
      # = accepted (under the target p)   . = rejected
```

A candidate $x$ is drawn from $q$, and a random height $u \cdot Mq(x)$ under the roof is chosen. If the height falls under the target curve the candidate is kept.

## Definition

Let $p$ be the **target** density we want to sample. Let $q$ be a **proposal** density that is easy to sample, and let $M \ge 1$ be a constant with

$$
p(x) \le M q(x) \qquad \text{for all } x
$$

The function $Mq$ is called the **envelope** (or roof). **Rejection sampling** repeats these steps until one value is kept:

1. Draw a candidate $X \sim q$.
2. Draw $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, independent of $X$.
3. If $U \le \frac{p(X)}{M q(X)}$, **accept** $X$ and stop. Otherwise **reject** it and go back to step 1.

**In words:** propose from the easy density, then accept with probability equal to the height of the target divided by the height of the roof.

The result: the accepted $X$ has density $p$, each candidate is accepted with probability $\frac{1}{M}$, and the number of candidates needed has mean $M$.

**Unnormalized target.** Often we know only $\tilde{p}(x) = Z p(x)$ for an unknown constant $Z$. Rejection sampling still works with $\tilde{p}(x) \le \tilde{M} q(x)$ and the test $U \le \frac{\tilde{p}(X)}{\tilde{M} q(X)}$. The acceptance probability is then $\frac{Z}{\tilde{M}}$. We never need $Z$.

## Why It Is True

Take a small interval around $x$ of width $dx$. A candidate lands there with probability $q(x) \thinspace dx$. It is then accepted with probability $\frac{p(x)}{Mq(x)}$. So

$$
P(X \in dx \text{ and accepted}) = q(x) \cdot \frac{p(x)}{M q(x)} \thinspace dx = \frac{p(x)}{M} \thinspace dx
$$

Add this over all $x$ to get the total acceptance probability:

$$
P(\text{accept}) = \int \frac{p(x)}{M} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{M} \qquad \text{(since } \int p = 1 \text{)}
$$

Now divide (the definition of conditional probability, [4.1](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.1-Conditional-Probability.md)):

$$
P(X \in dx \mid \text{accepted}) = \frac{p(x) \thinspace dx / M}{1 / M} = p(x) \thinspace dx
$$

So the accepted values have density $p$.

**How many candidates?** Each round is an independent trial that succeeds with probability $\frac{1}{M}$. The number of rounds $N$ until the first success is geometric ([7.3](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md)), so

$$
E[N] = M
$$

**Why $p \le Mq$ is needed.** If $p(x) \gt Mq(x)$ somewhere, the ratio $\frac{p}{Mq}$ exceeds 1 there and cannot be a probability, so the values near that $x$ are accepted too rarely and the result has the wrong shape. In particular the proposal must have tails at least as heavy as the target: a normal proposal cannot cover a Cauchy target, because $\frac{p}{q} \to \infty$ in the tails (Practice 4).

**The curse of dimensions.** In $d$ dimensions, a roof that is a constant over a box has a value $M$ that grows very fast with $d$, because the target's mass sits in a tiny part of the box. Example 3 shows the numbers.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Beta(2, 2) From a Uniform Proposal

**Problem.** Sample from the density $p(x) = 6x(1 - x)$ on $[0, 1]$ (the $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ law, [9.5](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md)), using the proposal $q = \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find $M$, the acceptance rate, and carry out two trials by hand.

**Solution.**

1. The density $p$ is largest at $x = 0.5$, where $p(0.5) = 6 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 1.5$. Since $q(x) = 1$ on $[0, 1]$, the smallest $M$ with $p \le Mq$ is $M = 1.5$.
2. The acceptance rate is $\frac{1}{M} = \frac{1}{1.5} = \frac{2}{3}$, and the mean number of candidates per accepted value is $1.5$.
3. Trial 1: candidate $x = 0.3$, $u = 0.5$. We have $p(0.3) = 6 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 1.26$, so $\frac{p}{Mq} = \frac{1.26}{1.5} = 0.84$. Since $0.5 \le 0.84$, accept.
4. Trial 2: candidate $x = 0.9$, $u = 0.7$. We have $p(0.9) = 6 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.54$, so $\frac{p}{Mq} = \frac{0.54}{1.5} = 0.36$. Since $0.7 \gt 0.36$, reject.

**Answer.** $M = 1.5$, acceptance rate $\frac{2}{3}$. The candidate $0.3$ is accepted and $0.9$ is rejected.

**Check.** Values near the edges, such as $0.9$, have a low target density and are rejected often, while values near $0.5$ are accepted with probability close to 1. The first code block below finds an acceptance rate of $0.6673$ and mean $0.5000$ (exact $0.5$).

### Example 2: Normal Numbers From Exponential Proposals

**Problem.** The half-normal density is $p(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$ for $x \ge 0$. Sample from it using the proposal $q(x) = e^{-x}$ ($\text{Exp}(1)$, rate 1). Find $M$ and the acceptance probability, then show how to get $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

**Solution.**

1. The ratio is $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \thinspace e^{x - x^2/2}$.
2. Maximize the exponent $x - \frac{x^2}{2}$: its derivative $1 - x$ is zero at $x = 1$, where the exponent is $\frac{1}{2}$. So

$$
M = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \thinspace e^{1/2} = \sqrt{\frac{2e}{\pi}} \approx 1.3155
$$

3. The acceptance probability of a candidate $x$ is

$$
\frac{p(x)}{M q(x)} = \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi} \thinspace e^{x - x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2/\pi} \thinspace e^{1/2}} = e^{x - x^2/2 - 1/2} = e^{-(x-1)^2/2}
$$

4. The overall acceptance rate is $\frac{1}{M} = 0.7602$.
5. To get a normal number, multiply the accepted value by $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. By symmetry the result is $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

**Answer.** $M = \sqrt{2e / \pi} \approx 1.3155$, the acceptance test is $U \le e^{-(X-1)^2/2}$, and 76% of the candidates are accepted.

**Check.** The second code block gives an acceptance rate of $0.7605$ (exact $0.7602$), a sample mean $-0.0017$, variance $1.0026$ and $P(Z \le 1.96) = 0.9749$ (exact $0.9750$). This sampler uses only exponentials, which come from the inverse transform of [19.4](19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md).

### Example 3: A Ball Inside a Box, in Many Dimensions

**Problem.** To pick a uniform point in the unit ball in $d$ dimensions, draw a uniform point in the cube $[-1, 1]^d$ and keep it if its distance to the centre is at most 1. What is the acceptance rate for $d = 2, 3, 10, 20$?

**Solution.**

1. The target is uniform on the ball, the proposal is uniform on the cube. The acceptance rate is $\frac{\text{volume of ball}}{\text{volume of cube}} = \frac{V_d}{2^d}$, where $V_d = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)}$ is the volume of the unit ball.
2. $d = 2$: $\frac{\pi}{4} = 0.7854$.
3. $d = 3$: $\frac{4\pi / 3}{8} = \frac{\pi}{6} = 0.5236$.
4. $d = 10$: $V_{10} = \frac{\pi^5}{120} = 2.5502$, and $2^{10} = 1024$, so the rate is $0.00249$.
5. $d = 20$: $V_{20} = \frac{\pi^{10}}{10!} = 0.02581$, and $2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$, so the rate is $2.46 \times 10^{-8}$.

**Answer.** The acceptance rate is $0.785$, $0.524$, $0.0025$ and $0.0000000246$ for $d = 2, 3, 10, 20$.

**Check.** The third code block confirms $d = 2, 3, 5, 10$ by simulation. For $d = 20$, about one candidate in 40 million is accepted, so the method is useless. In high dimensions almost all of the volume of the cube is in the corners, far from the centre. This is the **curse of dimensionality**, and it is the reason for MCMC ([19.7](19.7-MCMC/README.md)).

## Simulate It

First, Example 1 with $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ candidates:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_proposals = 1_000_000
M = 1.5                                          # max of p(x) = 6x(1-x) is 1.5 at x = 0.5

# Target Beta(2,2): p(x) = 6 x (1 - x). Proposal q = Unif(0,1), so q(x) = 1.
x = rng.random(n_proposals)                      # candidates from the proposal
u = rng.random(n_proposals)                      # uniform numbers for the accept test
accept = u <= 6 * x * (1 - x) / M                # accept with prob p(x) / (M q(x))
sample = x[accept]

rate = accept.mean()
p_low = np.mean(sample <= 0.25)
print(f"acceptance rate {rate:.4f} (exact {1 / M:.4f}, SE {np.sqrt(rate * (1 - rate) / n_proposals):.4f})")
print(f"accepted {sample.size}, mean {sample.mean():.4f} (exact 0.5000), var {sample.var(ddof=1):.4f} (exact 0.0500)")
print(f"P(X <= 0.25) {p_low:.4f} (exact {3 * 0.25**2 - 2 * 0.25**3:.4f}, SE {np.sqrt(p_low * (1 - p_low) / sample.size):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
acceptance rate 0.6673 (exact 0.6667, SE 0.0005)
accepted 667286, mean 0.5000 (exact 0.5000), var 0.0501 (exact 0.0500)
P(X <= 0.25) 0.1564 (exact 0.1562, SE 0.0004)
```

The accepted sample has the right mean, variance and CDF value. Next, the half-normal sampler of Example 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_proposals = 400_000
M = np.sqrt(2 * np.e / np.pi)                    # max of p(x) / q(x) at x = 1

# Target: half-normal p(x) = sqrt(2/pi) exp(-x^2/2), x >= 0. Proposal: Exp(1).
x = rng.exponential(1.0, n_proposals)
u = rng.random(n_proposals)
accept = u <= np.exp(-(x - 1) ** 2 / 2)          # p(x) / (M q(x)) simplifies to this
half = x[accept]

# Give each accepted value a random sign: the result is N(0, 1).
sign = np.where(rng.random(half.size) < 0.5, -1.0, 1.0)
z = sign * half
print(f"acceptance rate {accept.mean():.4f} (exact {1 / M:.4f})")
print(f"mean {z.mean():.4f}  var {z.var(ddof=1):.4f}  P(Z <= 1.96) {np.mean(z <= 1.96):.4f} (exact 0.9750)")
print(f"mean of |Z| {half.mean():.4f} (exact {np.sqrt(2 / np.pi):.4f}, SE {half.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(half.size):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
acceptance rate 0.7605 (exact 0.7602)
mean -0.0017  var 1.0026  P(Z <= 1.96) 0.9749 (exact 0.9750)
mean of |Z| 0.7987 (exact 0.7979, SE 0.0011)
```

Last, the curse of dimensionality from Example 3:

```python
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_proposals = 1_000_000

# Uniform in the unit ball by rejection from the cube [-1, 1]^d.
for d in [2, 3, 5, 10]:
    points = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(n_proposals, d))
    inside = (points**2).sum(axis=1) <= 1
    exact = math.pi ** (d / 2) / math.gamma(d / 2 + 1) / 2**d
    print(f"d = {d:>2}: acceptance {inside.mean():.5f}  exact {exact:.5f}")
print(f"d = 20: exact acceptance {math.pi ** 10 / math.gamma(11) / 2**20:.2e}")
```

Output:

```text
d =  2: acceptance 0.78581  exact 0.78540
d =  3: acceptance 0.52373  exact 0.52360
d =  5: acceptance 0.16416  exact 0.16449
d = 10: acceptance 0.00249  exact 0.00249
d = 20: exact acceptance 2.46e-08
```

For $d = 20$ the exact value is shown because a simulation with $10^6$ candidates would almost never see an accepted point.

## Common Mistakes

- **Choosing $M$ too small.** If $p(x) \gt Mq(x)$ for some $x$, the sample has the wrong shape. Always find the maximum of $\frac{p}{q}$ (by calculus, or by checking a fine grid) and set $M$ to it.
- **Choosing $M$ too large.** The result is still correct, but a huge $M$ wastes almost all candidates. Choose a proposal $q$ that looks like $p$ and take the smallest valid $M$.
- **Using a proposal with lighter tails than the target.** Then $\frac{p}{q}$ is unbounded and no finite $M$ exists. Use a heavier-tailed proposal (for a normal target, a Cauchy or a $t$ distribution works).
- **Drawing a new $U$ in the wrong way.** The accept test needs one fresh uniform number per candidate, independent of the candidate. Do not reuse the candidate's own uniform number.
- **Counting rejected values as samples.** The output is only the accepted values. If you need exactly $n$ samples, keep proposing until you have $n$ accepted values; do not stop after $n$ proposals.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** sampling from a truncated distribution (a normal limited to $[0, \infty)$) and from the posterior of a small model; the "accept or reject" test is the same idea as the Metropolis step in MCMC ([19.7.1](19.7-MCMC/19.7.1-Metropolis-Hastings.md)).
- **Computer science:** generating random points in a region of an unusual shape for tests, graphics and games (rejection from a bounding box); the polar method for normal numbers ([13.8](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md)).
- **Physics:** in particle simulations, a particle's energy and direction are drawn by rejection from a density that has no inverse.
- **Statistics:** random number libraries use rejection (the ziggurat method for normals, sampling from the gamma law) as the core of their samplers.
- **Everyday life:** throwing away unusable picks until a good one comes up, such as drawing a random card until it is not one you already have.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** The target is $p(x) = 2x$ on $[0, 1]$ and the proposal is $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find the smallest $M$ and the acceptance rate. Is the candidate $x = 0.8$ with $u = 0.5$ accepted?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The maximum of $p$ is $p(1) = 2$, so $M = 2$ and the acceptance rate is $\frac{1}{2}$. For $x = 0.8$: $\frac{p}{Mq} = \frac{1.6}{2} = 0.8$. Since $u = 0.5 \le 0.8$, accept.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You need 1,000 accepted samples and $M = 2.5$. About how many candidates must you draw?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each accepted value costs $M = 2.5$ candidates on average, so you need about $1000 \times 2.5 = 2500$ candidates.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You know only $\tilde{p}(x) = x^2 (1 - x)$ on $[0, 1]$ (the shape of $\text{Beta}(3, 2)$). Use the proposal $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find $\tilde{M}$ and the acceptance rate.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The maximum of $\tilde{p}$: its derivative $2x - 3x^2 = 0$ gives $x = \frac{2}{3}$, where $\tilde{p} = \frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{4}{27}$. So $\tilde{M} = \frac{4}{27}$. The constant $Z = \int_0^1 x^2 (1 - x) \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12}$. The acceptance rate is

$$\frac{Z}{\tilde{M}} = \frac{1/12}{4/27} = \frac{27}{48} = 0.5625$$

A simulation with 200,000 candidates gave $0.5635$ and an accepted mean of $0.5995$ (exact mean $\frac{3}{5}$).

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Why can you not sample the standard Cauchy density $p(x) = \frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$ by rejection from the standard normal proposal $q(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$? Which proposal works?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The ratio $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\pi} \cdot \frac{e^{x^2/2}}{1 + x^2}$ grows without bound as $x \to \infty$, because $e^{x^2/2}$ grows much faster than $1 + x^2$. So no finite $M$ exists. A proposal with heavy tails is needed, such as the Cauchy itself (then $M = 1$), or a Student $t$ with few degrees of freedom. In practice the Cauchy is sampled by inverse transform ([19.4](19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md), Practice 6).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove, for a discrete target $p$ on a finite set with proposal $q$ and $p(k) \le Mq(k)$, that the accepted value has the distribution $p$, and that the number of candidates $N$ has $E[N] = M$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In one round, the candidate is $k$ and it is accepted with probability $q(k) \cdot \frac{p(k)}{Mq(k)} = \frac{p(k)}{M}$. Summing over $k$ gives

$$P(\text{accept}) = \sum_k \frac{p(k)}{M} = \frac{1}{M}$$

Given acceptance, the value is $k$ with probability $\frac{p(k)/M}{1/M} = p(k)$, so the accepted value has distribution $p$. Rounds are independent, and each succeeds with probability $\frac{1}{M}$, so $N \sim \text{Geom}(\frac{1}{M})$ with $E[N] = \frac{1}{1/M} = M$. Because each round starts afresh, the accepted value comes from a single round with the law above, whatever the number of failures before it.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate the acceptance rate of rejection sampling of a uniform point in the unit ball for $d = 2, 3, 5$ with 200,000 candidates, and compare with $\frac{V_d}{2^d}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_proposals = 200_000
for d in [2, 3, 5]:
    points = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(n_proposals, d))     # proposal: uniform on the cube
    inside = (points**2).sum(axis=1) <= 1                  # accept points in the ball
    exact = math.pi ** (d / 2) / math.gamma(d / 2 + 1) / 2**d
    print(f"d = {d}: simulation {inside.mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
d = 2: simulation 0.7853, exact 0.7854
d = 3: simulation 0.5232, exact 0.5236
d = 5: simulation 0.1655, exact 0.1645
```

For $d = 5$ the standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.1645 \times 0.8355}{200{,}000}} = 0.0008$, so the difference $0.0010$ is within about 1.2 standard errors.

</details>

## Summary

- Rejection sampling draws $X \sim q$ and accepts with probability $\frac{p(X)}{Mq(X)}$, where $p \le Mq$.
- The accepted values have exactly the density $p$. The acceptance rate is $\frac{1}{M}$ and the mean number of candidates per sample is $M$.
- It works with an unnormalized target $\tilde{p} = Zp$: we never need $Z$.
- The proposal must have heavier tails than the target, and should look like it, to keep $M$ small.
- In high dimensions $M$ explodes (the curse of dimensionality), which is why MCMC is needed.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{accept } X \sim q \text{ with probability } \frac{p(X)}{M q(X)}, \qquad P(\text{accept}) = \frac{1}{M}}
$$
