**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/README.md)

> **Big idea:** When the exact answer is too hard to compute, let the computer run the random experiment many times and count: the fraction of runs where the event happens is an estimate, and its error is about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$.

---

## Why It Matters

Most real probability problems have no neat formula. A project has four tasks with random durations. A network has thousands of links that can fail. A game has too many states to list. In all of these, you can still write a program that plays the experiment once, and then run it many times. This is **simulation**. It also lets you check every formula in this book: if the formula and the simulation disagree, one of them has a bug. The rest of Module 19 shows how to do this well and how to know how far to trust the numbers.

## Intuition

Suppose you shuffle a deck of 52 cards and look at the cards one by one. What is the chance that **no card** is in its old place? The exact answer needs inclusion-exclusion ([3.5](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.5-Inclusion-Exclusion-for-Probability.md)) with 52 terms (see [2.11.2 Derangements and Matching](../02-COUNTING/2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.2-Derangements-and-Matching.md)). But you do not need that to get a good answer. Shuffle a deck, check, and write down "yes" or "no". Do it 200,000 times on the computer. If "yes" happened 73,500 times, the answer is about $\frac{73{,}500}{200{,}000} = 0.3675$.

This is exactly how you would find the chance of a six on a strange die: roll it a lot and count. The computer only makes the "roll it a lot" part fast. The idea works because of the Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)): the fraction of successes gets close to the true probability when the number of runs is large.

Two warnings. First, the estimate is never exact: a different seed gives a slightly different number, so we must always say **how large the error is**. Second, a simulation is only as good as the model inside it. If your program does not describe the real experiment, the answer is wrong, however many runs you make.

## Picture

```text
   +----------------+     +----------------+     +----------------+
   | 1. Model       | --> | 2. Draw random | --> | 3. Compute the |
   | the experiment |     |    inputs      |     |    outcome     |
   +----------------+     +----------------+     +----------------+
                                                         |
   +----------------+     +----------------+             |
   | 5. Average and | <-- | 4. Repeat n    | <-----------+
   |    error bar   |     |    times       |
   +----------------+     +----------------+
```

The simulation loop: run one random experiment, record the result, repeat, then average and report the error.

## Definition

A **simulation** is a computer program that plays a random experiment many times, using random numbers for the random parts. One run is called a **trial**. The **Monte Carlo method** is the use of such random trials to estimate a number, such as a probability or an expected value. (The name comes from the casino in Monaco.)

To estimate $p = P(A)$ for an event $A$, run $n$ independent trials. In trial $i$ record $I_i = 1$ if $A$ happens and $I_i = 0$ if it does not. Use the fraction of successes as the **estimate**:

$$
\hat{p}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} I_i
$$

**In words:** the estimate is the number of trials where $A$ happened, divided by the number of trials.

The typical size of the error is the **standard error** (SE). It is the standard deviation of the estimate:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat{p}_n) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_n (1 - \hat{p}_n)}{n}}
$$

where $n$ is the number of trials. The right-hand side is the version you can compute, because it uses $\hat{p}_n$ in place of the unknown $p$.

**In words:** the error is about a constant times $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. Four times more trials make the error half as big.

## Why It Is True

Each $I_i$ is a Bernoulli variable with success probability $p$ ([7.1](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.1-Bernoulli.md)): its mean is $p$ and its variance is $p(1-p)$. The trials are independent, so we can add variances ([6.6](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[\hat{p}_n] &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[I_i] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot np = p && \text{(linearity)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}_n) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n p (1 - p) = \frac{p(1 - p)}{n} && \text{(independent trials)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the estimate has the right mean (it is **unbiased**: it is not too high or too low on average) and its spread shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. The Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) adds that $\hat{p}_n$ is close to a normal variable, so about 95% of the time $\hat{p}_n$ lies within $1.96$ standard errors of $p$. The general version, for averages of any quantity, is the subject of [19.3](19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Simulation Needs a Model

**Problem.** You want the probability that the sum of three fair dice is at least 15. Describe the simulation, then find the exact answer to check it.

**Solution.**

1. Model: each die is a uniform number from 1 to 6, independent of the others.
2. One trial: draw three numbers, add them, record $I = 1$ if the sum is at least 15.
3. Repeat $n$ times and take the average of the $I$ values.
4. Exact answer, by counting the 216 equally likely triples. Sum 15 has 10 triples, sum 16 has 6, sum 17 has 3, and sum 18 has 1:

$$
P(\text{sum} \ge 15) = \frac{10 + 6 + 3 + 1}{216} = \frac{20}{216} \approx 0.0926
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{sum} \ge 15) = \frac{20}{216} \approx 0.0926$.

**Check.** With $n = 100{,}000$ trials the standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.0926 \times 0.9074}{100{,}000}} \approx 0.0009$. A simulation with 100,000 trials gave $0.0924$, well inside one standard error of the exact value.

### Example 2: A Project Deadline

**Problem.** A project has four tasks. Task A comes first and takes $\text{Unif}(2, 4)$ days. Then tasks B and C run in parallel: B takes $\text{Unif}(3, 7)$ days and C takes $\text{Unif}(4, 6)$ days. When both are finished, task D starts and takes $\text{Unif}(2, 5)$ days. All durations are independent. What is the probability that the project finishes within 13 days?

**Solution.**

1. The total time is $T = A + \max(B, C) + D$. The parallel part lasts as long as the slower of B and C.
2. The distribution of $\max(B, C)$ is piecewise ([13.6](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.6-Max-and-Min.md)), and adding $A$ and $D$ needs two more convolutions ([13.4](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md)). The exact integral is painful, but the simulation is a few lines.
3. Draw $A, B, C, D$, compute $T$, record $I = 1$ if $T \le 13$, and repeat. The code below does this with $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ trials.

**Answer.** $P(T \le 13) \approx 0.764$ with a standard error of about $0.0004$. The mean duration is about $12.04$ days.

**Check.** The mean of $T$ is $3 + E[\max(B, C)] + 3.5$. Since $E[\max(B, C)]$ is at least $E[B] = 5$ and at least $E[C] = 5$, the mean must be at least $11.5$. The simulated $12.04$ agrees. A promise of "13 days" is therefore kept about 3 times in 4: not safe enough for a contract.

## Simulate It

This code checks the card-shuffle question from the Intuition. The exact answer, from inclusion-exclusion, is $\sum_{k=0}^{52} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!} \approx 0.3679$, which is very close to $\frac{1}{e}$.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# Each row is one shuffled deck: the card that sits at each place.
decks = np.argsort(rng.random((n_trials, 52)), axis=1)
# A "match" is a card that is back in its starting place.
no_match = ~(decks == np.arange(52)).any(axis=1)

estimate = no_match.mean()
std_error = np.sqrt(estimate * (1 - estimate) / n_trials)
exact = sum((-1) ** k / math.factorial(k) for k in range(53))

print(f"Simulation: {estimate:.4f}  (standard error {std_error:.4f})")
print(f"Exact:      {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.3675  (standard error 0.0011)
Exact:      0.3679
```

The difference is $0.0004$, much smaller than one standard error ($0.0011$). Now the project example:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Task durations in days (independent, uniform).
a = rng.uniform(2, 4, n_trials)
b = rng.uniform(3, 7, n_trials)
c = rng.uniform(4, 6, n_trials)
d = rng.uniform(2, 5, n_trials)

# A first, then B and C in parallel (wait for the slower), then D.
total = a + np.maximum(b, c) + d

on_time = total <= 13
p_hat = on_time.mean()
std_error = np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n_trials)
print(f"P(done within 13 days) = {p_hat:.4f}  (standard error {std_error:.4f})")
print(f"Mean total duration    = {total.mean():.3f} days")
```

Output:

```text
P(done within 13 days) = 0.7640  (standard error 0.0004)
Mean total duration    = 12.041 days
```

No exact value is shown here: this is the kind of problem where simulation is the practical tool, and the standard error tells you the answer is good to about three decimal places.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reporting a simulation result without an error.** "The answer is 0.764" hides how many digits are real. Always give the standard error, or the number of trials, next to the estimate.
- **Too few trials.** With 100 trials the standard error of a probability near $\frac{1}{2}$ is $0.05$, so the second digit is noise. For one more correct digit, you need 100 times more trials.
- **Treating a simulation as a proof.** A simulation of a million trials cannot prove that $p$ is exactly $0.5$. It can only say that $p$ is probably within a small interval around the estimate.
- **Simulating the wrong experiment.** If the code forgets that the cards are drawn without replacement, or uses the wrong rate, the answer converges nicely to the wrong number. Check the model on a case where you know the answer.
- **Using Python loops when vectorized code is possible.** A loop of a million trials is slow. Draw all the random numbers at once with numpy, as in the examples.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** estimating an expected loss, sampling from a model, dropout and data augmentation, and reinforcement learning ("roll out" a policy many times and average the reward).
- **Computer science:** testing randomized algorithms, load balancers and hash functions, and estimating the performance of a network or a cache before building it.
- **Finance and insurance:** pricing options and measuring the risk of a portfolio when no formula exists.
- **Physics and engineering:** particle transport, the reliability of a machine with many parts, and weather forecasts that run many slightly different simulations.
- **Everyday life:** a coach who simulates a season to see how often a team makes the finals, or a planner who simulates travel times before choosing a flight.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You simulate 10,000 flips of a coin and get 5,060 heads. Is there evidence that the coin is biased?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If the coin is fair, the standard error of the fraction of heads is

$$\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{10{,}000}} = 0.005$$

The observed fraction is $0.506$, which is $0.006$ above $0.5$. That is $\frac{0.006}{0.005} = 1.2$ standard errors. A gap of 1.2 standard errors is common for a fair coin, so there is no real evidence of bias.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Name the five steps of a Monte Carlo estimate of $P(A)$, in order.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. Model the experiment. 2. Draw the random inputs. 3. Compute the outcome and record whether $A$ happened. 4. Repeat $n$ times. 5. Average the records and report the standard error.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You want to estimate a probability near $0.5$ with a standard error of at most $0.001$. How many trials do you need?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $\sqrt{\frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{n}} \le 0.001$:

$$n \ge \frac{0.25}{0.001^2} = 250{,}000$$

For a probability far from $0.5$, $p(1-p)$ is smaller and fewer trials are enough. The value $0.25$ is the largest possible $p(1-p)$, so $250{,}000$ trials always suffice.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A simulation with 1,000,000 trials gives $\hat{p} = 0.4999$. A student says: "This proves $p = 0.5$." What is wrong, and what can we say instead?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{1{,}000{,}000}} = 0.0005$, so $\hat{p}$ is only known to within about $\pm 0.001$ (two standard errors). The true $p$ could be $0.5003$ or $0.4991$. We can say that $p$ is very probably between $0.4989$ and $0.5009$. A proof needs mathematics, not a finite number of random trials.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $\hat{p}_n$ is unbiased, and that the number of successes $S_n = \sum_i I_i$ has the distribution $\text{Bin}(n, p)$. Use this to confirm $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}_n) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The $n$ trials are independent, and each is a success with probability $p$. This is the definition of the binomial, so $S_n \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ with mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$. Since $\hat{p}_n = \frac{S_n}{n}$:

$$E[\hat{p}_n] = \frac{np}{n} = p$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2} \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \frac{np(1-p)}{n^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Shuffle a deck of 5 cards 100,000 times and estimate the probability that no card is in its old place. The exact value is $\frac{44}{120}$, because there are 44 shuffles of 5 cards with no match out of $5! = 120$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
decks = np.argsort(rng.random((n_trials, 5)), axis=1)
no_match = ~(decks == np.arange(5)).any(axis=1)
print(f"Simulation: {no_match.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {44 / 120:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.3662
Exact:      0.3667
```

The standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.3667 \times 0.6333}{100{,}000}} \approx 0.0015$, and the difference $0.0005$ is well within it. Notice that 5 cards give almost the same answer as 52: the probability of no match is close to $\frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679$ for every deck size.

</details>

## Summary

- Simulation means writing code for the random experiment, running it many times, and averaging.
- The estimate of a probability is the fraction of successful trials. It is unbiased, and its standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$.
- The error shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$: ten times more precision costs a hundred times more trials.
- Always report the standard error next to the estimate, and check the model on a case with a known answer.
- A simulation estimates and checks; it does not prove.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\hat{p}_n = \frac{\text{number of successes}}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_n (1 - \hat{p}_n)}{n}}}
$$
