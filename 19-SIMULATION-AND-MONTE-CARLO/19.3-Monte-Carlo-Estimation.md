# 19.3 Monte Carlo Estimation

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 19: Simulation and Monte Carlo](README.md) / 19.3 Monte Carlo Estimation

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [19.2 Pseudo-Random Numbers and Seeds](19.2-Pseudo-Random-Numbers-and-Seeds.md)

> **Big idea:** Any expectation $E[f(X)]$, and so any probability or integral, can be estimated by averaging $f$ over random samples, and the error of the average is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, which we can estimate from the same samples.

---

## Why It Matters

In [19.1](19.1-Why-Simulate.md) we estimated probabilities by counting. Now we do the general step. Almost every quantity in probability is an expected value: a probability is $E[I_A]$, a mean is $E[X]$, a variance is $E[X^2] - E[X]^2$, and an integral is an expected value under a uniform variable. One recipe estimates all of them, and the same formula for the error works for all of them. This is the engine inside Bayesian inference, reinforcement learning and risk models.

## Intuition

Suppose you want the average height of 50 million people. You do not measure everyone. You pick 1,000 people at random and average their heights. The sample average is close to the true average, and you can say how close from the spread of the 1,000 heights.

Monte Carlo does the same with a formula instead of people. To find the area under a curve, pick random points $x$ in the interval, compute the height $g(x)$ of the curve at each, and average. The average height times the width of the interval is the area. Think of a lake with an unknown depth profile: drop a stick at 1,000 random places, and the average depth times the lake's area is the volume.

The error has a simple rule. If one sample has spread $\sigma$, an average of $n$ samples has spread $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$. Ten times more precision costs a hundred times more samples. The rule does not depend on how many dimensions the problem has, which is why Monte Carlo wins on large problems.

## Picture

```text
  Standard error of the estimate of pi (quarter-circle method)

  n = 100        ################                   0.164
  n = 10,000     ##                                 0.016
  n = 1,000,000  .                                  0.0016

  (one # = 0.01)    100 times more points  ->  10 times smaller error
```

The error falls like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$: each extra correct digit needs 100 times more samples.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a random variable (or vector) and $f$ a function, and suppose we want

$$
\theta = E[f(X)]
$$

The **Monte Carlo estimator** draws $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}$ the law of $X$ and averages:

$$
\hat{\theta}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i)
$$

**In words:** estimate the mean of $f(X)$ by the average of $f$ over $n$ random samples.

Let $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(f(X))$, and assume it is finite. Then:

$$
E[\hat{\theta}_n] = \theta, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

In practice $\sigma$ is unknown, so we replace it by the **sample standard deviation** $s$ of the values $f(X_1), \dots, f(X_n)$:

$$
s^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big( f(X_i) - \hat{\theta}_n \big)^2, \qquad \widehat{\operatorname{SE}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

An approximate 95% **confidence interval** (a range that contains $\theta$ in about 95% of repeated experiments) is

$$
\hat{\theta}_n \pm 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

To get an error of at most $\varepsilon$ with 95% confidence, you need about

$$
n \ge \left( \frac{1.96 \thinspace \sigma}{\varepsilon} \right)^2
$$

**In words:** the answer is the sample average, the error bar is $1.96$ times $s$ over $\sqrt{n}$, and halving the error needs four times more samples.

Two special cases come from [19.1](19.1-Why-Simulate.md) and from integration:

- **Probability.** $f = I_A$ gives $\theta = P(A)$ and $\sigma^2 = p(1-p)$.
- **Integral.** If $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, then $E[g(U)] = \int_0^1 g(x) \thinspace dx$, because the density of $U$ is 1 on $[0, 1]$ (LOTUS, [8.6](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md)). On an interval $[a, b]$ use $(b - a) \thinspace E[g(a + (b - a)U)]$.

## Why It Is True

The $f(X_i)$ are i.i.d. with mean $\theta$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Linearity and independence ([6.6](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)) give

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[\hat{\theta}_n] &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[f(X_i)] = \theta && \text{(unbiased)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(f(X_i)) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} && \text{(independent samples)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the standard error is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$. Two limit theorems finish the job:

- The **Strong Law of Large Numbers** ([17.4](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) says $\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta$ with probability 1. The estimator is consistent: more samples always help.
- The **Central Limit Theorem** ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) says $\frac{\hat{\theta}_n - \theta}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$ is close to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, so $\theta$ lies within $1.96 \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ of $\hat{\theta}_n$ with probability about $0.95$. Replacing $\sigma$ by $s$ is allowed by Slutsky's theorem ([17.8](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md)).

**A guarantee instead of an approximation.** If $f$ takes values in $[a, b]$, Hoeffding's inequality ([16.7](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)) gives $P(\lvert \hat{\theta}_n - \theta \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le 2 e^{-2n\varepsilon^2 / (b-a)^2}$ for every $n$, with no normal approximation. It needs more samples than the CLT recipe (Practice 5).

**Why the rate does not depend on the dimension.** The formula $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ never mentions the number of coordinates of $X$. A grid in 10 dimensions with only 2 points per axis already needs $2^{10} = 1024$ points, and 10 points per axis need $10^{10}$. Monte Carlo uses the same $n$ in any dimension, provided $\sigma$ stays reasonable.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: An Integral With No Closed Form

**Problem.** Estimate $\theta = \int_0^1 e^{-x^2} \thinspace dx$. First do it by hand with 4 numbers, then read the result with $n = 100{,}000$ from the code below.

**Solution.**

1. Write $\theta = E[f(U)]$ with $f(u) = e^{-u^2}$ and $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$.
2. Take the 4 numbers $u = 0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 0.9$. Compute $f$:

$$
f(0.1) = e^{-0.01} = 0.9900, \quad f(0.4) = e^{-0.16} = 0.8521, \quad f(0.7) = e^{-0.49} = 0.6126, \quad f(0.9) = e^{-0.81} = 0.4449
$$

3. The average is $\hat{\theta}_4 = \frac{0.9900 + 0.8521 + 0.6126 + 0.4449}{4} = \frac{2.8996}{4} = 0.7249$.
4. The sample variance is $s^2 = \frac{1}{3} \sum (f_i - 0.7249)^2 = \frac{(0.2651)^2 + (0.1272)^2 + (-0.1123)^2 + (-0.2800)^2}{3} = \frac{0.1775}{3} = 0.0592$, so $s = 0.2433$ and $\widehat{\operatorname{SE}} = \frac{0.2433}{\sqrt{4}} = 0.1216$.
5. With 4 points the error bar is large, as it should be. With $n = 100{,}000$ the true spread of $f(U)$ is $\sigma = 0.2010$, so $\operatorname{SE} = \frac{0.2010}{\sqrt{100{,}000}} = 0.00064$.

**Answer.** $\theta \approx 0.7468$. The code gives $0.74640 \pm 0.00064$, and the exact value, written with the error function, is $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \operatorname{erf}(1) = 0.74682$.

**Check.** The exact value is $0.0004$ above the estimate, which is less than one standard error ($0.00064$), so the answer is fully consistent. The 4-point estimate $0.7249$ is $0.022$ away from the exact value, less than one standard error of $0.12$.

### Example 2: Estimating Pi and Planning the Sample Size

**Problem.** Throw a random point $(X, Y)$ into the unit square. It falls inside the quarter circle $X^2 + Y^2 \le 1$ with probability $\frac{\pi}{4}$ (area, [3.8](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.8-Geometric-Probability.md)). Use this to estimate $\pi$. How many points give an error of at most $0.001$ with 95% confidence?

**Solution.**

1. Let $f = 4 \cdot I(X^2 + Y^2 \le 1)$. Then $E[f] = 4 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \pi$.
2. $f$ is $4$ with probability $p = \frac{\pi}{4}$ and $0$ otherwise, so its variance is $\sigma^2 = 16 \thinspace p (1 - p) = 16 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} \left( 1 - \frac{\pi}{4} \right) = \pi (4 - \pi) = 2.697$. Hence $\sigma = 1.642$.
3. The standard error is $\frac{1.642}{\sqrt{n}}$. For $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ this is $0.00164$.
4. For an error of at most $0.001$ with 95% confidence:

$$
n \ge \left( \frac{1.96 \times 1.642}{0.001} \right)^2 = (3218.3)^2 \approx 10{,}357{,}000
$$

**Answer.** About 10.4 million points.

**Check.** Ten million points for three correct digits shows why Monte Carlo is a poor way to get digits of $\pi$, but a good way to handle problems with no formula. The code below shows the standard errors $0.147$, $0.0164$ and $0.0016$ for $n = 100$, $10{,}000$ and $1{,}000{,}000$, each 10 times smaller for 100 times more points.

### Example 3: Why Rare Events Are Expensive

**Problem.** A server fails in a given hour with probability $p = 10^{-4}$. You want to estimate $p$ by simulation with a relative error of 10%, meaning $\frac{\operatorname{SE}}{p} \le 0.1$. How many trials do you need?

**Solution.**

1. For a probability, $\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$, so the relative error is $\frac{\operatorname{SE}}{p} = \sqrt{\frac{1-p}{np}}$.
2. Set it equal to $0.1$ and solve for $n$:

$$
\frac{1 - p}{np} = 0.01 \quad \implies \quad n = \frac{1 - p}{0.01 \thinspace p} = \frac{0.9999}{0.01 \times 0.0001} \approx 1{,}000{,}000
$$

**Answer.** About one million trials, and only about $np = 100$ of them are failures.

**Check.** You need around 100 successes to get 10% accuracy, whatever $p$ is. For $p = 10^{-9}$ that would mean $10^{11}$ trials, which is too slow. This is the problem that importance sampling solves ([19.6](19.6-Importance-Sampling.md)).

## Simulate It

This code estimates the integral of Example 1 and reports the estimate, the standard error and the 95% interval next to the exact value.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 100_000

# The integral of exp(-x^2) over [0, 1] equals E[f(U)], with U ~ Unif(0, 1).
u = rng.random(n_samples)
f = np.exp(-u**2)

estimate = f.mean()
std_error = f.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples)
exact = math.sqrt(math.pi) / 2 * math.erf(1)

print(f"Estimate:      {estimate:.5f}")
print(f"Std error:     {std_error:.5f}")
print(f"95% interval:  ({estimate - 1.96 * std_error:.5f}, {estimate + 1.96 * std_error:.5f})")
print(f"Exact:         {exact:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Estimate:      0.74640
Std error:     0.00064
95% interval:  (0.74515, 0.74764)
Exact:         0.74682
```

The exact value is inside the interval. But one interval does not prove that the error bar is honest. A better test is to repeat the whole experiment 5,000 times with $n = 1{,}000$ samples each, and see how often the interval contains the exact value (it should be about 95% of the time) and whether the reported standard error matches the real spread of the estimates.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples, n_experiments = 1_000, 5_000
exact = math.sqrt(math.pi) / 2 * math.erf(1)

# Repeat the whole Monte Carlo experiment many times.
u = rng.random((n_experiments, n_samples))
f = np.exp(-u**2)
estimates = f.mean(axis=1)
std_errors = f.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples)

covered = np.abs(estimates - exact) <= 1.96 * std_errors
print(f"Coverage of the 95% interval: {covered.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Real spread of the estimates: {estimates.std():.5f}")
print(f"Average reported SE:          {std_errors.mean():.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Coverage of the 95% interval: 0.9490
Real spread of the estimates: 0.00631
Average reported SE:          0.00636
```

The interval covers the truth 94.9% of the time, and the reported error bar ($0.00636$) matches the real spread ($0.00631$). Last, the $\pi$ estimates of Example 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for n in [100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    x, y = rng.random(n), rng.random(n)
    f = 4.0 * (x**2 + y**2 <= 1)            # E[f] = pi
    print(f"n = {n:>9,}: estimate {f.mean():.4f}  SE {f.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n):.4f}")
print(f"exact pi = {np.pi:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =       100: estimate 3.3600  SE 0.1474
n =    10,000: estimate 3.1468  SE 0.0164
n = 1,000,000: estimate 3.1425  SE 0.0016
exact pi = 3.1416
```

Each estimate is within about one standard error of $\pi$ ($3.3600$ is $1.5$ standard errors away, which happens by chance in one run out of seven).

## Common Mistakes

- **Reporting the standard deviation $s$ instead of the standard error $\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$.** The spread of the samples is not the error of the average. The error is $\sqrt{n}$ times smaller.
- **Forgetting that error shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, not $\frac{1}{n}$.** Doubling $n$ gives only 29% less error. To halve the error, use four times more samples.
- **Using the standard error formula for dependent samples.** The formula needs independent samples. For MCMC samples ([19.7](19.7-MCMC/README.md)) neighbours are correlated, and the true error is larger.
- **Trusting the formula when the variance is infinite.** If $\operatorname{Var}(f(X)) = \infty$ (for example $f(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{u}}$ on $(0, 1)$, whose mean is 2), the average still tends to the mean, but the CLT error bar is not valid and the estimate is erratic. Look at the largest sample values before you trust a Monte Carlo number ([17.9](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).
- **Estimating a tiny probability with a small $n$.** If you see 0 or 1 successes, the estimate $\hat{p} = 0$ is not a useful answer. You need about 100 successes (Example 3).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the loss on a minibatch is a Monte Carlo estimate of the expected loss, and its noise is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\text{batch size}}}$. Policy gradient methods estimate an expected reward from sampled episodes.
- **Computer science:** estimating the size of a huge set (how many solutions does a puzzle have?) by sampling, and checking the quality of a hash function.
- **Finance:** pricing a derivative is $E[\text{payoff}]$ under a model, estimated from simulated price paths ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Physics and graphics:** ray tracing averages light paths for each pixel; the noise in a picture is the Monte Carlo error, and it falls like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ with the number of rays per pixel.
- **Everyday life:** a poll is a Monte Carlo estimate: the margin of error of plus or minus 3% comes from the same formula.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You average $n = 400$ values of $f(X_i)$ and get $\hat{\theta} = 0.8$ with sample standard deviation $s = 2$. Give the standard error and a 95% interval.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\widehat{\operatorname{SE}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2}{20} = 0.1$$

$$0.8 \pm 1.96 \times 0.1 = 0.8 \pm 0.196, \text{ that is, } (0.604, \thinspace 0.996)$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A simulation has a standard error of $0.02$ with $n = 1{,}000$ samples. How many samples do you need for a standard error of $0.005$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The error must shrink by a factor of $\frac{0.02}{0.005} = 4$. Since $\operatorname{SE} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, the sample size must grow by $4^2 = 16$: $n = 16{,}000$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You estimate $E[X^2]$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ from $n = 10{,}000$ samples. What is the exact value, what is the standard error, and what does the simulation give with `default_rng(42)`?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[X^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + E[X]^2 = 1$. For $f(X) = X^2$ the variance is $E[X^4] - (E[X^2])^2 = 3 - 1 = 2$ (the fourth moment of the standard normal is 3, [6.7](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)). So

$$\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{10{,}000}} = 0.01414$$

The code below gives the estimate $1.0127$ with estimated standard error $0.0145$, within one standard error of the exact value $1$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
f = rng.standard_normal(10_000) ** 2
print(f"Estimate:  {f.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Std error: {f.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(f)):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Estimate:  1.0127
Std error: 0.0145
```

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A function $f(X)$ has standard deviation $\sigma = 0.5$. How many samples give a 95% error of at most $0.001$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$n \ge \left( \frac{1.96 \times 0.5}{0.001} \right)^2 = 980^2 = 960{,}400$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $f(X) \in [0, 1]$ (so $b - a = 1$). Use Hoeffding's inequality to find the number of samples that guarantees $P(\lvert \hat{\theta}_n - \theta \rvert \ge 0.01) \le 0.05$. Compare it with the CLT recipe in the worst case $\sigma = 0.5$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Hoeffding says $P(\lvert \hat{\theta}_n - \theta \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le 2 e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$. We need $2 e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} \le \delta$ with $\delta = 0.05$ and $\varepsilon = 0.01$. Take logarithms and solve for $n$:

$$n \ge \frac{\ln(2 / \delta)}{2 \varepsilon^2} = \frac{\ln 40}{2 \times 0.0001} = \frac{3.689}{0.0002} \approx 18{,}445$$

The CLT recipe with $\sigma = 0.5$ gives

$$n \ge \left( \frac{1.96 \times 0.5}{0.01} \right)^2 = 98^2 = 9{,}604$$

Hoeffding needs about twice as many samples, but it is a guarantee for every $n$, not an approximation.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate $\int_0^{\pi} \sin x \thinspace dx = 2$ with $n = 100{,}000$ samples. Use $\theta = E[\pi \sin(\pi U)]$ with $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, and report the standard error.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 100_000
u = rng.random(n_samples)
f = np.pi * np.sin(np.pi * u)          # (b - a) * g(a + (b - a) U) with a = 0, b = pi
print(f"Estimate:  {f.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Std error: {f.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples):.4f}")
print("Exact:     2.0000")
```

Output:

```text
Estimate:  2.0013
Std error: 0.0031
Exact:     2.0000
```

The exact value is within half a standard error of the estimate.

</details>

## Summary

- To estimate $E[f(X)]$, average $f$ over i.i.d. samples. Probabilities and integrals are special cases.
- The estimator is unbiased, consistent (Strong Law), and its standard error is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, estimated by $\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$.
- A 95% interval is $\hat{\theta}_n \pm 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$ (Central Limit Theorem). The sample size for error $\varepsilon$ is $n \ge \left( \frac{1.96 \sigma}{\varepsilon} \right)^2$.
- The rate $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ does not depend on the dimension. Rare events need about $\frac{100}{p}$ trials.
- The error bar needs independent samples and a finite variance. Check it by repeating the experiment with new seeds.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\hat{\theta}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i), \qquad \operatorname{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}
$$
