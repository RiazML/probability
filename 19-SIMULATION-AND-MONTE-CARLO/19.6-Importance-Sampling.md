# 19.6 Importance Sampling

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 19: Simulation and Monte Carlo](README.md) / 19.6 Importance Sampling

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [19.5 Rejection Sampling](19.5-Rejection-Sampling.md)

> **Big idea:** To estimate $E_p[f(X)]$, sample from a different density $q$ that puts more samples where $f(X)$ matters, and correct each sample by the weight $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$; a good choice of $q$ can cut the error by a large factor, especially for rare events.

---

## Why It Matters

In [19.3](19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md) we saw that a rare event with probability $10^{-4}$ needs about a million trials, and one with probability $10^{-9}$ is out of reach. Yet rare events are often the ones we care about: a server crash, a bank loss, a plane failure. Importance sampling spends the samples where the rare event actually happens and fixes the bias with weights. The same weights appear in machine learning: off-policy reinforcement learning, variational inference, and the correction for training on data from a different distribution than the test data.

## Intuition

Suppose you want to know what fraction of a country's wealth is held by billionaires. If you pick people uniformly at random, almost nobody you meet is a billionaire, and your estimate is very noisy. A better plan: ask many more billionaires than their share of the population, and then **weight** each answer by how rare that type of person is. A billionaire who was 1,000 times over-sampled counts $\frac{1}{1000}$ as much as an ordinary person. You get a precise answer from far fewer interviews.

That is importance sampling. We choose a sampling plan $q$ that is not the true distribution $p$, we over-sample the important region, and each sample carries a weight $\frac{p}{q}$ that undoes the over-sampling. The average of weighted values is still correct on average, but its variance can be far smaller. Choose badly, though (an unimportant region, or a $q$ with thin tails), and the variance gets worse, even infinite.

## Picture

```text
  density
  |                pppp                 qqqq
  |               ppppppp             qqqqqqqq
  |              ppppppppp           qqqqqqqqqq
  |            pppppppppppp         qqqqqqqqqqqq
  |           ppppppppppppppp      qqqqqqqqqqqqqq
  |         pppppppppppppppppp   qqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqq
  |       pppppppppppppppppppp###qqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqqq
  +------------------------------------------------------------
                     0                   4
```

The target $p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ almost never produces a value above 4. The proposal $q = \mathcal{N}(4, 1)$ puts half of its samples there. (The letters show which density is higher; `#` is where they overlap.)

## Definition

Let $X$ have density $p$ (the **target**) and let $f$ be a function. We want

$$
\theta = E_p[f(X)] = \int f(x) \thinspace p(x) \thinspace dx
$$

Let $q$ be another density (the **proposal**) with $q(x) \gt 0$ whenever $f(x) p(x) \ne 0$. The **importance weight** is

$$
w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
$$

The **importance sampling estimator** draws $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} q$ and averages the weighted values:

$$
\hat{\theta}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i) \thinspace w(X_i)
$$

**In words:** sample from $q$, multiply each value of $f$ by the ratio target density over proposal density, and average.

Its standard error is estimated as in [19.3](19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md), from the sample standard deviation $s$ of the numbers $f(X_i) w(X_i)$:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

**Self-normalized version.** If $p$ is known only up to a constant ($\tilde{p} = Zp$), use the weights $\tilde{w} = \frac{\tilde{p}}{q}$ and divide by their sum:

$$
\hat{\theta}_n^{\text{SN}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i) \thinspace \tilde{w}(X_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{w}(X_i)}
$$

It is slightly biased for finite $n$ but consistent, and the unknown $Z$ cancels. To see how many of the $n$ samples are really useful, use the **effective sample size**:

$$
\text{ESS} = \frac{\left( \sum_i w_i \right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}
$$

**In words:** if all weights are equal, ESS equals $n$; if one weight dominates, ESS is close to 1.

## Why It Is True

**The estimator is unbiased.** Change the integral from $p$ to $q$ (the weight cancels the change):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_q[f(X) w(X)] &= \int f(x) \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} q(x) \thinspace dx && \text{(LOTUS under } q \text{)} \\
&= \int f(x) \thinspace p(x) \thinspace dx = E_p[f(X)] && \text{(cancel } q \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This needs $q(x) \gt 0$ wherever $f(x)p(x) \ne 0$: if $q$ never visits a region that matters, the integral is missing that part.

**The variance.** The estimator is an average of $n$ i.i.d. values $f(X) w(X)$, so $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \frac{1}{n} \operatorname{Var}_q(f w)$ with

$$
\operatorname{Var}_q(f w) = \int \frac{f(x)^2 p(x)^2}{q(x)} \thinspace dx - \theta^2
$$

Plain Monte Carlo is the special case $q = p$ and $w = 1$. Importance sampling wins when $q$ is large where $\lvert f \rvert p$ is large, because then $\frac{f^2 p^2}{q}$ is not large anywhere. It loses badly when $q$ is small where $f p$ is not small.

**The best proposal.** By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality ([16.4](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.4-Cauchy-Schwarz-Inequality.md)):

$$
\left( \int \lvert f \rvert p \thinspace dx \right)^2 = \left( \int \frac{\lvert f \rvert p}{\sqrt{q}} \sqrt{q} \thinspace dx \right)^2 \le \int \frac{f^2 p^2}{q} \thinspace dx \cdot \int q \thinspace dx = \int \frac{f^2 p^2}{q} \thinspace dx
$$

The left side does not depend on $q$, so no $q$ can beat it, and equality holds when $q \propto \lvert f \rvert p$. For $f \ge 0$ the choice $q^{\ast}(x) = \frac{f(x) p(x)}{\theta}$ makes $f w = \theta$ constant: zero variance. We cannot use it in practice (it contains the unknown $\theta$), but it tells us to make $q$ look like $f p$.

**Thin tails are dangerous.** If $q$ has lighter tails than $p$, the weight $\frac{p}{q}$ becomes huge in the tails. Example 3 shows that the variance can then be infinite and a few samples dominate.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Beating Plain Monte Carlo on a Simple Integral

**Problem.** Estimate $\theta = E[X^2]$ for $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. The exact value is $\frac{1}{3}$. Compare plain Monte Carlo with importance sampling from $q(x) = 2x$ on $[0, 1]$, which favours large $x$ where $x^2$ is large.

**Solution.**

1. Plain Monte Carlo: $f(X) = X^2$ has $E[X^4] = \frac{1}{5}$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X^2) = \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{4}{45} = 0.0889$, and the standard deviation is $0.2981$.
2. Importance sampling: the weight is $w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{1}{2x}$, so $f(x) w(x) = \frac{x^2}{2x} = \frac{x}{2}$.
3. To sample from $q$, use inverse transform ([19.4](19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md)): $F_q(x) = x^2$, so $X = \sqrt{U}$. By hand, $U = 0.25, 0.64, 0.81$ give $X = 0.5, 0.8, 0.9$, and $f w = \frac{x}{2} = 0.25, 0.40, 0.45$, with average $0.3667$.
4. The variance of $f w$ under $q$ is $E_q\big[\frac{X^2}{4}\big] - \frac{1}{9} = \int_0^1 \frac{x^2}{4} \cdot 2x \thinspace dx - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{1}{8} - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{1}{72} = 0.0139$, with standard deviation $0.1179$.

**Answer.** The variance per sample falls from $0.0889$ to $0.0139$, a factor of $6.4$. The standard error falls by $\sqrt{6.4} = 2.5$.

**Check.** The best proposal here is $q^{\ast}(x) \propto x^2$, that is $3x^2$, which makes $f w = \frac{1}{3}$ for every sample: zero variance. Our $q = 2x$ is between the plain $q = 1$ and the best $3x^2$, and its variance lies between $0.0889$ and $0$, as it should. The first code block shows the standard errors $0.00094$ and $0.00037$ for $n = 100{,}000$.

### Example 2: A Rare Event Beyond Four Standard Deviations

**Problem.** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Estimate $P(Z \gt 4) = 3.167 \times 10^{-5}$ with $n = 100{,}000$ samples, by plain Monte Carlo and by importance sampling with $q = \mathcal{N}(4, 1)$.

**Solution.**

1. Plain Monte Carlo: the expected number of hits is $np = 100{,}000 \times 3.167 \times 10^{-5} = 3.2$. The relative error is $\sqrt{\frac{1 - p}{np}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{3.2}} = 0.56$, so the answer is wrong by more than half.
2. Importance sampling: draw $X = 4 + Z'$ with $Z' \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The weight is the ratio of the two normal densities:

$$
w(x) = \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(x-4)^2/2}} = e^{-x^2/2 + (x-4)^2/2} = e^{8 - 4x}
$$

3. The value of each sample is $I(X \gt 4) \cdot e^{8 - 4X}$. About half of the $X$ are above 4, and for those $e^{8-4X} \le e^{-8} = 0.000335$ is small, so the sum is a mean of many small numbers instead of a few lucky hits.

**Answer.** $P(Z \gt 4) \approx 3.17 \times 10^{-5}$ with a relative error of well under 1%.

**Check.** The second code block shows plain Monte Carlo giving $9.0 \times 10^{-5}$ from only 9 hits (expected 3.2, an unlucky run, and nearly three times the truth), while importance sampling gives $3.173 \times 10^{-5}$ with standard error $2.1 \times 10^{-7}$, a relative error of $0.0067$. The exact value is $3.167 \times 10^{-5}$.

### Example 3: When the Proposal Has Thin Tails

**Problem.** The target is $p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $f(x) = x^2$, so $\theta = 1$. Use proposals $q = \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$ with $s = 0.5$ (narrower than $p$) and $s = 2$ (wider). Which one works?

**Solution.**

1. The weight is $w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$. The second moment of the weight under $q$ is

$$
E_q[w^2] = \int \frac{p(x)^2}{q(x)} dx = \frac{s}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int e^{-x^2 \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2s^2} \right)} dx
$$

2. This integral is finite only if $1 - \frac{1}{2s^2} \gt 0$, that is, $s^2 \gt \frac{1}{2}$. For $s = 0.5$ we have $s^2 = 0.25 \lt 0.5$, so $E_q[w^2] = \infty$: the estimator has infinite variance. For $s = 2$ it is finite.
3. In practice with $s = 0.5$, a rare sample far out in the tail gets an enormous weight and moves the whole average.

**Answer.** The wide proposal $s = 2$ works. The narrow proposal $s = 0.5$ has infinite variance and gives unstable answers.

**Check.** The third code block repeats the estimate 5 times with 10,000 samples. With $s = 0.5$ the five estimates are $1.122, 0.823, 0.682, 1.243, 4.731$ (the last one is wild), and the effective sample size is only about 621 of 10,000. With $s = 2$ the estimates are all within $0.01$ of $1$, and the ESS is about 6,600.

## Simulate It

First, Example 1:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 100_000

# Goal: theta = E[X^2] = 1/3 for X ~ Unif(0, 1).
# Plain Monte Carlo: sample from p = Unif(0, 1).
x = rng.random(n_samples)
plain = x**2

# Importance sampling: sample from q(x) = 2x (inverse transform: X = sqrt(U)),
# then weight by w(x) = p(x) / q(x) = 1 / (2x).
y = np.sqrt(rng.random(n_samples))
weighted = y**2 / (2 * y)

for name, v in [("plain", plain), ("importance", weighted)]:
    print(f"{name:<10} estimate {v.mean():.5f}  SE {v.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples):.5f}")
print(f"exact      estimate {1 / 3:.5f}")
print(f"sd per sample: plain {np.sqrt(4 / 45):.4f}, importance {np.sqrt(1 / 72):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
plain      estimate 0.33387  SE 0.00094
importance estimate 0.33310  SE 0.00037
exact      estimate 0.33333
sd per sample: plain 0.2981, importance 0.1179
```

Both estimates are within one standard error of $\frac{1}{3}$, and importance sampling has a $2.5$ times smaller error. Now the rare event of Example 2:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 100_000
exact = norm.sf(4)                               # P(Z > 4)

# Plain Monte Carlo: almost no sample falls beyond 4.
z = rng.standard_normal(n_samples)
hit = z > 4
se_plain = hit.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples)
print(f"plain      estimate {hit.mean():.3e}  hits {hit.sum()}  SE {se_plain:.2e}")

# Importance sampling: sample from q = N(4, 1), weight w(x) = exp(8 - 4x).
x = rng.standard_normal(n_samples) + 4
v = (x > 4) * np.exp(8 - 4 * x)
se_is = v.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples)
print(f"importance estimate {v.mean():.3e}  SE {se_is:.2e}")
print(f"exact      {exact:.3e}")
print(f"relative error: plain {se_plain / exact:.3f}, importance {se_is / exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
plain      estimate 9.000e-05  hits 9  SE 3.00e-05
importance estimate 3.173e-05  SE 2.13e-07
exact      3.167e-05
relative error: plain 0.947, importance 0.0067
```

Finally, thin and wide proposals (Example 3), with the effective sample size:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 10_000

# Target p = N(0, 1), f(x) = x^2, so theta = 1. Compare two proposals N(0, s^2).
for s in [0.5, 2.0]:
    estimates, ess = [], []
    for _ in range(5):
        x = rng.normal(0, s, n_samples)
        w = norm.pdf(x) / norm.pdf(x, scale=s)
        estimates.append(np.mean(w * x**2))
        ess.append(w.sum() ** 2 / (w**2).sum())
    print(f"s = {s}: estimates {np.round(estimates, 3)}")
    print(f"        mean ESS {np.mean(ess):,.0f} of {n_samples:,}")
```

Output:

```text
s = 0.5: estimates [1.122 0.823 0.682 1.243 4.731]
        mean ESS 621 of 10,000
s = 2.0: estimates [0.99  1.002 0.998 0.994 0.997]
        mean ESS 6,603 of 10,000
```

Always look at the weights (or the ESS) before you trust an importance sampling estimate: a small ESS is a warning.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the weights.** Sampling from $q$ and averaging $f(X)$ estimates $E_q[f]$, not $E_p[f]$. The weight $\frac{p}{q}$ is what moves the answer back.
- **Using the weight the wrong way round.** The weight is target over proposal, $\frac{p}{q}$. Writing $\frac{q}{p}$ gives a wrong answer that may still look reasonable.
- **A proposal with thinner tails than the target, or with zero density where the target has mass.** The variance can be infinite, or part of the integral is never seen. Make $q$ at least as spread out as $p$ where $f p$ matters.
- **Trusting the standard error when the weights are dominated by a few samples.** Check the ESS and the largest weight. If one weight is 50% of the total, the estimate is really based on one sample.
- **Believing that importance sampling always helps.** With a poor $q$ it is worse than plain Monte Carlo. It helps only when $q$ is close to $\lvert f \rvert p$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** off-policy reinforcement learning corrects data collected by an old policy with the weight $\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}}{\pi_{\text{old}}}$. Variational methods and importance weighted autoencoders use the same weights, and covariate shift correction re-weights training data to look like the test data.
- **Computer science:** estimating the probability of a rare failure in a network or a chip, rendering light paths in graphics (sample where the light is strong), and particle filters.
- **Finance and insurance:** the chance of a huge loss, and pricing options that pay only in extreme markets.
- **Physics and engineering:** the chance that a particle passes through thick shielding, and the failure probability of a structure.
- **Everyday life:** surveys that over-sample a small group and weight it down to its true share of the population.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In Example 1, what is the weight and the value $f(x) w(x)$ for the sample $x = 0.5$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$w(0.5) = \frac{1}{2 \times 0.5} = 1, \qquad f(0.5) \thinspace w(0.5) = 0.25 \times 1 = 0.25$$

At $x = 0.5$ the target and proposal densities are equal ($p = 1$, $q = 2 \times 0.5 = 1$), so the weight is 1.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You want $E[X]$ for $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (mean 1), and you sample from $q = \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Why does this fail?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The proposal $q$ is zero for $x \gt 1$, but the target has mass there: $P(X \gt 1) = e^{-1} = 0.368$. The condition that $q(x) \gt 0$ wherever $f p \ne 0$ is violated. No weight can recover the part of the integral over $x \gt 1$, so the estimate is biased, whatever $n$ is.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** To estimate $P(Z \gt 3)$ you sample from $q = \mathcal{N}(3, 1)$. What is the weight of the sample $x = 3.5$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$w(x) = e^{-x^2/2 + (x - 3)^2/2} = e^{4.5 - 3x}, \qquad w(3.5) = e^{4.5 - 10.5} = e^{-6} \approx 0.00248$$

A sample at $3.5$ is common under $q$ and rare under $p$, so its weight is small.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Five weights are $4, 1, 1, 1, 1$. Compute the effective sample size.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{ESS} = \frac{(4 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1)^2}{16 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1} = \frac{64}{20} = 3.2$$

The five samples are worth only 3.2 equal samples, because the first one counts four times as much as the others.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $q = \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$. Show that $E_q[w^2]$ is finite if and only if $s^2 \gt \frac{1}{2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write the densities: $p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$ and $q(x) = \frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/(2s^2)}$. Then

$$E_q[w^2] = \int \frac{p(x)^2}{q(x)} dx = \frac{s}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int \exp\left( -x^2 + \frac{x^2}{2s^2} \right) dx = \frac{s}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int \exp\left( -x^2 \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2s^2} \right) \right) dx$$

This is a Gaussian integral. It converges exactly when the coefficient $1 - \frac{1}{2s^2}$ is positive, that is $s^2 \gt \frac{1}{2}$. If the coefficient is zero or negative, the integrand does not decay and the integral is infinite. Then the variance of the estimator is infinite.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate $P(Z \gt 3) = 0.00135$ with $n = 100{,}000$ samples, by plain Monte Carlo and by importance sampling from $\mathcal{N}(3, 1)$, and compare the standard errors.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 100_000

plain = (rng.standard_normal(n_samples) > 3).astype(float)
x = rng.standard_normal(n_samples) + 3                  # proposal q = N(3, 1)
weighted = (x > 3) * np.exp(4.5 - 3 * x)                # weight p(x) / q(x)

for name, v in [("plain", plain), ("importance", weighted)]:
    print(f"{name:<10} {v.mean():.6f}  SE {v.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_samples):.6f}")
print(f"exact      {norm.sf(3):.6f}")
```

Output:

```text
plain      0.001390  SE 0.000118
importance 0.001353  SE 0.000008
exact      0.001350
```

Importance sampling has a standard error about 15 times smaller, which is worth about $15^2 = 225$ times more plain samples.

</details>

## Summary

- Importance sampling estimates $E_p[f(X)]$ by drawing $X \sim q$ and averaging $f(X) \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}$. It is unbiased if $q \gt 0$ wherever $f p \ne 0$.
- Its variance is $\frac{1}{n}\left( \int \frac{f^2 p^2}{q} - \theta^2 \right)$. The best proposal is proportional to $\lvert f \rvert p$.
- It is the tool for rare events: put the samples where the event happens, and weight them down.
- A proposal with thinner tails than the target can give infinite variance. Check the weights and the effective sample size.
- The self-normalized form works when $p$ is known only up to a constant.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E_p[f(X)] = E_q\left[ f(X) \frac{p(X)}{q(X)} \right]}
$$
