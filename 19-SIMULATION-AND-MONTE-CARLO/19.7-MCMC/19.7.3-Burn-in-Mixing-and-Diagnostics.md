# 19.7.3 Burn-in, Mixing and Diagnostics

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 19: Simulation and Monte Carlo](../README.md) / [19.7 MCMC](README.md) / 19.7.3 Burn-in, Mixing and Diagnostics

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [19.7.2 Gibbs Sampling](19.7.2-Gibbs-Sampling.md)

> **Big idea:** An MCMC run is only trustworthy if you throw away the start-up part (burn-in), measure how correlated the samples are (the effective sample size gives the true error bar), and check that several chains from different starts agree (R-hat).

---

## Why It Matters

MCMC gives samples that are correlated and that begin at an arbitrary point. A chain can look perfectly fine and still give a wrong answer: it may not have reached the target yet, or it may be stuck in one peak of a two-peak target. This topic gives the tools that tell you how many samples you really have and whether to trust them. They are the difference between "the code ran" and "the answer is right". It also makes concrete the speed of convergence of [18.3.8](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md).

## Intuition

Think of a hiker dropped by helicopter on a mountain range and told to visit places in proportion to their height. The first part of the walk is the hiker climbing out of a low, rare valley where the helicopter landed. Those first steps say nothing about the typical places, so we **discard** them. That is the **burn-in**.

After that, the hiker takes tiny steps, so each position is almost the same as the last. A thousand steps may carry the same information as ten independent visits. This is the **mixing** problem: a chain that moves slowly through the target is **slowly mixing**, and its samples are worth much less than independent samples.

Last, imagine two hikers dropped on two sides of a tall ridge. Each wanders happily on its own side, and each thinks the world is its own side. Only by comparing the two hikers do we find out that something is wrong. This is why we run several chains from different starts and compare them.

## Definition

**Burn-in.** The first $b$ samples of the chain are discarded, because the chain started at an arbitrary point and has not yet reached the target distribution. The estimate uses only $x_{b+1}, \dots, x_{n}$.

**Autocorrelation.** For a stationary chain with variance $\sigma^2$, the **autocorrelation at lag $k$** is

$$
\rho_k = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k})}{\sigma^2}
$$

**In words:** how strongly a sample is linked to the sample $k$ steps later. For independent samples $\rho_k = 0$ for all $k \ge 1$.

**Integrated autocorrelation time and effective sample size.**

$$
\tau = 1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_k, \qquad \text{ESS} = \frac{n}{\tau}
$$

where $n$ is the number of samples used. The **effective sample size** (ESS) is the number of independent samples that would give the same accuracy. The standard error of the average $\bar{x}_n$ of a function of the chain is

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\bar{x}_n) \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\text{ESS}}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \sqrt{\tau}
$$

**In words:** a chain with autocorrelation time $\tau$ needs $\tau$ times more steps than independent sampling for the same error.

**R-hat (Gelman-Rubin diagnostic).** Run $m$ chains of length $n$ from different starts. Let $\bar{x}_j$ and $s_j^2$ be the mean and variance of chain $j$, and $\bar{x}$ the mean of all chain means. Compute

$$
W = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} s_j^2, \qquad B = \frac{n}{m - 1} \sum_{j=1}^{m} (\bar{x}_j - \bar{x})^2, \qquad \hat{R} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{n - 1}{n} W + \frac{1}{n} B}{W}}
$$

$W$ is the **within-chain** variance and $B$ is the **between-chain** variance (times $n$). **In words:** if all chains explore the same distribution, the between-chain spread is small compared with the within-chain spread and $\hat{R} \approx 1$. A common rule is to want $\hat{R} \lt 1.01$ (older papers use $1.1$).

## Why It Is True

**Why the correlation changes the error.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be stationary with variance $\sigma^2$ and autocorrelations $\rho_k$. The variance of a sum is the sum of all covariances ([11.1](../../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/README.md)):

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left( \sum_{t=1}^{n} X_t \right) = \sum_{s=1}^{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} \operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \sigma^2 \sum_{s, t} \rho_{\lvert s - t \rvert}
$$

There are $n$ pairs with $s = t$ and $2(n - k)$ pairs with lag $k \ge 1$. Dividing by $n^2$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left( 1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \left( 1 - \frac{k}{n} \right) \rho_k \right) \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \tau
$$

for large $n$ when the autocorrelations die out. So the variance is $\tau$ times larger than for independent samples. This is the Central Limit Theorem for a Markov chain, with a bigger variance.

**The autoregressive example.** If $\rho_k = r^k$ (as in the Gibbs sampler of [19.7.2](19.7.2-Gibbs-Sampling.md), where $r = \rho^2$), then $\sum_{k \ge 1} r^k = \frac{r}{1 - r}$ and

$$
\tau = 1 + \frac{2r}{1 - r} = \frac{1 + r}{1 - r}, \qquad \text{ESS} = n \frac{1 - r}{1 + r}
$$

For $r = 0.9$ we get $\tau = 19$: nineteen steps are worth one independent sample.

**Why burn-in matters.** The law of $X_t$ tends to the target only as $t \to \infty$, at a speed set by the second eigenvalue of the chain ([18.3.8](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md)). The early samples have the wrong law, so including them adds a bias that shrinks only like $\frac{1}{n}$ (Example 1). Discarding too few is the danger; discarding too many costs only a little.

**Why R-hat works.** In equilibrium the between-chain and within-chain spreads estimate the same variance, so $\frac{n-1}{n} W + \frac{B}{n}$ and $W$ are about equal. If the chains sit in different places, $B$ is large, the numerator is larger than $W$, and $\hat{R} \gt 1$. Note that $\hat{R} \approx 1$ shows that the chains agree. It does not prove they found all the peaks: if every chain misses the same distant peak, $\hat{R}$ cannot tell.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Cost of a Bad Start

**Problem.** Random-walk Metropolis with step $1$ targets $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, but the chain starts at $x_0 = 50$. After how many steps does it first come inside $[-3, 3]$, and what happens to the estimates of the mean and the standard deviation if we do not discard the start?

**Solution.**

1. At $x = 50$ the target density is $e^{-1250}$, essentially 0. Any move toward 0 is accepted. A move away is accepted with tiny probability. So the chain slides toward 0, one step of size about 1 at a time, but some proposals are rejected.
2. The code below records when the chain first has $\lvert x \rvert \lt 3$.
3. Compare the mean and standard deviation of the chain with burn-in of 0, 100 and 500 steps.

**Answer.** The chain first enters $[-3, 3]$ at step $120$. With no burn-in the mean is $0.738$ and the standard deviation is $4.894$. With burn-in 100 they are $0.104$ and $1.156$. With burn-in 500 they are $0.076$ and $1.037$ (exact values $0$ and $1$).

**Check.** The first values of the chain are $50, 50, 48.05, 48.05, 48.03, \dots$ and at steps 100 to 104 they are still $11.37, 11.37, 11.37, 9.32, 8.47$: far from the target region. A few values near 50 dominate the standard deviation $4.894$. Burn-in 100 is slightly too short (the sd $1.156$ is a bit high), and 500 is enough. The leftover mean $0.076$ is Monte Carlo noise from correlated samples, of size about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{ESS}}}$.

### Example 2: Autocorrelation Time and Step Size

**Problem.** (a) For an autoregressive chain with $r = 0.9$ and $n = 100{,}000$ steps, find $\tau$, the ESS and the standard error of the mean when $\sigma = 1$. (b) For random-walk Metropolis on $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, compare the step sizes $0.1$, $2.4$ and $20$.

**Solution.**

(a)

1. $\tau = \frac{1 + 0.9}{1 - 0.9} = \frac{1.9}{0.1} = 19$.
2. $\text{ESS} = \frac{100{,}000}{19} = 5{,}263$.
3. $\operatorname{SE} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5263}} = 0.0138$, against the naive $\frac{1}{\sqrt{100{,}000}} = 0.0032$. The naive error bar is $\sqrt{19} = 4.4$ times too small.

(b)

4. A tiny step ($0.1$) is accepted almost always but moves slowly, so $\tau$ is large. A huge step ($20$) is mostly rejected, so the chain stays put, and $\tau$ is also large. A step close to the width of the target works best.
5. The code below runs 50,000 steps for each step size and estimates $\tau$ by summing the autocorrelations up to the first negative one.

**Answer.** (a) $\tau = 19$, ESS $= 5{,}263$, $\operatorname{SE} = 0.0138$. (b) The table is in the code output: the step $2.4$ has acceptance $0.442$, $\tau \approx 4.6$ and ESS $\approx 10{,}800$ of $50{,}000$. The steps $0.1$ and $20$ give ESS about $130$ and $1{,}850$.

**Check.** The best step $2.4$ gives an acceptance rate $0.44$, which agrees with the known optimum for a one-dimensional random-walk Metropolis ($\approx 0.44$; in many dimensions the optimum is about $0.23$). The step $0.1$ has acceptance $0.969$, so a high acceptance rate alone is not a sign of a good chain: the lag-1 autocorrelation is $0.995$.

### Example 3: Two Peaks and R-hat

**Problem.** The target is $0.5 \thinspace \mathcal{N}(-4, 1) + 0.5 \thinspace \mathcal{N}(4, 1)$. Run four chains from the starts $-6, -2, 2, 6$ with random-walk step $0.5$ and step $4$. Compute $\hat{R}$, first by hand for a tiny case.

**Solution.**

1. By hand, take $m = 2$ chains of length $n = 4$: chain 1 is $(1, 3, 2, 2)$ and chain 2 is $(3, 5, 4, 4)$. The chain means are $2$ and $4$, the grand mean is $3$.
2. Each chain has variance $s^2 = \frac{(-1)^2 + 1^2 + 0 + 0}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$, so $W = \frac{2}{3}$.
3. $B = \frac{4}{1} \left( (2 - 3)^2 + (4 - 3)^2 \right) = 4 \times 2 = 8$.
4. $\frac{n-1}{n} W + \frac{B}{n} = \frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{3} + \frac{8}{4} = 0.5 + 2 = 2.5$, so $\hat{R} = \sqrt{\frac{2.5}{2/3}} = \sqrt{3.75} = 1.94$. Far above 1: the chains disagree.
5. For the real target, the code discards the first 1,000 steps and computes $\hat{R}$ for each step size.

**Answer.** With step $0.5$: $\hat{R} = 3.495$, and three chains sit near $-4$ while one sits near $+4$. With step $4$: $\hat{R} = 1.000$, and all four chains have mean near $0$ and spend about half of the time on each side.

**Check.** The exact mean of the target is $0$ and $P(X \gt 0) = 0.5$. With step $0.5$ the proposal almost never crosses the valley between the peaks (the density there is about $e^{-8}$ of the peak), so each chain reports the mean of one peak: wrong, and $\hat{R}$ says so. With step $4$ the chain jumps between peaks, and each chain gives $P(X \gt 0)$ between $0.49$ and $0.52$.

## Simulate It

First, the burn-in experiment of Example 1:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def metropolis_normal(x0, n_steps, step):
    """Random-walk Metropolis for the N(0, 1) target; returns the chain."""
    x, chain = x0, np.empty(n_steps)
    for t in range(n_steps):
        proposal = x + step * rng.standard_normal()
        if np.log(rng.random()) < (x**2 - proposal**2) / 2:   # log p(x') - log p(x)
            x = proposal
        chain[t] = x
    return chain

chain = metropolis_normal(x0=50.0, n_steps=5_000, step=1.0)
first_inside = np.argmax(np.abs(chain) < 3)                   # first step inside [-3, 3]
print(f"first time inside [-3, 3]: step {first_inside}")
for burn_in in [0, 100, 500]:
    print(f"burn-in {burn_in:>3}: mean {chain[burn_in:].mean():.3f}, sd {chain[burn_in:].std():.3f}  (exact 0 and 1)")
print("first 5 values:  ", np.round(chain[:5], 2))
print("values 100-104:  ", np.round(chain[100:105], 2))
```

Next, the autocorrelation time for three step sizes (Example 2):

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def metropolis_normal(x0, n_steps, step):
    x, chain, n_accept = x0, np.empty(n_steps), 0
    for t in range(n_steps):
        proposal = x + step * rng.standard_normal()
        if np.log(rng.random()) < (x**2 - proposal**2) / 2:
            x, n_accept = proposal, n_accept + 1
        chain[t] = x
    return chain, n_accept / n_steps

def autocorr(v, max_lag):
    v = v - v.mean()
    acf = np.correlate(v, v, mode="full")[len(v) - 1:] / (v @ v)
    return acf[: max_lag + 1]

def effective_sample_size(v, max_lag=1000):
    acf = autocorr(v, max_lag)
    # Add autocorrelations until the first negative one (a simple cutoff rule).
    cutoff = np.argmax(acf[1:] < 0) if np.any(acf[1:] < 0) else max_lag
    tau = 1 + 2 * acf[1 : cutoff + 1].sum()
    return len(v) / tau, tau

n_steps = 50_000
print("step   accept   lag-1 acf   tau    ESS     SE of the mean")
for step in [0.1, 2.4, 20.0]:
    chain, rate = metropolis_normal(0.0, n_steps, step)
    ess, tau = effective_sample_size(chain)
    print(f"{step:>5}  {rate:6.3f}   {autocorr(chain, 1)[1]:8.3f}  {tau:6.1f} {ess:7.0f}   {1 / np.sqrt(ess):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
step   accept   lag-1 acf   tau    ESS     SE of the mean
  0.1   0.969      0.995   378.7     132   0.0870
  2.4   0.442      0.622     4.6   10793   0.0096
 20.0   0.063      0.916    27.1    1845   0.0233
```

The error bar for the mean of the chain with step $2.4$ is $0.0096$, while the naive formula $\frac{1}{\sqrt{50{,}000}} = 0.0045$ would be about twice too small. (The estimate of $\tau$ for the step $0.1$ is rough, since the chain is too short to see its autocorrelation die out.) Last, the $\hat{R}$ for the two-peak target (Example 3):

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def log_target(x):
    # Two well-separated peaks: 0.5 N(-4, 1) + 0.5 N(4, 1), constants dropped.
    return np.log(np.exp(-(x + 4) ** 2 / 2) + np.exp(-(x - 4) ** 2 / 2))

def run_chains(starts, n_steps, step):
    x = np.array(starts, dtype=float)
    chains = np.empty((len(x), n_steps))
    for t in range(n_steps):
        proposal = x + step * rng.standard_normal(len(x))
        accept = np.log(rng.random(len(x))) < log_target(proposal) - log_target(x)
        x = np.where(accept, proposal, x)
        chains[:, t] = x
    return chains

def r_hat(chains):
    m, n = chains.shape
    W = chains.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean()                  # average variance inside chains
    B = n * chains.mean(axis=1).var(ddof=1)                # n times variance of chain means
    v_hat = (n - 1) / n * W + B / n
    return np.sqrt(v_hat / W)

for step in [0.5, 4.0]:
    chains = run_chains([-6, -2, 2, 6], 10_000, step)[:, 1_000:]   # discard burn-in
    print(f"step {step}: R-hat = {r_hat(chains):.3f}")
    print(f"   chain means        {np.round(chains.mean(axis=1), 2)}")
    print(f"   P(X > 0) per chain {np.round((chains > 0).mean(axis=1), 2)}")
```

Output:

```text
step 0.5: R-hat = 3.495
   chain means        [-3.98 -3.97 -3.76  4.02]
   P(X > 0) per chain [0.   0.   0.03 1.  ]
step 4.0: R-hat = 1.000
   chain means        [ 0.05 -0.03  0.12  0.17]
   P(X > 0) per chain [0.51 0.49 0.52 0.52]
```

With step $0.5$ the chains disagree and $\hat{R}$ is far above 1. (The third chain started on the right side, but it spent almost all of its time in the left peak.) With step $4$ they agree.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reporting the naive standard error of MCMC samples.** $\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$ ignores the correlation and can be several times too small. Use $\frac{s}{\sqrt{\text{ESS}}}$, batch means, or the spread of independent chains.
- **Judging a chain by its acceptance rate alone.** A rate near 1 can mean tiny steps and a useless chain (step $0.1$ above), and a rate near 0 means the chain does not move. Look at the autocorrelation and the ESS.
- **Running one chain and trusting the trace plot.** A single chain can sit happily in one peak forever. Run at least four chains from widely spread starts, and compare them with $\hat{R}$.
- **Believing that $\hat{R} \approx 1$ proves convergence.** It shows only that the chains agree. If all chains miss the same far-away region, no diagnostic using these chains can see it.
- **Thinning to "remove correlation".** Keeping every 10th sample does not add information, and you throw data away. It is useful only to save memory. The error bar must still be based on the ESS.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** every Bayesian model fitted by MCMC (in Stan, PyMC and similar tools) reports ESS and R-hat for each parameter. Practitioners reject a fit whose ESS is small or whose R-hat is above $1.01$. In energy-based models, the samples used for training must come from a chain that has mixed.
- **Computer science:** the number of steps a randomized sampling algorithm needs is its mixing time, which is studied in theory (for example for sampling graph colourings).
- **Statistics:** convergence diagnostics are a standard part of every Bayesian data analysis, and the effective sample size is also used for data with serial correlation (time series, [18.1](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)).
- **Physics:** simulations of lattices report autocorrelation times, and special algorithms (cluster updates) are designed to reduce them.
- **Everyday life:** 1,000 daily temperature readings in a row are worth far fewer than 1,000 independent readings, because each day is similar to the previous one.

**2. (Easy)** A chain on $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ starts at $x_0 = 50$. A student keeps all samples. Is the estimated mean too high, too low or unbiased, and why? Is discarding too many early samples harmful?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The estimate is biased upward (here to $0.738$ in the example), because the early samples come from the path from $50$ to the bulk and have the wrong distribution. Discarding too many early samples is almost harmless: it only wastes a little data, whereas discarding too few leaves a bias. So choose a burn-in that is clearly long enough, for example from the trace plot, or from where several chains meet.

**3. (Medium)** Two chains of length $n = 4$ are $(1, 3, 2, 2)$ and $(3, 5, 4, 4)$. Compute $W$, $B$ and $\hat{R}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each chain has variance $\frac{2}{3}$, so $W = \frac{2}{3}$. The chain means are $2$ and $4$, with grand mean $3$, so

$$B = \frac{4}{1} \left( (2 - 3)^2 + (4 - 3)^2 \right) = 8$$

$$\hat{R} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{3} + \frac{8}{4}}{2/3}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.5}{0.6667}} = \sqrt{3.75} \approx 1.94$$

The chains clearly disagree.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A chain of $n = 20{,}000$ samples with $\sigma = 2$ has $\rho_1 = 0.6$, $\rho_2 = 0.3$, $\rho_3 = 0.1$, and $\rho_k \approx 0$ for $k \ge 4$. Find $\tau$, the ESS, and the standard errors of the mean with and without the correction.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\tau = 1 + 2(0.6 + 0.3 + 0.1) = 3, \qquad \text{ESS} = \frac{20{,}000}{3} \approx 6{,}667$$

$$\operatorname{SE}_{\text{naive}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{20{,}000}} = 0.0141, \qquad \operatorname{SE}_{\text{correct}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{6667}} = 0.0245$$

The naive error bar is too small by a factor $\sqrt{3} = 1.73$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left( 1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (1 - \frac{k}{n}) \rho_k \right)$ for a stationary sequence, and show that $\tau = \frac{1 + r}{1 - r}$ when $\rho_k = r^k$ with $0 \le r \lt 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By bilinearity of covariance, $\operatorname{Var}(\sum_t X_t) = \sum_{s,t} \operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t)$. By stationarity, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \sigma^2 \rho_{\lvert s - t \rvert}$. The number of ordered pairs $(s, t)$ with $\lvert s - t \rvert = k$ is $n$ for $k = 0$ and $2(n - k)$ for $1 \le k \le n - 1$. So

$$\operatorname{Var}\left( \sum_t X_t \right) = \sigma^2 \left( n + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (n - k) \rho_k \right)$$

Divide by $n^2$ to get the formula. As $n \to \infty$, $(1 - \frac{k}{n}) \to 1$ and the sum tends to $\tau = 1 + 2 \sum_{k \ge 1} \rho_k$ (when it converges). For $\rho_k = r^k$ the geometric series gives $\sum_{k \ge 1} r^k = \frac{r}{1 - r}$, so

$$\tau = 1 + \frac{2r}{1 - r} = \frac{1 - r + 2r}{1 - r} = \frac{1 + r}{1 - r}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Run 40 independent random-walk Metropolis chains with step $2.4$ for $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, 50,000 steps each, starting at $0$. Compare the naive standard error of the mean of one chain, its ESS-based standard error, and the real spread of the 40 chain means.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_chains, n_steps, step = 40, 50_000, 2.4

# 40 independent random-walk Metropolis chains for the N(0, 1) target, run side by side.
x = np.zeros(n_chains)
chains = np.empty((n_chains, n_steps))
for t in range(n_steps):
    proposal = x + step * rng.standard_normal(n_chains)
    accept = np.log(rng.random(n_chains)) < (x**2 - proposal**2) / 2
    x = np.where(accept, proposal, x)
    chains[:, t] = x

# Effective sample size of chain 0: add autocorrelations up to the first negative one.
v = chains[0] - chains[0].mean()
acf = np.correlate(v, v, mode="full")[n_steps - 1:] / (v @ v)
cutoff = np.argmax(acf[1:] < 0)
tau = 1 + 2 * acf[1 : cutoff + 1].sum()
print(f"tau = {tau:.2f}, ESS = {n_steps / tau:,.0f} of {n_steps:,}")
print(f"naive SE of the mean       {chains[0].std() / np.sqrt(n_steps):.4f}")
print(f"ESS-based SE of the mean   {chains[0].std() / np.sqrt(n_steps / tau):.4f}")
print(f"real spread of 40 means    {chains.mean(axis=1).std(ddof=1):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
tau = 4.14, ESS = 12,065 of 50,000
naive SE of the mean       0.0045
ESS-based SE of the mean   0.0091
real spread of 40 means    0.0088
```

The ESS-based error bar ($0.0091$) matches the real spread ($0.0088$), while the naive one ($0.0045$) is about half of it.

</details>

## Summary

- Discard a burn-in at the start: the early samples have the wrong distribution. Too little burn-in leaves a bias, too much costs only a little data.
- MCMC samples are correlated. The integrated autocorrelation time $\tau = 1 + 2 \sum_k \rho_k$ gives the effective sample size $\text{ESS} = \frac{n}{\tau}$ and the error bar $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\text{ESS}}}$.
- Tune the step size: both tiny and huge steps give a large $\tau$. A random-walk step with acceptance near $0.44$ (one dimension) or $0.23$ (many dimensions) is a good start.
- Run several chains from spread-out starts and check $\hat{R} \approx 1$. A large $\hat{R}$ means the chains disagree (not mixed, or stuck in different peaks).
- No diagnostic proves convergence; they only detect failure. Look at trace plots, ESS and $\hat{R}$ together.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{ESS} = \frac{n}{1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_k}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar{x}_n) \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\text{ESS}}}}
$$
