# 19.7.1 Metropolis-Hastings

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 19: Simulation and Monte Carlo](../README.md) / [19.7 MCMC](README.md) / 19.7.1 Metropolis-Hastings

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [19.6 Importance Sampling](../19.6-Importance-Sampling.md)

> **Big idea:** To sample from a target $p$ that we can only evaluate up to a constant, walk a random walker around: propose a move, accept it with probability $\min\big(1, \frac{p(x')}{p(x)}\big)$ (for a symmetric proposal), and the long-run positions of the walker follow $p$.

---

## Why It Matters

Rejection sampling ([19.5](../19.5-Rejection-Sampling.md)) and importance sampling ([19.6](../19.6-Importance-Sampling.md)) break down in many dimensions: the roof is too far above the target, the weights are too uneven. **Markov chain Monte Carlo** (**MCMC**) avoids the problem. It does not draw independent samples. It builds a Markov chain ([18.3](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/README.md)) whose stationary distribution ([18.3.5](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md)) is the target, and then runs it. The most important recipe is the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. It is the engine of Bayesian inference, where the posterior is known only up to a constant.

## Intuition

Picture a hiker who wants to spend time in each place in proportion to its height on a landscape of hills. At each step the hiker looks at a random nearby spot. If it is higher, the hiker always walks there. If it is lower, the hiker flips a biased coin and walks there only with probability equal to $\frac{\text{height of the new spot}}{\text{height of the old spot}}$. Otherwise the hiker stays and counts the current spot once more.

Why does this work? The hiker climbs hills easily but can also go down, so every part of the landscape gets visited. A spot twice as high as its neighbour is entered twice as often as it is left, so the hiker spends twice as much time on it. In the long run the time spent in a place is proportional to its height. Note that only the **ratio** of heights is used, so we never need to know the total area (the normalizing constant).

## Picture

```text
  p(x)
   |             .-'''-.
   |           .'   B   '.           A to B: uphill, always accept
   |         .'    ^      '.
   |       .'      |        '.       B to C: downhill, accept with
   |    A .'       |          '.  C  probability p(C) / p(B),
   |  .-'                        '-. otherwise stay at B
   +----------------------------------- x
```

An uphill proposal is always accepted. A downhill proposal is accepted with probability equal to the ratio of heights, so the walker spends more time on high ground.

## Definition

Let $p$ be the target density (or PMF). Suppose we can compute $\tilde{p}(x) = Z p(x)$ for an unknown constant $Z$. Let $q(x' \mid x)$ be a **proposal**: a rule that suggests a new point $x'$ when the walker is at $x$. The **Metropolis-Hastings algorithm** starts at some $x_0$ and repeats, for $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$:

1. Propose $x' \sim q(\cdot \mid x_t)$.
2. Compute the **acceptance probability**

$$
\alpha(x_t, x') = \min \left( 1, \thinspace \frac{\tilde{p}(x') \thinspace q(x_t \mid x')}{\tilde{p}(x_t) \thinspace q(x' \mid x_t)} \right)
$$

3. Draw $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. If $U \le \alpha$, **accept**: set $x_{t+1} = x'$. Otherwise **reject**: set $x_{t+1} = x_t$ (the walker stays, and $x_t$ is recorded again).

**In words:** accept a proposed move with probability equal to how much more likely the new point is under $p$, corrected for how likely the proposal was to go forward versus backward.

Two common cases:

- **Metropolis algorithm** (symmetric proposal, $q(x' \mid x) = q(x \mid x')$): the $q$ terms cancel and $\alpha = \min\big(1, \frac{\tilde{p}(x')}{\tilde{p}(x)}\big)$.
- **Random-walk Metropolis:** $x' = x + s \thinspace \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (or uniform). The number $s$ is the **step size**. This proposal is symmetric.

The constant $Z$ cancels in the ratio, so only $\tilde{p}$ is needed. After a start-up period (the **burn-in**, [19.7.3](19.7.3-Burn-in-Mixing-and-Diagnostics.md)), the values $x_t$ are samples from $p$, and we estimate $E[f(X)]$ by $\frac{1}{n} \sum f(x_t)$ as in [19.3](../19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md). The samples are **correlated**, so the error bar needs care (Example 2 and 3, and [19.7.3](19.7.3-Burn-in-Mixing-and-Diagnostics.md)).

## Why It Is True

Let $K(x \to x')$ be the transition density of the chain for $x' \ne x$: first propose $x'$, then accept. So

$$
K(x \to x') = q(x' \mid x) \thinspace \alpha(x, x')
$$

**Detailed balance.** Multiply by $\tilde{p}(x)$ and use $\tilde{p}(x) \min(1, r) = \min(\tilde{p}(x), \tilde{p}(x) r)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{p}(x) \thinspace K(x \to x') &= \tilde{p}(x) \thinspace q(x' \mid x) \min \left( 1, \frac{\tilde{p}(x') q(x \mid x')}{\tilde{p}(x) q(x' \mid x)} \right) \\
&= \min \big( \tilde{p}(x) q(x' \mid x), \thinspace \tilde{p}(x') q(x \mid x') \big) && \text{(move } \tilde{p}(x) q(x' \mid x) \text{ inside)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last expression does not change when we swap $x$ and $x'$. So

$$
\tilde{p}(x) \thinspace K(x \to x') = \tilde{p}(x') \thinspace K(x' \to x)
$$

This is **detailed balance** ([18.3.6](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.6-Detailed-Balance-and-Reversibility.md)): in equilibrium the flow from $x$ to $x'$ equals the flow back. (For $x' = x$ there is nothing to check.)

**Stationarity.** Detailed balance implies that $p$ is stationary. Sum (or integrate) both sides over $x$:

$$
\int p(x) K(x \to x') \thinspace dx = \int p(x') K(x' \to x) \thinspace dx = p(x') \int K(x' \to x) \thinspace dx = p(x')
$$

The last step holds because from $x'$ the chain must go somewhere (or stay), so the transition probabilities add up to 1. This is Theorem 1 of [18.3.6](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.6-Detailed-Balance-and-Reversibility.md) with sums replaced by integrals.

**Convergence.** A stationary law is only the target if the chain reaches it from any start. This needs the chain to be irreducible and aperiodic ([18.3.4](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.4-Irreducible-and-Aperiodic-Chains.md)) and then the law of $X_t$ tends to $p$ ([18.3.8](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md)). A random-walk proposal with a density that is positive everywhere can reach any region, and rejections give a positive chance of staying, which removes any rhythm. The average of $f(X_t)$ then tends to $E_p[f(X)]$ (the ergodic theorem, the Markov chain version of the Law of Large Numbers).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Four-State Chain, Checked by Hand

**Problem.** The target on the states $1, 2, 3, 4$ is $\tilde{p} = (1, 2, 3, 4)$, so $p = (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4)$. The proposal is: move left or right with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each (a proposal outside $1$ to $4$ is rejected). Write the transition probabilities and check that $p$ is stationary.

**Solution.**

1. The proposal is symmetric, so $\alpha = \min\big(1, \frac{p(x')}{p(x)}\big)$.
2. From state 1: right to 2 with probability $\frac{1}{2} \min(1, \frac{2}{1}) = 0.5$. Left is outside, so stay with probability $0.5$.
3. From state 2: left to 1 with probability $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 0.25$, right to 3 with probability $\frac{1}{2} \min(1, \frac{3}{2}) = 0.5$, stay with probability $0.25$.
4. From state 3: left to 2 with probability $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$, right to 4 with probability $0.5$, stay with probability $\frac{1}{6}$.
5. From state 4: left to 3 with probability $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{4} = 0.375$, stay with probability $0.625$.
6. Detailed balance: $p_1 P_{12} = 0.1 \times 0.5 = 0.05 = 0.2 \times 0.25 = p_2 P_{21}$. Then $p_2 P_{23} = 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1 = 0.3 \times \frac{1}{3} = p_3 P_{32}$. Then $p_3 P_{34} = 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15 = 0.4 \times 0.375 = p_4 P_{43}$.
7. Stationarity of state 1: $\sum_i p_i P_{i1} = 0.1 \times 0.5 + 0.2 \times 0.25 = 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.1 = p_1$.

**Answer.** All three pairs balance, so $p = (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4)$ is stationary for this chain.

**Check.** The first code block runs the chain for 200,000 steps and finds visit frequencies $0.0992, 0.1985, 0.3004, 0.4019$, close to the target.

### Example 2: A Distribution With Two Peaks

**Problem.** The target is $p = 0.4 \thinspace \mathcal{N}(-2, 1) + 0.6 \thinspace \mathcal{N}(2, 1)$ (a mixture of two normals, [10.8](../../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)). It has no simple inverse CDF. Use random-walk Metropolis to estimate $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $P(X \gt 0)$, and give error bars.

**Solution.**

1. Exact values: $E[X] = 0.4(-2) + 0.6(2) = 0.4$. $E[X^2] = 0.4 \times (4 + 1) + 0.6 \times (4 + 1) = 5$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 5 - 0.4^2 = 4.84$. $P(X \gt 0) = 0.4 \times 0.02275 + 0.6 \times 0.97725 = 0.5954$.
2. The proposal is $x' = x + 2.5 \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The step $2.5$ is large enough to jump between the peaks (they are 4 apart).
3. We run 50 independent chains of 40,000 steps, each from its own random start, discarding the first 1,000 steps of each. The error bar is the spread of the 50 chain averages divided by $\sqrt{50}$. This is valid because the chains are independent, even though the steps inside one chain are not.

**Answer.** The code gives $E[X] \approx 0.401 \pm 0.005$, $\operatorname{Var}(X) \approx 4.837$ and $P(X \gt 0) \approx 0.5953 \pm 0.0010$. The acceptance rate is $0.577$.

**Check.** All three are within one standard error of the exact values $0.400$, $4.840$ and $0.5954$. The standard error of $E[X]$ is larger than for independent samples would be, because neighbouring steps of one chain are correlated: in 2 million steps the information is worth far fewer independent samples ([19.7.3](19.7.3-Burn-in-Mixing-and-Diagnostics.md)).

### Example 3: Bayesian Inference for a Coin

**Problem.** A coin shows 7 heads in 10 flips. The prior for the heads probability $\theta$ is $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$. The posterior is $\text{Beta}(9, 5)$ ([4.6](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md), [9.5](../../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md)). Pretend we do not know this, and run Metropolis on the unnormalized posterior. Do two steps by hand.

**Solution.**

1. Unnormalized posterior = prior $\times$ likelihood: $\tilde{p}(\theta) = \theta (1 - \theta) \cdot \theta^7 (1 - \theta)^3 = \theta^8 (1 - \theta)^4$ on $(0, 1)$, and 0 outside.
2. The walker is at $\theta = 0.5$. Propose $\theta' = 0.6$. Then $\tilde{p}(0.5) = 0.5^{12} = 0.000244$ and $\tilde{p}(0.6) = 0.6^8 \times 0.4^4 = 0.01680 \times 0.0256 = 0.000430$. The ratio is $\frac{0.000430}{0.000244} = 1.761 \gt 1$, so the move is accepted for sure.
3. From $\theta = 0.6$, propose $\theta' = 0.9$. Then $\tilde{p}(0.9) = 0.9^8 \times 0.1^4 = 0.4305 \times 0.0001 = 0.0000430$. The ratio is $\frac{0.0000430}{0.000430} = 0.100$. So the move is accepted with probability $0.10$. With $U = 0.4$ it is rejected, and the walker stays at $0.6$ and records $0.6$ again.

**Answer.** After the code runs 100,000 steps, the posterior mean is $0.6422 \pm 0.0012$ (exact $\frac{9}{14} = 0.6429$), the posterior standard deviation is $0.1244$ (exact $0.1237$) and $P(\theta \gt 0.5) = 0.8634 \pm 0.0031$ (exact $0.8666$).

**Check.** The error bars come from 20 batch means, because one chain's samples are correlated. The estimates are within about one standard error of the truth. A proposal that leaves $(0, 1)$ has $\tilde{p} = 0$, so it is always rejected and the walker never leaves the valid range.

## Simulate It

First, the four-state chain of Example 1:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p = np.array([0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4])               # target on states 0..3 (labels 1..4)
n_steps = 200_000

state, counts = 0, np.zeros(4)
for _ in range(n_steps):
    proposal = state + rng.choice([-1, 1])        # symmetric proposal: left or right
    if 0 <= proposal < 4 and rng.random() < min(1, p[proposal] / p[state]):
        state = proposal                          # accept the move
    counts[state] += 1                            # a rejected move still counts the old state

print("visit frequencies:", np.round(counts / n_steps, 4))
print("target:           ", p)
```

Output:

```text
visit frequencies: [0.0992 0.1985 0.3004 0.4019]
target:            [0.1 0.2 0.3 0.4]
```

Next, the two-peak target of Example 2, with 50 chains run side by side in numpy:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def log_target(x):
    # Unnormalized: 0.4 N(-2, 1) + 0.6 N(2, 1); the constant 1/sqrt(2 pi) is dropped.
    return np.log(0.4 * np.exp(-(x + 2) ** 2 / 2) + 0.6 * np.exp(-(x - 2) ** 2 / 2))

n_chains, n_steps, burn_in, step = 50, 40_000, 1_000, 2.5
x = rng.normal(0, 3, n_chains)                       # a different start for each chain
sums, n_accept = np.zeros((3, n_chains)), 0
for t in range(n_steps):
    proposal = x + step * rng.standard_normal(n_chains)           # random-walk proposal
    accept = np.log(rng.random(n_chains)) < log_target(proposal) - log_target(x)
    x = np.where(accept, proposal, x)
    n_accept += accept.sum()
    if t >= burn_in:                                               # discard the burn-in
        sums += np.array([x, x**2, x > 0])

mean_per_chain = sums / (n_steps - burn_in)                        # rows: x, x^2, I(x > 0)
est = mean_per_chain.mean(axis=1)
se = mean_per_chain.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_chains)        # spread across chains
print(f"acceptance rate {n_accept / (n_steps * n_chains):.3f}")
print(f"mean     {est[0]:.3f} +- {se[0]:.3f}   (exact 0.400)")
print(f"variance {est[1] - est[0]**2:.3f}            (exact 4.840)")
print(f"P(X > 0) {est[2]:.4f} +- {se[2]:.4f} (exact {0.4 * 0.022750 + 0.6 * 0.977250:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
acceptance rate 0.577
mean     0.401 +- 0.005   (exact 0.400)
variance 4.837            (exact 4.840)
P(X > 0) 0.5953 +- 0.0010 (exact 0.5954)
```

We compare in log scale (`log(U) < log ratio`) because tiny densities can underflow to 0 in floating point. Last, the coin posterior of Example 3:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def unnormalized_posterior(t):
    # Prior Beta(2,2) times likelihood of 7 heads in 10 flips: t^8 (1-t)^4 on (0, 1).
    return np.where((t > 0) & (t < 1), t**8 * (1 - t) ** 4, 0.0)

n_steps, step, burn_in = 100_000, 0.2, 1_000
theta, n_accept = 0.5, 0
samples = np.empty(n_steps)
for i in range(n_steps):
    proposal = theta + step * rng.uniform(-1, 1)                   # symmetric proposal
    ratio = unnormalized_posterior(proposal) / unnormalized_posterior(theta)
    if rng.random() < ratio:                                       # accept with prob min(1, ratio)
        theta = proposal
        n_accept += 1
    samples[i] = theta
kept = samples[burn_in:]

def batch_se(values, n_batches=20):
    # Samples are correlated, so use the spread of 20 batch means for the error bar.
    batches = values[: len(values) // n_batches * n_batches].reshape(n_batches, -1).mean(axis=1)
    return batches.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_batches)

print(f"acceptance rate {n_accept / n_steps:.3f}")
print(f"posterior mean {kept.mean():.4f} +- {batch_se(kept):.4f} (exact {9 / 14:.4f})")
print(f"posterior sd   {kept.std():.4f}           (exact {beta.std(9, 5):.4f})")
print(f"P(theta > 0.5) {np.mean(kept > 0.5):.4f} +- {batch_se(kept > 0.5):.4f} (exact {beta.sf(0.5, 9, 5):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
acceptance rate 0.703
posterior mean 0.6422 +- 0.0012 (exact 0.6429)
posterior sd   0.1244           (exact 0.1237)
P(theta > 0.5) 0.8634 +- 0.0031 (exact 0.8666)
```

Here `ratio` can be larger than 1, and `rng.random() < ratio` is then always true, which is the rule $\min(1, \text{ratio})$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Dropping the repeated value when a move is rejected.** On rejection the chain stays and the old value must be recorded again. Recording only accepted moves removes the time spent at high-density points and gives the wrong distribution.
- **Using an asymmetric proposal without the Hastings correction.** If $q(x' \mid x) \ne q(x \mid x')$, the ratio must contain $\frac{q(x \mid x')}{q(x' \mid x)}$ (Practice 5). Without it, detailed balance fails and the chain has the wrong stationary law.
- **Working with raw densities that underflow.** A density like $e^{-800}$ is 0 in floating point. Use $\log \tilde{p}$ and compare $\log U$ with the difference of logs.
- **Treating MCMC samples as independent.** Neighbouring samples are close to each other. The naive standard error $\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$ is too small. Use several chains, batch means, or the effective sample size ([19.7.3](19.7.3-Burn-in-Mixing-and-Diagnostics.md)).
- **A bad step size.** A tiny step is accepted almost always but the chain crawls. A huge step lands in low-density regions and is almost always rejected, so the chain stays put. Tune the step so that a reasonable fraction of moves (often 20% to 50%) is accepted.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Bayesian neural networks and Bayesian models in tools like PyMC and Stan (which use the Hamiltonian variant of the same accept-reject idea), sampling from energy-based models, and training some generative models.
- **Computer science:** simulated annealing for hard optimization problems is Metropolis with a probability $\propto e^{-E/T}$ and a falling temperature $T$. Randomized counting and sampling of combinatorial objects (colourings, matchings) use it too.
- **Physics:** the original use, from 1953: sampling the Boltzmann distribution $\propto e^{-E/kT}$ of a system of particles without ever computing the partition function.
- **Statistics and medicine:** posterior distributions of drug effects, disease models and survey models with no closed form.
- **Everyday life:** a recommender or a search that wanders between options, mostly keeping good ones and sometimes trying a worse one, behaves like a cooled Metropolis walker.

**2. (Easy)** The target is $\tilde{p}(x) = e^{-x^2/2}$ (a standard normal with the constant dropped). The walker is at $x = 1$ and the symmetric proposal is $x' = 2$. Find $\alpha$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\alpha = \min\left(1, \frac{e^{-2}}{e^{-1/2}}\right) = e^{-1.5} \approx 0.2231$$

We did not need the constant $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$, because it cancels in the ratio.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The target on the states $1, 2, 3$ is $p = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$. The proposal picks one of the two other states with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Find $P_{12}$, $P_{13}$, $P_{11}$, and verify detailed balance between states 1 and 2.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The proposal is symmetric. $P_{12} = \frac{1}{2} \min(1, \frac{0.3}{0.5}) = 0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.3$. $P_{13} = \frac{1}{2} \min(1, \frac{0.2}{0.5}) = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2$. $P_{11} = 1 - 0.3 - 0.2 = 0.5$. For the reverse move, $P_{21} = \frac{1}{2} \min(1, \frac{0.5}{0.3}) = 0.5$. Then

$$p_1 P_{12} = 0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.15 \qquad p_2 P_{21} = 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15$$

so detailed balance holds for the pair $(1, 2)$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** You run random-walk Metropolis on a standard normal target. Describe what happens to the chain when the step size is $0.01$, and when it is $100$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With step $0.01$, almost every move is accepted (the new point has almost the same density), but each move is tiny, so the chain crawls and takes a very long time to cover the range $-3$ to $3$. The samples are strongly correlated. With step $100$, the proposed point is usually far in the tail, where the density is almost 0, so almost every move is rejected and the chain stays at the same value for a long time. Both give a poor estimate. A step of order of the width of the target (about $1$ to $2.4$ here) works much better.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $x \gt 0$ and propose $x' = x e^{\sigma Z}$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (a log-normal random walk). Show that $\frac{q(x \mid x')}{q(x' \mid x)} = \frac{x'}{x}$, and write the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Since $\ln x' \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln x, \sigma^2)$, the density of $x'$ given $x$ is, by the change of variables from $\ln x'$ to $x'$ (a factor $\frac{1}{x'}$),

$$q(x' \mid x) = \frac{1}{x' \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(\ln x' - \ln x)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$$

Swapping the roles gives $q(x \mid x') = \frac{1}{x \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(\ln x - \ln x')^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$. The exponentials are equal (the square does not care about the sign), so

$$\frac{q(x \mid x')}{q(x' \mid x)} = \frac{x'}{x}$$

The acceptance probability is therefore

$$\alpha = \min\left( 1, \frac{\tilde{p}(x') \thinspace x'}{\tilde{p}(x) \thinspace x} \right)$$

The proposal is not symmetric: it goes to larger values more easily, and the factor $\frac{x'}{x}$ corrects for this.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Sample from the $\text{Exp}(1)$ density $\tilde{p}(x) = e^{-x}$ on $x \gt 0$ with the log-normal proposal of Problem 5 ($\sigma = 0.5$, 200,000 steps, start at $1$). Estimate $E[X] = 1$ and $P(X \gt 2) = e^{-2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_steps, sigma = 200_000, 0.5

x, samples = 1.0, np.empty(n_steps)
for i in range(n_steps):
    proposal = x * np.exp(sigma * rng.standard_normal())       # log-normal random walk
    # Target e^{-x}; the Hastings factor q(x | x') / q(x' | x) equals x' / x.
    log_ratio = (-proposal + np.log(proposal)) - (-x + np.log(x))
    if np.log(rng.random()) < log_ratio:
        x = proposal
    samples[i] = x
kept = samples[1_000:]
print(f"mean {kept.mean():.3f} (exact 1.000)")
print(f"P(X > 2) {np.mean(kept > 2):.4f} (exact {np.exp(-2):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
mean 0.994 (exact 1.000)
P(X > 2) 0.1348 (exact 0.1353)
```

Without the factor $\log x'$ minus $\log x$ the chain would have a different stationary law.

</details>

## Summary

- MCMC builds a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the target, so a long run of the chain gives (correlated) samples from the target.
- Metropolis-Hastings: propose $x' \sim q(\cdot \mid x)$, accept with probability $\min\big(1, \frac{\tilde{p}(x') q(x \mid x')}{\tilde{p}(x) q(x' \mid x)}\big)$, otherwise stay.
- It needs the target only up to a constant, because the constant cancels in the ratio.
- It works because the rule satisfies detailed balance, so $p$ is stationary. Irreducibility and aperiodicity make the chain converge to it.
- Samples are correlated: use burn-in, several chains and batch means (or ESS) for the error bar.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\alpha(x, x') = \min \left( 1, \thinspace \frac{\tilde{p}(x') \thinspace q(x \mid x')}{\tilde{p}(x) \thinspace q(x' \mid x)} \right)}
$$
