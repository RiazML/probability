# 16.1 Markov Inequality

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.1 Markov Inequality

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 15: Generating Functions](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/README.md)

> **Big idea:** A variable that cannot be negative and has a small mean cannot be large very often: $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}$.

---

## Why It Matters

Often you cannot find a probability exactly, but you still need a number you can trust. **Markov's inequality** gives one from **only the mean**. It needs no formula for the distribution, no variance, and no independence. It is the weakest inequality in the module, and also the root of all the others: Chebyshev ([16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)), the Chernoff bound ([16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)) and Hoeffding ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)) are all Markov's inequality applied to a clever new variable.

## Intuition

A town has 1,000 people. The **average** monthly income is 40,000 taka. Nobody earns a negative income. How many people can earn 200,000 taka or more?

Suppose 300 people earned 200,000 each. Those 300 people alone would add up to $300 \times 200{,}000 = 60{,}000{,}000$ taka. But the whole town earns only $1{,}000 \times 40{,}000 = 40{,}000{,}000$ taka in total. That is not possible, because the other 700 people cannot earn less than zero.

The most we can allow is $\frac{40{,}000{,}000}{200{,}000} = 200$ rich people, and then everybody else earns exactly 0. So **at most 200 out of 1,000, which is 20%**, can earn 200,000 taka or more. The reason is simple: the rich people use up the budget.

This is Markov's inequality. In real life the true fraction is much smaller than 20%, because real incomes are not like that extreme case. But the bound is **always true**, for every town, whatever the shape of the incomes.

## Picture

```text
  y
  |                       /
  |                     /
  |                   /
a | . . . . . . . . o===========================   y = a * I(X >= a)
  |               / |
  |             /   |
  |           /     |
  |         /       |
  |       /         |
  |     /           |
  |   /             |
--+-----------------+-----------------------------  x
  0                 a

  The slanted line is y = x.  The step is 0 before a and equals a from a on.
  The line is never below the step: X >= a * I(X >= a) for every X >= 0.
```

The step function lies under the line $y = x$ everywhere. Taking the average of both sides gives Markov's inequality.

## Definition

**Markov's inequality.** Let $X$ be a random variable with $X \ge 0$ (it never takes a negative value), and let $a \gt 0$. Then

$$
P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}
$$

where:

- $E[X]$ is the mean of $X$. We assume it is a finite number.
- $a$ is the threshold. The bound is useful only when $a \gt E[X]$. Otherwise $\frac{E[X]}{a} \ge 1$, and every probability is at most 1 anyway.

**In words:** the chance that a non-negative variable is at least $a$ is at most its mean divided by $a$.

A form that is easy to remember uses a multiple of the mean. Put $a = c \thinspace E[X]$ with $c \gt 0$ (and $E[X] \gt 0$):

$$
P\big(X \ge c \thinspace E[X]\big) \le \frac{1}{c}
$$

**In words:** a non-negative variable is at least 10 times its mean with probability at most $\frac{1}{10}$.

There is also a **general Markov inequality**. Let $g$ be a non-decreasing function with $g(x) \ge 0$ for all $x$, and let $g(a) \gt 0$. Then

$$
P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[g(X)]}{g(a)}
$$

This is the version that the other inequalities use. With $g(x) = x^2$ we get the idea of Chebyshev, and with $g(x) = e^{tx}$ we get the Chernoff bound.

## Why It Is True

**The indicator proof.** The **indicator** $I_A$ of an event $A$ is the variable that equals 1 when $A$ happens and 0 otherwise ([5.5](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.5-Indicator-Random-Variables.md)). Its mean is $E[I_A] = P(A)$. Fix $a \gt 0$ and look at the event $\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace$. We claim that for every value of $X$:

$$
a \cdot I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace} \le X
$$

There are two cases:

- If $X \ge a$, the left side is $a \cdot 1 = a$, and $a \le X$. True.
- If $X \lt a$, the left side is $a \cdot 0 = 0$, and $0 \le X$ because $X \ge 0$. True. **This is the place where we need $X \ge 0$.**

Now take the expectation of both sides. Expectation keeps inequalities (a bigger variable has a bigger mean), and a constant comes out in front ([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
a \cdot P(X \ge a) &= E\big[a \cdot I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace}\big] && \text{(constant out; } E[I_A] = P(A) \text{)} \\
&\le E[X] && \text{(the inequality above, averaged)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $a \gt 0$ and we get $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}$.

**The same proof for a density.** Let $X \ge 0$ have density $f$. Split the integral for the mean at $a$, and throw away the part below $a$ (it is not negative):

$$
E[X] = \int_0^a x f(x) \thinspace dx + \int_a^{\infty} x f(x) \thinspace dx \ge \int_a^{\infty} x f(x) \thinspace dx \ge \int_a^{\infty} a f(x) \thinspace dx = a \thinspace P(X \ge a)
$$

The last step uses $x \ge a$ on the range of the integral. In words: the mean must pay for the tail, and every point in the tail costs at least $a$.

**The general version.** If $g$ is non-decreasing and $g \ge 0$, then $X \ge a$ implies $g(X) \ge g(a)$. So $g(a) \thinspace I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace} \le g(X)$, and the same two steps give $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[g(X)]}{g(a)}$.

**When is it tight?** The proof loses something in two places: when $X \lt a$ (we replace $X$ by 0) and when $X \ge a$ (we replace $X$ by $a$). There is no loss only if $X$ takes just two values, $0$ and $a$. Take $P(X = a) = p$ and $P(X = 0) = 1 - p$. Then $E[X] = ap$, and

$$
P(X \ge a) = p = \frac{ap}{a} = \frac{E[X]}{a}
$$

So Markov's inequality is an **equality** for this variable. No better bound can be written using only the mean.

> **Note:** A useful special case. If $X$ takes whole-number values $0, 1, 2, \dots$ and $E[X] \lt 1$, then $P(X \ge 1) \le E[X]$. In words: if you expect fewer than one bad thing, the chance of any bad thing is at most the expected number. The union bound ([16.5](16.5-Union-Bound-Revisited.md)) is exactly this, with $X$ = the number of bad events.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Fair Die, Bound Against the Exact Value

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. Compare Markov's bound with the exact probability $P(X \ge a)$ for $a = 2, 4, 6$.

**Solution.**

1. The mean is $E[X] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = 3.5$. A die roll is never negative, so Markov applies.
2. For each $a$, the bound is $\frac{3.5}{a}$, and the exact value is (number of faces $\ge a$) divided by 6:

| $a$ | Markov bound $\frac{3.5}{a}$ | Exact $P(X \ge a)$ | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | $1.75$ | $\frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833$ | bound above 1, useless |
| 4 | $0.875$ | $\frac{3}{6} = 0.5$ | true but loose |
| 6 | $0.583$ | $\frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$ | about 3.5 times too big |

**Answer.** The bound is always true, but it is often far above the exact value.

**Check.** For $a = 2$ the bound $1.75$ is above 1, so it says nothing. The simulation below agrees with the exact column.

### Example 2: Restarting a Randomized Algorithm

**Problem.** A randomized algorithm (a "Las Vegas" algorithm: its answer is always correct, but the running time is random) has expected running time $E[T] = 10$ milliseconds. We know nothing else about $T$. Show that we can make it finish within 200 milliseconds except with probability below $\frac{1}{1000}$.

**Solution.**

1. The running time is $T \ge 0$. Markov with $a = 20$ gives:

$$
P(T \ge 20) \le \frac{10}{20} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

2. So one run finishes within 20 ms with probability **at least** $\frac{1}{2}$.
3. New plan: run the algorithm for at most 20 ms. If it is not done, stop it and start again with fresh random numbers. Each attempt is independent, and each fails with probability at most $\frac{1}{2}$.
4. We can afford 10 attempts in 200 ms. All 10 fail with probability at most:

$$
\left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{10} = \frac{1}{1024} \lt \frac{1}{1000}
$$

**Answer.** With 10 restarts the algorithm finishes within 200 ms with probability above $0.999$.

**Check.** Markov's weak bound (only 50% per attempt) is enough, because independent restarts multiply the failure chances. This "run, cut off, restart" trick is used in practice for randomized search and for SAT solvers.

### Example 3: A Server and Its Slow Requests

**Problem.** A web server answers requests in 200 milliseconds on average. What fraction of requests can take 1 second (1,000 milliseconds) or more?

**Solution.**

1. Response times are not negative. The mean is $E[X] = 200$ and the threshold is $a = 1000$.
2. Markov:

$$
P(X \ge 1000) \le \frac{200}{1000} = 0.2
$$

**Answer.** At most 20% of requests are that slow.

**Check.** If someone tells you "only 1% are that slow", that is allowed, since $0.01 \le 0.2$. If someone says "30% are that slow", the mean must be wrong: those requests alone would give $0.3 \times 1000 = 300$ milliseconds of average time, more than 200.

## Simulate It

We take $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1, so mean 1) and compare the simulated tail, the exact tail $e^{-a}$ and the Markov bound $\frac{1}{a}$. We also check the die from Example 1.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# X ~ Exp(1) (rate 1) has mean 1, so Markov says P(X >= a) <= 1/a.
x = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=n_trials)

print("  a   simulated   exact e^(-a)   Markov 1/a")
for a in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10]:
    print(f"{a:3d}   {np.mean(x >= a):.5f}     {np.exp(-a):.5f}       {1 / a:.5f}")

# A fair die has mean 3.5. Compare P(X >= 6) with Markov.
die = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
print(f"Die: P(X >= 6) simulated {np.mean(die >= 6):.4f}, exact {1 / 6:.4f}, Markov {3.5 / 6:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
  a   simulated   exact e^(-a)   Markov 1/a
  1   0.36759     0.36788       1.00000
  2   0.13544     0.13534       0.50000
  3   0.04961     0.04979       0.33333
  5   0.00670     0.00674       0.20000
 10   0.00004     0.00005       0.10000
Die: P(X >= 6) simulated 0.1666, exact 0.1667, Markov 0.5833
```

The Markov column is always above the true tail, as it must be. For the exponential, the true tail falls like $e^{-a}$ (very fast) while Markov only falls like $\frac{1}{a}$ (slowly). At $a = 10$, the bound is about 2,000 times too big.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using it for a variable that can be negative.** Take $X = -100$ or $+100$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Then $E[X] = 0$, but $P(X \ge 100) = \frac{1}{2}$, which is not $\le 0$. Markov needs $X \ge 0$. For a general $Y$, apply it to $\lvert Y \rvert$ or to $(Y - \mu)^2$ (see [16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)).
- **Reading the bound as the answer.** Markov says "at most", not "equal to". For the exponential at $a = 5$ it gives $0.2$, but the true value is $0.0067$. Use it when nothing better is known.
- **Choosing $a$ below the mean.** If $a \le E[X]$ the bound is $\ge 1$ and tells you nothing.
- **Turning it around.** Markov bounds the **upper** tail. It does not say that $X$ is likely to be near its mean, and it gives no lower bound on $P(X \ge a)$.
- **Forgetting that $E[X]$ must be finite.** If the mean is infinite, the right side is infinite and the bound says nothing.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Markov's inequality turns "the expected error is small" into "the error is large only with small probability". It is the first step in many proofs about learning algorithms.
- **Computer science:** the restart trick of Example 2 and the analysis of randomized algorithms. From the *expected* running time you get a *probability* of finishing on time. In hashing, the expected number of collisions is small, so collisions are rare.
- **The first-moment method:** if the expected number of bad objects is below 1, then with positive probability there are none, so a good object exists. This proves that good objects exist without building them.
- **Engineering and finance:** with only a mean (average load, average loss) you can still give a safe worst-case fraction, as in Example 3.
- **Everyday life:** "the average person here earns 40,000 taka" already tells you that at most 1 person in 10 can earn 400,000 taka or more.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** The average number of emails a person gets in a day is 20. Use Markov's inequality to bound the probability that a person gets 100 or more emails in a day.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The number of emails is not negative, with $E[X] = 20$ and $a = 100$:

$$P(X \ge 100) \le \frac{20}{100} = 0.2$$

At most 20% of people get 100 or more emails.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A fair coin is flipped 10 times. Let $X$ be the number of heads. Use Markov to bound $P(X \ge 8)$ and compare it with the exact value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $E[X] = 10 \times 0.5 = 5$, so the bound is:

$$P(X \ge 8) \le \frac{5}{8} = 0.625$$

The exact value is the number of ways to get 8, 9 or 10 heads, divided by $2^{10}$:

$$P(X \ge 8) = \frac{\binom{10}{8} + \binom{10}{9} + \binom{10}{10}}{1024} = \frac{45 + 10 + 1}{1024} = \frac{56}{1024} \approx 0.0547$$

The bound is true, but about 11 times too large.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A non-negative variable $X$ has $E[X] = 3$. What is the largest possible value of $P(X \ge 12)$? Give a distribution that reaches it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Markov says $P(X \ge 12) \le \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}$. To reach it, use the two-point variable from "When is it tight?". Let $X = 12$ with probability $\frac{1}{4}$ and $X = 0$ with probability $\frac{3}{4}$. Then:

$$E[X] = 12 \times \tfrac{1}{4} + 0 \times \tfrac{3}{4} = 3 \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(X \ge 12) = \tfrac{1}{4}$$

So the largest possible value is exactly $\frac{1}{4}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X \ge 0$ with $E[X] = 0$. Prove that $P(X = 0) = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For any $a \gt 0$, Markov gives $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{0}{a} = 0$. The event $\lbrace X \gt 0 \rbrace$ is the union of the events $\lbrace X \ge \frac{1}{m} \rbrace$ for $m = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, and each of these has probability 0. The union bound for a countable union ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)) gives:

$$P(X \gt 0) \le \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} P\left(X \ge \tfrac{1}{m}\right) = 0$$

So $P(X \gt 0) = 0$, and since $X \ge 0$, we get $P(X = 0) = 1$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X \ge 0$ with $E[X] = \mu \gt 0$. Show that the general Markov inequality with $g(x) = x^2$ gives $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X^2]}{a^2}$. For which values of $a$ is this better than the plain Markov bound $\frac{\mu}{a}$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The function $g(x) = x^2$ is non-decreasing on $x \ge 0$ and $g(a) = a^2 \gt 0$. The event $\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace$ is the same as $\lbrace X^2 \ge a^2 \rbrace$, so Markov for the variable $X^2 \ge 0$ gives:

$$P(X \ge a) = P(X^2 \ge a^2) \le \frac{E[X^2]}{a^2}$$

It beats $\frac{\mu}{a}$ when $\frac{E[X^2]}{a^2} \lt \frac{\mu}{a}$, that is, when $a \gt \frac{E[X^2]}{\mu}$. So it wins for large $a$ when $X$ is concentrated. Example: $X = 1$ always, so $E[X] = E[X^2] = 1$. For $a = 10$ the plain bound is $\frac{1}{10}$ and the squared bound is $\frac{1}{100}$. This idea is the start of Chebyshev and Chernoff.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Let $X$ be the number of heads in 10 fair coin flips. Estimate $P(X \ge 8)$ by simulation and compare it with the exact value and the Markov bound from Problem 2.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
heads = rng.binomial(10, 0.5, size=n_trials)      # heads in 10 fair flips
print(f"simulated P(X >= 8) = {np.mean(heads >= 8):.4f}")
print(f"exact               = {56 / 1024:.4f}")
print(f"Markov bound        = {5 / 8:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
simulated P(X >= 8) = 0.0547
exact               = 0.0547
Markov bound        = 0.6250
```

</details>

## Summary

- Markov's inequality: for $X \ge 0$ and $a \gt 0$, $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}$. It uses only the mean.
- The proof is one line: $a \cdot I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace} \le X$, then take the expectation. Non-negativity is needed.
- It is tight only for variables that take just the values $0$ and $a$. Usually it is far too large, so treat it as a safe worst case.
- The general form $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[g(X)]}{g(a)}$ is the engine of Chebyshev and Chernoff.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a} \qquad (X \ge 0, \ a \gt 0)}
$$
