# 16.2 Chebyshev Inequality

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.2 Chebyshev Inequality

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [16.1 Markov Inequality](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)

> **Big idea:** Whatever the shape of the distribution, a random variable is within $k$ standard deviations of its mean with probability at least $1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$.

---

## Why It Matters

Markov's inequality ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)) uses only the mean, so it is weak. **Chebyshev's inequality** also uses the variance, and it bounds the chance that a variable is far from its mean on **either** side. It is the tool that turns the standard deviation from a vague "typical spread" ([6.5](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)) into a real guarantee. Its most famous use is the proof of the Weak Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)): the average of many independent measurements gets close to the true mean.

## Intuition

An exam has a class average of 60 marks and a standard deviation of 10 marks. You know nothing else: the marks could be bell-shaped, or lumpy, or anything.

How many students can be more than 20 marks away from the average (below 40 or above 80)? Think of the **variance** as the average of the squared distances from the mean. If 30% of the class were 20 or more away, those students alone would add at least $0.30 \times 20^2 = 120$ to the average squared distance. But the whole average squared distance is only $10^2 = 100$. Impossible.

So at most $\frac{100}{400} = \frac{1}{4}$, which is 25%, of the class can be that far away. Everybody else, at least 75%, is between 40 and 80. This is true for **every** distribution with this mean and standard deviation. The distance of 20 marks is 2 standard deviations, and $1 - \frac{1}{2^2} = 0.75$.

```text
    within 1 sd of mean:   at least  0%           68.3%
    within 2 sd of mean:   at least 75%           95.4%
    within 3 sd of mean:   at least 88.9%         99.7%
    within 4 sd of mean:   at least 93.75%        99.99%

```

The guarantee holds for every distribution, so it is smaller than what a bell curve gives. That is the price of knowing only $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

## Definition

**Chebyshev's inequality.** Let $X$ be a random variable with mean $\mu$ and a finite variance $\sigma^2$. For every number $k \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge k\sigma\big) \le \frac{1}{k^2}
$$

The same statement in terms of a distance $\varepsilon \gt 0$ (put $\varepsilon = k\sigma$):

$$
P\big(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

where:

- $\mu = E[X]$ and $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)$ are the mean and variance of $X$.
- $\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon$ is the event "far from the mean", in either direction.

**In words:** the chance that $X$ lands $\varepsilon$ or more away from its mean is at most its variance divided by $\varepsilon^2$.

Taking complements gives the "inside" form. A variable is **within $k$ standard deviations** of the mean with probability at least $1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$:

$$
P\big(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \lt k\sigma\big) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{k^2}
$$

The bound is useful only for $k \gt 1$. For $k \le 1$ the right side is at least 1, and says nothing.

| $k$ | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chebyshev: at most outside | $1$ | $0.444$ | $0.25$ | $0.111$ | $0.0625$ | $0.04$ | $0.01$ |
| Chebyshev: at least inside | $0$ | $0.556$ | $0.75$ | $0.889$ | $0.9375$ | $0.96$ | $0.99$ |

## Why It Is True

**Proof from Markov's inequality.** The variable $Y = (X - \mu)^2$ is never negative, and its mean is the variance: $E[Y] = \sigma^2$. The event $\lbrace \lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon \rbrace$ is the same event as $\lbrace (X - \mu)^2 \ge \varepsilon^2 \rbrace$, because squaring keeps order for non-negative numbers. Now apply Markov ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)) to $Y$ with threshold $\varepsilon^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\big(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) &= P\big((X - \mu)^2 \ge \varepsilon^2\big) && \text{(same event)} \\
&\le \frac{E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big]}{\varepsilon^2} && \text{(Markov for } Y \ge 0 \text{)} \\
&= \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2} && \text{(definition of variance)}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\varepsilon = k\sigma$ the right side is $\frac{\sigma^2}{k^2\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{k^2}$. Done.

**The intuition proof, made exact.** Let $A$ be the event $\lbrace \lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon \rbrace$. The variance is the average squared distance. Keep only the part of the average that comes from $A$ and throw away the rest (which is not negative). On $A$, each squared distance is at least $\varepsilon^2$:

$$
\sigma^2 = E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] \ge E\big[(X - \mu)^2 \thinspace I_A\big] \ge \varepsilon^2 \thinspace P(A)
$$

Divide by $\varepsilon^2$. This is the same as the exam story: the far-away students alone would use up at least $\varepsilon^2 P(A)$ of the variance budget $\sigma^2$.

**Is it tight?** Yes, for some distribution. Take $k \ge 1$ and let $X$ have mean 0 and three values:

$$
P(X = -k\sigma) = P(X = k\sigma) = \frac{1}{2k^2}, \qquad P(X = 0) = 1 - \frac{1}{k^2}
$$

The mean is 0 by symmetry. The variance is $2 \cdot \frac{1}{2k^2} \cdot k^2\sigma^2 = \sigma^2$. And $P(\lvert X \rvert \ge k\sigma) = \frac{1}{k^2}$ exactly. So **no better bound exists** if you know only $\mu$ and $\sigma$. (The simulation below uses $k = 2$, $\sigma = 1$.) A normal curve is far from this extreme case, which is why the bound looks so weak on it.

**Chebyshev for the sample mean.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent with the same mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and let $\bar{X}_n$ be their average. Variances of independent variables add ([6.6](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)), so

$$
E[\bar{X}_n] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}
$$

Chebyshev applied to $\bar{X}_n$ gives, for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2}
$$

**In words:** the average is far from the true mean with a probability that shrinks like $\frac{1}{n}$. As $n \to \infty$ the right side goes to 0 for every fixed $\varepsilon$. This is exactly the Weak Law of Large Numbers, which [17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md) states and uses.

<details>
<summary>A one-sided version: Cantelli's inequality (optional)</summary>

Chebyshev bounds both tails together. For one tail we can do better than half of it. **Cantelli's inequality** says, for $a \gt 0$:

$$P(X - \mu \ge a) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + a^2}$$

Proof. Pick any number $u \ge 0$. If $X - \mu \ge a$ then $X - \mu + u \ge a + u \gt 0$, so $(X - \mu + u)^2 \ge (a + u)^2$. Markov for the variable $(X - \mu + u)^2$ and $E[X - \mu] = 0$ give:

$$P(X - \mu \ge a) \le \frac{E[(X - \mu + u)^2]}{(a + u)^2} = \frac{\sigma^2 + u^2}{(a + u)^2}$$

Choose $u = \frac{\sigma^2}{a}$. Then the top is $\sigma^2 \frac{a^2 + \sigma^2}{a^2}$ and the bottom is $\frac{(a^2 + \sigma^2)^2}{a^2}$, so the ratio is $\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2 + \sigma^2}$. For example, with $\sigma = 10$ and $a = 20$ the bound is $\frac{100}{500} = 0.2$, better than the $0.25$ that Chebyshev allows for a single tail of an unknown distribution.

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Exam Marks

**Problem.** A class of 200 students has mean mark 60 and standard deviation 10. Nothing else is known. (a) At least how many students scored between 40 and 80 (that is, within 20 of the mean)? (b) At most how many scored 90 or more or 30 or less? (c) What does Markov say about the number who scored 80 or more?

**Solution.**

1. Here $\mu = 60$ and $\sigma = 10$.
2. (a) A distance of 20 is $k = 2$ standard deviations. So $P(\lvert X - 60 \rvert \lt 20) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$. That is at least $0.75 \times 200 = 150$ students. (Strictly between 40 and 80; the closed range also has at least 150.)
3. (b) A distance of 30 is $k = 3$. So $P(\lvert X - 60 \rvert \ge 30) \le \frac{1}{9}$, which is at most $\frac{200}{9} \approx 22.2$, so at most 22 students.
4. (c) The marks are not negative, so Markov applies: $P(X \ge 80) \le \frac{60}{80} = 0.75$, at most 150 students.

**Answer.** (a) At least 150 students. (b) At most 22 students. (c) Markov says at most 150, which is a much weaker statement than what Chebyshev gives: the students at 80 or more are part of the group outside the band, and Chebyshev says that group has at most $\frac{1}{4}$, so at most 50 students.

**Check.** Using the variance gave 50 instead of 150 for the high scorers. More information, a tighter bound.

### Example 2: How Many Flips to Estimate a Probability?

**Problem.** You want to estimate the chance $p$ that a coin (or a button, or an ad) gives a "success". You do $n$ independent trials and use the fraction of successes $\bar{X}_n$. How large must $n$ be so that $\bar{X}_n$ is within $0.05$ of $p$ with probability at least $0.95$, whatever $p$ is?

**Solution.**

1. Each trial is a Bernoulli variable with mean $p$ and variance $p(1 - p)$. The largest value of $p(1 - p)$ is $\frac{1}{4}$, at $p = \frac{1}{2}$. (Check: $p(1-p) = \frac{1}{4} - (p - \frac{1}{2})^2 \le \frac{1}{4}$.)
2. Chebyshev for the sample mean with $\varepsilon = 0.05$:

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge 0.05\big) \le \frac{p(1-p)}{n (0.05)^2} \le \frac{1/4}{n \cdot 0.0025} = \frac{100}{n}
$$

3. We want this to be at most $0.05$:

$$
\frac{100}{n} \le 0.05 \quad \implies \quad n \ge \frac{100}{0.05} = 2000
$$

**Answer.** $n = 2000$ trials are enough, for any $p$.

**Check.** This is a guarantee, but a very careful one. The exact chance of missing by $0.05$ with $n = 2000$ at $p = \frac{1}{2}$ is about $0.000008$, far below $0.05$ (see the simulation). Chebyshev uses only the variance, so it must protect against the worst possible distribution. Hoeffding's inequality ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)) shows that 738 trials are already enough.

### Example 3: Chebyshev Against Real Distributions

**Problem.** Compare the Chebyshev bound with the exact value of $P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge k\sigma)$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and for $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1), at $k = 2$ and $k = 3$.

**Solution.**

1. Chebyshev: $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$ for $k = 2$ and $\frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$ for $k = 3$.
2. Normal, from the 68-95-99.7 rule ([9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)): $P(\lvert Z \rvert \ge 2) \approx 0.0455$ and $P(\lvert Z \rvert \ge 3) \approx 0.0027$.
3. Exponential with rate 1 has $\mu = 1$ and $\sigma = 1$. The low side $X \le 1 - k$ is impossible for $k \ge 1$, so only the upper tail counts:

$$
P(\lvert X - 1 \rvert \ge k) = P(X \ge 1 + k) = e^{-(1+k)}
$$

This gives $e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$ for $k = 2$ and $e^{-4} \approx 0.0183$ for $k = 3$.

| $k$ | Chebyshev bound | Normal (exact) | Exponential (exact) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | $0.25$ | $0.0455$ | $0.0498$ |
| 3 | $0.111$ | $0.0027$ | $0.0183$ |

**Answer.** The bound is correct in both cases and is 5 to 40 times too large.

**Check.** The exponential has a heavier right tail than the normal, so it is closer to the bound than the normal. It is still far from tight, because only the three-point variable reaches the bound.

## Simulate It

We check four different distributions against the bound at $k = 2$, and then the sample-mean example (2000 flips) with its exact binomial value.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
k = 2.0

# Four very different distributions, with their exact mean and standard deviation.
# Chebyshev bounds P(|X - mu| >= 2 sigma) by 1/4 for all of them.
cases = {
    "Normal(0, 1)": (rng.normal(0, 1, n_trials), 0.0, 1.0),
    "Exp(1)": (rng.exponential(1.0, n_trials), 1.0, 1.0),
    "Unif(0, 1)": (rng.random(n_trials), 0.5, np.sqrt(1 / 12)),
    # Three-point variable: -2, 0, 2 with probabilities 1/8, 3/4, 1/8 (mean 0, sd 1).
    "Three-point": (rng.choice([-2.0, 0.0, 2.0], size=n_trials, p=[0.125, 0.75, 0.125]), 0.0, 1.0),
}

print(f"Chebyshev bound 1/k^2 = {1 / k**2:.4f}")
for name, (x, mu, sigma) in cases.items():
    tail = np.mean(np.abs(x - mu) >= k * sigma)
    print(f"{name:13s} P(|X - mu| >= 2 sigma) = {tail:.4f}")

# Heads in n = 2000 fair flips. Chebyshev: P(|Xbar - 0.5| >= eps) <= 0.25 / (n eps^2).
n_flips = 2000
heads = rng.binomial(n_flips, 0.5, size=1_000_000)
for eps in [0.05, 0.02]:
    gap = round(n_flips * eps)                     # |Xbar - 0.5| >= eps  means  |heads - 1000| >= gap
    exact = 2 * binom.sf(1000 + gap - 1, n_flips, 0.5)
    print(f"eps = {eps}: simulated {np.mean(np.abs(heads - 1000) >= gap):.6f}, "
          f"exact {exact:.6f}, Chebyshev {0.25 / (n_flips * eps**2):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Chebyshev bound 1/k^2 = 0.2500
Normal(0, 1)  P(|X - mu| >= 2 sigma) = 0.0457
Exp(1)        P(|X - mu| >= 2 sigma) = 0.0497
Unif(0, 1)    P(|X - mu| >= 2 sigma) = 0.0000
Three-point   P(|X - mu| >= 2 sigma) = 0.2499
eps = 0.05: simulated 0.000012, exact 0.000008, Chebyshev 0.0500
eps = 0.02: simulated 0.077902, exact 0.077287, Chebyshev 0.3125
```

The three-point variable reaches the bound ($0.25$), so the bound cannot be improved in general. The uniform variable never goes 2 standard deviations away (its sd is $0.289$, and $2 \times 0.289 = 0.577$ is more than the largest possible distance $0.5$). For the coin average, the bound is true but far too large.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing $\frac{1}{k}$ or $\frac{1}{\sigma^2}$.** The bound is $\frac{1}{k^2}$ when the distance is $k\sigma$, and $\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$ when the distance is $\varepsilon$. Mixing $\sigma$ with $\sigma^2$ gives wrong numbers.
- **Using it with $k \le 1$.** The bound is then 1 or more, which is no information. "Within 1 sd with probability at least 0" is true but empty.
- **Mixing it up with the 95% rule.** For a normal curve, 95% lies within 2 standard deviations. Chebyshev promises only 75% for a general distribution. To promise 95% in general you need $\frac{1}{k^2} = 0.05$, which is $k = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$ standard deviations.
- **Using it for one tail and dividing by 2.** Chebyshev bounds the **two** tails together. Half of it is correct only if the distribution is symmetric. For one tail without symmetry use Cantelli's inequality above.
- **Using it when the variance is infinite.** Heavy-tailed variables such as the Cauchy ([9.9](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md)) have no variance, so the right side is infinite and the bound is empty.

## Where It Is Used

- **Law of Large Numbers:** the proof of the weak law is one line: Chebyshev applied to the sample mean ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** if an estimator (a gradient, a Monte Carlo average, a test accuracy) is unbiased and its variance is $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, Chebyshev tells you how large $n$ must be for a safe estimate. It also gives the simple "median of means" trick for heavy-tailed data.
- **Computer science:** the second-moment method for randomized algorithms. Show that a count has a large mean and a small variance, and Chebyshev says it is close to its mean with high probability.
- **Quality control and risk:** a distribution-free promise such as "at least 89% of items are within 3 standard deviations" when you do not trust a normal model.
- **Everyday life:** a rough guide for how surprised to be. A value 5 standard deviations away from the mean happens at most 4% of the time for any distribution, and almost never for a bell curve.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A variable has mean 50 and standard deviation 5. Use Chebyshev to give a lower bound for $P(40 \lt X \lt 60)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The distance 10 is $k = \frac{10}{5} = 2$ standard deviations:

$$P(\lvert X - 50 \rvert \lt 10) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{2^2} = 0.75$$

So $P(40 \lt X \lt 60) \ge 0.75$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** The delivery time of a parcel has mean 30 minutes and variance 16 (minutes squared). Bound the probability that a delivery takes 10 or more minutes away from 30.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the $\varepsilon$ form with $\varepsilon = 10$ and $\sigma^2 = 16$:

$$P(\lvert X - 30 \rvert \ge 10) \le \frac{16}{100} = 0.16$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** How many standard deviations around the mean do you need so that Chebyshev guarantees at least 99% of the probability? Compare with the normal curve.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $1 - \frac{1}{k^2} = 0.99$, so $\frac{1}{k^2} = 0.01$ and $k = 10$. For a normal curve, only $k \approx 2.576$ standard deviations are needed. Chebyshev pays almost 4 times as many standard deviations for the safety of "any distribution".

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Estimating a probability $p$ from $n$ independent trials, how many trials guarantee (by Chebyshev, for any $p$) that $\bar{X}_n$ is within $0.02$ of $p$ with probability at least $0.95$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use $p(1-p) \le \frac{1}{4}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.02$:

$$P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge 0.02) \le \frac{1/4}{n \cdot 0.0004} = \frac{625}{n}$$

We need $\frac{625}{n} \le 0.05$, so $n \ge 12{,}500$. (Hoeffding in [16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md) needs only 4,612.)

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove: if $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0$, then $P(X = \mu) = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For any $\varepsilon \gt 0$, Chebyshev gives $P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{0}{\varepsilon^2} = 0$. The event $\lbrace X \ne \mu \rbrace$ is the union of the events $\lbrace \lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \frac{1}{m} \rbrace$ for $m = 1, 2, \dots$. By the union bound for a countable union ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)):

$$P(X \ne \mu) \le \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} P\left(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \tfrac{1}{m}\right) = 0$$

So $P(X = \mu) = 1$: a variable with no variance is a constant.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Let $X$ be the total of 12 rolls of a fair die. Compute the mean and variance, then compare the Chebyshev bound with a simulation for $P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge 10)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

One die has mean $3.5$ and variance $\frac{35}{12}$. For 12 independent rolls, $\mu = 42$ and $\sigma^2 = 12 \times \frac{35}{12} = 35$. So Chebyshev gives $\frac{35}{100} = 0.35$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
total = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(n_trials, 12)).sum(axis=1)   # total of 12 rolls
print(f"simulated P(|X - 42| >= 10) = {np.mean(np.abs(total - 42) >= 10):.4f}")
print(f"Chebyshev bound            = {35 / 100:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
simulated P(|X - 42| >= 10) = 0.1083
Chebyshev bound            = 0.3500
```

The true value is about 3 times smaller than the bound.

</details>

## Summary

- Chebyshev: $P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$, or $P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge k\sigma) \le \frac{1}{k^2}$. It needs only a finite mean and variance.
- The proof is Markov's inequality applied to $(X - \mu)^2$.
- It is tight only for a three-point variable, so for most real distributions it is very loose. Its strength is that it always holds.
- For a sample mean, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, so $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$. This proves the Weak Law of Large Numbers.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\big(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}}
$$
