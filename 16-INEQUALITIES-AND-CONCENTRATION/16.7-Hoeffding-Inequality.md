# 16.7 Hoeffding Inequality

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.7 Hoeffding Inequality

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 65 minutes · **Before this:** [16.6 Chernoff Bounds](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)

> **Big idea:** The average of $n$ independent numbers that all lie in an interval of length $b - a$ is farther than $\varepsilon$ from its mean with probability at most $2\exp\left(-\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2}\right)$, whatever the distribution.

---

## Why It Matters

You want to know the fraction of voters who support a candidate, the accuracy of a model, or the chance that a coin lands heads. You ask $n$ times and take the average. **Hoeffding's inequality** tells you exactly how large $n$ must be for the average to be within $\pm\varepsilon$ of the truth with probability 95%, and it needs only one fact: each answer lies in a known range. It is the most used concentration inequality in machine learning, because it gives a clean formula for the number of samples. Its proof is the Chernoff method ([16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)) plus one small lemma.

## Intuition

You flip a coin $n$ times to learn the chance $p$ of heads. With 10 flips, the fraction of heads can easily be 0.3 or 0.8, so you learn little. With 1,000 flips it is usually within 0.03 of the truth. With 100,000 flips it is within 0.003. Each step of 100 times more data makes the answer about 10 times more precise.

Hoeffding turns this into a promise with a formula. The key fact is that every answer is **bounded**: a coin flip is 0 or 1, a star rating is 1 to 5, a probability is in $[0, 1]$. A bounded number cannot be wildly large. So the sum of many such numbers is a sum of many small, independent pushes, and the pushes mostly cancel. The chance that they do not cancel decays like $e^{-n}$, the same exponential speed as in the Chernoff bound.

## Picture

```text
  Half-width eps that Hoeffding guarantees with 95% confidence,
  for the average of n values in [0, 1]:   eps = sqrt( ln(40) / (2n) )

  n          eps
  --------   ----------------------------------   -------
        10   ##################################   0.430
       100   ###########                          0.136
     1,000   ###                                  0.043
    10,000   #                                    0.014

  100 times more data  ->  10 times smaller eps
```

The width of the guarantee shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$: to be 10 times more precise you need 100 times more samples.

## Definition

**Hoeffding's inequality.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be **independent** random variables with $a_i \le X_i \le b_i$ for each $i$ (each has its own known range). Let $S = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$. Then for every $s \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(S - E[S] \ge s\big) \le \exp\left( -\frac{2s^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (b_i - a_i)^2} \right)
$$

and the same bound holds for $P(S - E[S] \le -s)$. Both tails together are at most twice that.

**The sample-mean form (the one you will use most).** If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent, all in $[a, b]$, and $\bar{X}_n$ is their average, then for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - E[\bar{X}_n] \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \le 2\exp\left( -\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2} \right)
$$

where:

- $n$ is the number of independent samples.
- $[a, b]$ is an interval that surely contains every sample, so $b - a$ is the range.
- $\varepsilon$ is how close we want the average to be to its mean.

**In words:** the chance that the average of $n$ bounded independent numbers is off by $\varepsilon$ or more shrinks exponentially in $n\varepsilon^2$.

**Sample size.** Set the right side equal to a failure probability $\delta$ (so confidence is $1 - \delta$) and solve for $n$:

$$
n \ge \frac{(b-a)^2 \ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2} \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad \varepsilon = (b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}
$$

**When to use it.** Use Hoeffding when (1) the samples are independent, (2) each one is bounded, and (3) you want a guarantee that holds for every $n$ and every distribution, without knowing the variance. Do not use it when the values are not bounded (use Chebyshev or a sub-Gaussian assumption), or when the events are very rare. For a rare event (small $p$) the Chernoff forms of [16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md) are much better, because Hoeffding ignores the variance.

## Why It Is True

The proof has two steps: a lemma about one bounded variable, and the Chernoff method for the sum.

**Step 1: Hoeffding's lemma.** If $E[Y] = 0$ and $a \le Y \le b$, then for every real $t$:

$$
E\big[e^{tY}\big] \le \exp\left( \frac{t^2 (b-a)^2}{8} \right)
$$

**In words:** a bounded variable with mean 0 has an MGF at most that of a normal variable with variance $\frac{(b-a)^2}{4}$. (We call this "sub-Gaussian": its tails are no heavier than a normal's.)

<details>
<summary>Full proof of the lemma (optional)</summary>

Since $E[Y] = 0$ we have $a \le 0 \le b$. If $a = b = 0$ the claim is trivial, so let $b \gt a$.

*Step A: replace the curve by its chord.* The function $y \mapsto e^{ty}$ is convex, so on $[a, b]$ it lies below its chord ([16.3](16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md), Practice 5):

$$e^{ty} \le \frac{b - y}{b - a} e^{ta} + \frac{y - a}{b - a} e^{tb}$$

Take the expectation, using $E[Y] = 0$, and put $p = \frac{-a}{b-a}$ (so $0 \le p \le 1$ and $1 - p = \frac{b}{b-a}$):

$$E[e^{tY}] \le \frac{b}{b-a} e^{ta} + \frac{-a}{b-a} e^{tb} = (1-p)e^{ta} + p e^{tb}$$

*Step B: write it as one exponential.* Let $u = t(b-a)$. Then $ta = -pu$, so the right side equals $e^{-pu}(1 - p + pe^u) = e^{\varphi(u)}$ with

$$\varphi(u) = -pu + \ln(1 - p + pe^u)$$

*Step C: Taylor's theorem.* We have $\varphi(0) = 0$. The first derivative is $\varphi'(u) = -p + q(u)$ with $q(u) = \frac{pe^u}{1 - p + pe^u}$, and $q$ lies between 0 and 1. So $\varphi'(0) = -p + p = 0$. Since $q'(u) = q(u)(1 - q(u))$, the second derivative is $\varphi''(u) = q(1-q) \le \frac{1}{4}$ (the largest value of $q(1-q)$ is at $q = \frac{1}{2}$). By Taylor's theorem with remainder there is a $\xi$ between 0 and $u$ with

$$\varphi(u) = \varphi(0) + \varphi'(0)u + \tfrac{1}{2}\varphi''(\xi)u^2 \le \frac{u^2}{8} = \frac{t^2(b-a)^2}{8}$$

Hence $E[e^{tY}] \le e^{\varphi(u)} \le e^{t^2(b-a)^2/8}$.

</details>

**Step 2: the Chernoff method.** Let $c_i = b_i - a_i$ and $C = \sum_i c_i^2$. Put $Y_i = X_i - E[X_i]$. Then $E[Y_i] = 0$ and $Y_i$ lies in an interval of length $c_i$. Fix $t \gt 0$. Chernoff's bound ([16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)), then independence (the MGF of a sum is a product, [15.3](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)), then the lemma:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(S - E[S] \ge s) &\le e^{-ts} \thinspace E\big[e^{t\sum_i Y_i}\big] && \text{(Chernoff, Markov for } e^{tX} \text{)} \\
&= e^{-ts} \prod_{i=1}^{n} E\big[e^{tY_i}\big] && \text{(independence)} \\
&\le e^{-ts} \prod_{i=1}^{n} e^{t^2 c_i^2 / 8} && \text{(Hoeffding's lemma)} \\
&= \exp\left( -ts + \frac{t^2 C}{8} \right) && \text{(add the exponents)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The exponent $-ts + \frac{t^2 C}{8}$ is smallest where its derivative $-s + \frac{tC}{4}$ is 0, that is $t = \frac{4s}{C}$. There its value is $-\frac{4s^2}{C} + \frac{16s^2}{C^2} \cdot \frac{C}{8} = -\frac{2s^2}{C}$. This proves the sum form. The lower tail is the same argument for $-X_i$.

**The sample-mean form.** Take all $b_i - a_i = b - a$, so $C = n(b-a)^2$, and take $s = n\varepsilon$ (since $\bar{X}_n - E[\bar{X}_n] \ge \varepsilon$ means $S - E[S] \ge n\varepsilon$):

$$
\exp\left( -\frac{2n^2\varepsilon^2}{n(b-a)^2} \right) = \exp\left( -\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2} \right)
$$

Add the two tails to get the factor 2.

**Compared with Chebyshev.** For a coin ($b - a = 1$), Chebyshev gives $\frac{1}{4n\varepsilon^2}$ at best, which falls like $\frac{1}{n}$. Hoeffding gives $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$, which falls like $e^{-n}$. The exponential wins by a huge margin once $n\varepsilon^2$ is moderately large.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: How Many Flips for Plus or Minus 0.02 With 95% Confidence?

**Problem.** You estimate the chance $p$ of heads of a coin by the fraction $\bar{X}_n$ of heads in $n$ independent flips. How large must $n$ be so that $\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \lt 0.02$ with probability at least $0.95$? Compare with Chebyshev and with the normal approximation.

**Solution.**

1. Each flip is in $[0, 1]$, so $b - a = 1$. We need confidence $0.95$, so $\delta = 0.05$, and $\varepsilon = 0.02$.
2. Hoeffding says the failure probability is at most $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$. We need:

$$
2e^{-2n(0.02)^2} \le 0.05 \quad \implies \quad e^{-0.0008n} \le 0.025 \quad \implies \quad 0.0008n \ge \ln 40
$$

3. Here $\ln 40 \approx 3.6889$, so:

$$
n \ge \frac{3.6889}{0.0008} = 4611.1 \quad \implies \quad n = 4612
$$

4. For comparison, Chebyshev needs $\frac{1}{4n\varepsilon^2} \le 0.05$, so $n \ge \frac{1}{4 \times 0.0004 \times 0.05} = 12{,}500$. The normal approximation (CLT, [17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) uses $1.96 \times \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{n}} \le 0.02$, so $n \ge \frac{1.96^2 \times 0.25}{0.0004} = 2401$.

| $\varepsilon$ | Chebyshev | Hoeffding | Normal approximation |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0.05$ | 2,000 | 738 | 385 |
| $0.02$ | 12,500 | 4,612 | 2,401 |
| $0.01$ | 50,000 | 18,445 | 9,604 |

**Answer.** $n = 4{,}612$ flips guarantee $\pm 0.02$ at 95% confidence, whatever the coin is.

**Check.** Hoeffding is between the two. It is a guarantee for every $n$, unlike the normal approximation (which is only approximate), and it is 2.7 times smaller than the Chebyshev number. It is about twice the normal value because it must cover the worst case. The simulation shows that the real failure rate at $n = 4{,}612$ is only about $0.0066$ for a fair coin.

### Example 2: How Accurate Is a Test Accuracy?

**Problem.** A classifier gets 90% of 2,000 independent test examples right. What range for the true accuracy can you give with 95% confidence?

**Solution.**

1. Each test example is a 0-or-1 variable (right or wrong), so $b - a = 1$. The sample mean is $\bar{X}_n = 0.90$ with $n = 2000$.
2. Use $\varepsilon = (b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}$ with $\delta = 0.05$:

$$
\varepsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\ln 40}{2 \times 2000}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.6889}{4000}} = \sqrt{0.00092222} \approx 0.0304
$$

3. With probability at least 0.95 the true accuracy is within $0.0304$ of the measured one:

$$
0.90 \pm 0.0304 \quad \implies \quad [0.8696, \thinspace 0.9304]
$$

**Answer.** The true accuracy is between about 87.0% and 93.0%, with 95% confidence.

**Check.** A model that improves on another by 1% cannot be distinguished from it on 2,000 test examples. With 20,000 examples the half-width would be $0.0096$, about 1%.

### Example 3: Average Star Rating

**Problem.** Customers give 1 to 5 stars. You want the average rating of a product to within $\pm 0.1$ stars, with 95% confidence, from independent ratings. How many ratings do you need?

**Solution.**

1. Each rating is in $[1, 5]$, so the range is $b - a = 4$. Here $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and $\delta = 0.05$.
2. Use the sample size formula:

$$
n \ge \frac{(b-a)^2 \ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2} = \frac{16 \times 3.6889}{2 \times 0.01} = \frac{59.02}{0.02} = 2951.1
$$

**Answer.** $n = 2{,}952$ ratings.

**Check.** The range enters as $(b-a)^2 = 16$: a range 4 times bigger needs 16 times more samples than for a 0-1 variable with the same $\varepsilon$. The simulation uses a made-up rating distribution (mean 3.6) and finds that the real failure rate at $n = 2{,}952$ is far below $0.05$.

## Simulate It

We check three things: Hoeffding against the exact value and Chebyshev for a fair coin at $n = 100$ and $n = 400$; the real failure rate at the promised sample size $n = 4{,}612$; and the star ratings of Example 3.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# 1. Fair coin, P(|Xbar - 0.5| >= 0.1): exact, Hoeffding 2 exp(-2 n eps^2), Chebyshev 0.25 / (n eps^2).
for n in [100, 400]:
    eps = 0.1
    gap = round(n * eps)                                # |Xbar - 0.5| >= eps  means  |heads - n/2| >= gap
    heads = rng.binomial(n, 0.5, size=n_trials)
    exact = 2 * binom.sf(n // 2 + gap - 1, n, 0.5)
    print(f"n={n}: sim {np.mean(np.abs(heads - n / 2) >= gap):.6f}  exact {exact:.6f}  "
          f"Hoeffding {2 * np.exp(-2 * n * eps**2):.6f}  Chebyshev {0.25 / (n * eps**2):.4f}")

# 2. The sample size n = 4612 promised for eps = 0.02 and 95%. How often does it really fail?
n, eps = 4612, 0.02
for p in [0.5, 0.2, 0.05]:
    heads = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_trials)
    print(f"n = {n}, p = {p}: failure rate {np.mean(np.abs(heads - n * p) >= n * eps):.5f} (promise: at most 0.05)")

# 3. Star ratings in {1,...,5}, range 4: n = 2952 should give +-0.1 with 95%.
values = np.arange(1, 6)
probs = np.array([0.1, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.3])             # mean rating 3.6
means = rng.multinomial(2952, probs, size=200_000) @ values / 2952
print(f"ratings, n = 2952: failure rate {np.mean(np.abs(means - 3.6) >= 0.1):.5f} (promise: at most 0.05)")
```

Output:

```text
n=100: sim 0.056735  exact 0.056888  Hoeffding 0.270671  Chebyshev 0.2500
n=400: sim 0.000076  exact 0.000074  Hoeffding 0.000671  Chebyshev 0.0625
n = 4612, p = 0.5: failure rate 0.00655 (promise: at most 0.05)
n = 4612, p = 0.2: failure rate 0.00069 (promise: at most 0.05)
n = 4612, p = 0.05: failure rate 0.00000 (promise: at most 0.05)
ratings, n = 2952: failure rate 0.00003 (promise: at most 0.05)
```

Look at the first two lines. At $n = 100$ the Hoeffding bound ($0.27$) is even a little worse than Chebyshev ($0.25$), because $n\varepsilon^2 = 1$ is small. At $n = 400$ Hoeffding ($0.00067$) is 93 times better than Chebyshev ($0.0625$). The promise is never broken, and it is conservative: for the fair coin the real failure rate is $0.0066$, and for an unfair coin with small $p$ it is almost 0, because Hoeffding ignores the variance.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the wrong range.** The formula uses $b - a$, the length of the range, squared. For a 0-1 variable it is 1. For ratings from 1 to 5 it is 4, not 5.
- **Mixing the sum and the mean form.** For the sum, the exponent is $-\frac{2s^2}{n(b-a)^2}$. For the average, put $s = n\varepsilon$ to get $-\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2}$. Do not divide by $n$ twice.
- **Forgetting the factor 2.** The bound $e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$ is for one side. The two-sided event $\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon$ gets $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$.
- **Using it without independence or without bounds.** The proof multiplies MGFs, which needs independence. Without bounded values the lemma fails. Variables with heavy tails need other tools ([9.13](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).
- **Thinking it is tight.** It is a guarantee, not an estimate of the actual error. In Example 1 the real failure rate is about 8 times below the promise. If the variance is small (a rare event), Hoeffding is far from the truth.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** test-set size and confidence intervals for accuracy (Example 2); generalization bounds for a finite set of models (with the union bound, [16.5](16.5-Union-Bound-Revisited.md)); A/B testing; and the analysis of stochastic gradient estimates and of bandit algorithms such as UCB, whose confidence bonus is the Hoeffding half-width.
- **Computer science:** randomized approximation algorithms that estimate a count by sampling; the number of samples a Monte Carlo method needs for a guaranteed error ([19.3](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md)).
- **Statistics:** distribution-free confidence intervals for a mean of bounded data.
- **Polls and surveys:** with 1,000 people the Hoeffding half-width at 95% is $0.043$ (the usual margin of error, from the normal approximation, is $0.031$).
- **Everyday life:** you can rely on an average of independent bounded things; a small sample can mislead, a large one almost never will.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A coin is flipped 1,000 times. Use Hoeffding to bound $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge 0.05)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Range 1, $n = 1000$, $\varepsilon = 0.05$:

$$P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge 0.05) \le 2e^{-2 \times 1000 \times 0.0025} = 2e^{-5} \approx 0.0135$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A poll asks 2,500 independent random people a yes-or-no question. What is the half-width of a 95% confidence interval from Hoeffding?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\varepsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\ln 40}{2 \times 2500}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.6889}{5000}} \approx 0.0272$$

So the answer is about $\pm 2.7$ percentage points.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** How many independent flips guarantee $\pm 0.03$ with 99% confidence?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\delta = 0.01$, $\varepsilon = 0.03$ and the range is 1:

$$n \ge \frac{\ln(2/0.01)}{2 \times 0.0009} = \frac{\ln 200}{0.0018} = \frac{5.2983}{0.0018} \approx 2943.5$$

So $n = 2{,}944$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Fifty independent variables each lie in $[0, 2]$. Bound $P(S - E[S] \ge 10)$ for their sum $S$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each range is $b_i - a_i = 2$, so $\sum (b_i - a_i)^2 = 50 \times 4 = 200$. With $s = 10$:

$$P(S - E[S] \ge 10) \le \exp\left( -\frac{2 \times 100}{200} \right) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent with $P(X_i = 1) = P(X_i = -1) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $S = \sum X_i$. Prove directly (without the lemma) that $P(S \ge s) \le e^{-s^2/(2n)}$. Compare with Hoeffding's formula.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For one variable, $E[e^{tX_i}] = \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}$ (the odd powers cancel). Since $(2k)! \ge 2^k k!$ for all $k \ge 0$, we get:

$$\frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2} \le \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{t^{2k}}{2^k k!} = e^{t^2/2}$$

By independence $E[e^{tS}] \le e^{nt^2/2}$, so $P(S \ge s) \le e^{-ts + nt^2/2}$. The best $t$ is $\frac{s}{n}$, which gives $e^{-s^2/(2n)}$. Hoeffding's formula with $b_i - a_i = 2$ gives $\exp\left(-\frac{2s^2}{4n}\right) = e^{-s^2/(2n)}$, the same. For $n = 100$ and $s = 20$ (that is, 60 or more heads) the bound is $e^{-2} \approx 0.135$, and the exact value is $0.0284$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** For $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and 95% confidence, Hoeffding says that $n = 738$ flips are enough. Simulate fair-coin averages with $n = 738$ and find the real failure rate.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, eps = 738, 0.05                                      # n from  2 exp(-2 n eps^2) <= 0.05
heads = rng.binomial(n, 0.5, size=1_000_000)
fail = np.mean(np.abs(heads - n * 0.5) >= n * eps)
print(f"hoeffding bound at n = {n}: {2 * np.exp(-2 * n * eps**2):.4f}")
print(f"simulated failure rate:    {fail:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
hoeffding bound at n = 738: 0.0499
simulated failure rate:    0.0071
```

The real failure rate $0.0071$ is much below the guaranteed $0.05$, as expected for the worst-case guarantee.

</details>

## Summary

- Hoeffding: independent $X_i \in [a, b]$ give $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le 2\exp\left(-\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2}\right)$. For a sum with different ranges, the exponent is $-\frac{2s^2}{\sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2}$.
- Proof: Hoeffding's lemma ($E[e^{tY}] \le e^{t^2(b-a)^2/8}$ for mean-zero bounded $Y$) plus the Chernoff method.
- Sample size: $n \ge \frac{(b-a)^2 \ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2}$. For $\pm 0.02$ at 95% with coin flips this is 4,612.
- It is distribution-free but conservative: it ignores the variance, so it is loose for rare events. With the union bound it handles many quantities at once.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \le 2\exp\left( -\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2} \right)}
$$
