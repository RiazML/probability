# 16.3 Jensen Inequality

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.3 Jensen Inequality

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [16.2 Chebyshev Inequality](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)

> **Big idea:** For a convex function $f$, the average of $f(X)$ is at least $f$ of the average, $f(E[X]) \le E[f(X)]$, and for a concave function the inequality flips.

---

## Why It Matters

A very common mistake is to write $E[f(X)] = f(E[X])$. It is true when $f$ is a straight line ([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)), and false for almost every other function ([6.3](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.3-LOTUS.md)). **Jensen's inequality** says in which direction it is wrong. This one idea proves that variance is never negative, that the geometric mean is below the arithmetic mean, that the KL divergence of machine learning is never negative, and that the MGF ([15.1](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)) is always above $e^{tE[X]}$. It also explains why a bet with a good average can still ruin you.

## Intuition

A machine cuts square tiles. The side is either 2 cm or 4 cm, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. The average side is 3 cm. So the "typical" tile has area $3^2 = 9$ square cm. But the areas are $2^2 = 4$ and $4^2 = 16$, and their average is $\frac{4 + 16}{2} = 10$.

The average area (10) is bigger than the area of the average side (9). Why? The big tile is much bigger in area than the small tile is smaller. Squaring **rewards spread**: stretching the sides apart adds more on the high end than it takes away on the low end.

A function with this property bends upward, like a smile. We call it **convex**. For such functions, "average then apply" is smaller than "apply then average". For a function that bends downward, like $\ln x$ or $\sqrt{x}$, the order flips. Averaging the square roots of 4 and 16 gives $\frac{2 + 4}{2} = 3$, which is less than $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$.

## Picture

```text
  f(x) = x^2
  |                                      **
  |                                     **
  |                                   ***
  |                                 *B*
  |                              .***
  |                           ..***
  |                        ...***
  |                      .M****
  |                   ...*F*
  |                ..****
  |             ..****
  |         ***A**
  | *********
  +-----------------------------------------
               1          2          3   x

  X is 1 or 3 with probability 1/2 each, so E[X] = 2.
  A = (1, f(1)), B = (3, f(3)).  The dots are the chord from A to B.
  F = (2, f(2)) = (2, 4) is on the curve:  f(E[X]) = 4.
  M = (2, 5) is on the chord:  E[f(X)] = (1 + 9) / 2 = 5.
```

The chord of a convex curve is always above the curve, so the point $M$ is above the point $F$. The gap here is $5 - 4 = 1$, which is $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

## Definition

A function $f$ is **convex** on an interval $I$ if every chord lies above the graph. That is, for all $x, y$ in $I$ and every $\lambda$ between 0 and 1:

$$
f\big(\lambda x + (1 - \lambda) y\big) \le \lambda f(x) + (1 - \lambda) f(y)
$$

It is **strictly convex** if the inequality is strict whenever $x \ne y$ and $0 \lt \lambda \lt 1$. A function $f$ is **concave** if $-f$ is convex (it bends downward). If $f$ has a second derivative, the test is simple: $f'' \ge 0$ everywhere means convex, and $f'' \le 0$ means concave.

| Function | On | $f''$ | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x^2$, $x^4$ | all $x$ | $2$, $12x^2$ | convex |
| $\lvert x \rvert$ | all $x$ | (a corner) | convex |
| $e^{tx}$ | all $x$ | $t^2 e^{tx}$ | convex |
| $\frac{1}{x}$ | $x \gt 0$ | $\frac{2}{x^3}$ | convex |
| $x \ln x$ | $x \gt 0$ | $\frac{1}{x}$ | convex |
| $\ln x$ | $x \gt 0$ | $-\frac{1}{x^2}$ | concave |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $x \ge 0$ | $-\frac{1}{4} x^{-3/2}$ | concave |

**Jensen's inequality.** Let $X$ be a random variable with values in $I$, with finite mean, and let $f$ be convex on $I$. Then

$$
f\big(E[X]\big) \le E\big[f(X)\big]
$$

If $f$ is concave, the inequality is reversed: $f(E[X]) \ge E[f(X)]$. If $f$ is strictly convex, equality holds only when $X$ is a constant.

**In words:** for a smile-shaped function, the function of the average is at most the average of the function.

> **Tip:** To remember the direction, use $f(x) = x^2$. We always have $E[X^2] \ge (E[X])^2$, because the difference is the variance. So convex means $E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X])$.

## Why It Is True

**The two-point case is the definition.** Let $X = x$ with probability $\lambda$ and $X = y$ with probability $1 - \lambda$. Then $E[X] = \lambda x + (1 - \lambda) y$ and $E[f(X)] = \lambda f(x) + (1 - \lambda) f(y)$. The definition of convexity says exactly $f(E[X]) \le E[f(X)]$. (For $n$ values, the same step repeated gives Jensen for any finite distribution.)

**The general proof, with a tangent line.** Let $f$ be convex and differentiable, and put $\mu = E[X]$. The tangent line of $f$ at $\mu$ is $\ell(x) = f(\mu) + f'(\mu)(x - \mu)$. We first show that a convex curve lies **above its tangent lines**:

$$
f(x) \ge f(\mu) + f'(\mu)(x - \mu) \qquad \text{for every } x
$$

To see this, define $g(x) = f(x) - f(\mu) - f'(\mu)(x - \mu)$. Then $g(\mu) = 0$ and $g'(x) = f'(x) - f'(\mu)$. For a convex $f$ the slope $f'$ never decreases. So $g' \le 0$ to the left of $\mu$ and $g' \ge 0$ to the right of $\mu$. This means $g$ goes down until $\mu$ and up after it, so its smallest value is $g(\mu) = 0$. Hence $g(x) \ge 0$ everywhere.

Now replace $x$ by the random variable $X$ and take the expectation. The expectation of a sum splits, and $E[X - \mu] = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[f(X)] &\ge E\big[f(\mu) + f'(\mu)(X - \mu)\big] && \text{(tangent inequality, averaged)} \\
&= f(\mu) + f'(\mu) \thinspace E[X - \mu] && \text{(linearity)} \\
&= f(\mu) && \text{(} E[X - \mu] = 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is $E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X])$. For a convex function with a corner (such as $\lvert x \rvert$) the same proof works with any supporting line, a line that touches the graph at $\mu$ and stays below it, in place of the tangent.

**The equality case.** If $f$ is strictly convex, then $g(x) \gt 0$ for every $x \ne \mu$. Then $E[g(X)] = 0$ with $g(X) \ge 0$ forces $g(X) = 0$ almost surely ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md), Practice 4), so $X = \mu$ almost surely: $X$ is a constant.

**Five quick consequences.** Each one is Jensen with a different $f$:

1. $f(x) = x^2$ gives $E[X^2] \ge (E[X])^2$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 \ge 0$.
2. $f(x) = \lvert x \rvert$ gives $\lvert E[X] \rvert \le E[\lvert X \rvert]$.
3. $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ for $X \gt 0$ gives $E\left[\frac{1}{X}\right] \ge \frac{1}{E[X]}$.
4. $f(x) = -\ln x$ for $X \gt 0$ gives $E[\ln X] \le \ln E[X]$. For equally likely positive numbers $x_1, \dots, x_n$ this reads $\ln \frac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n} \ge \frac{\ln x_1 + \cdots + \ln x_n}{n}$, so $\sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdots x_n} \le \frac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n}$: the **geometric mean is at most the arithmetic mean**.
5. $f(x) = e^{tx}$ gives $M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}] \ge e^{tE[X]}$, the fact noted in [15.1](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Average Area and the Variance

**Problem.** The side $S$ of a tile is 2 or 4, with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Compare $E[S^2]$ with $(E[S])^2$ and explain the gap.

**Solution.**

1. $E[S] = \frac{2 + 4}{2} = 3$, so $(E[S])^2 = 9$.
2. The area $S^2$ is 4 or 16, so $E[S^2] = \frac{4 + 16}{2} = 10$.
3. The function $x^2$ is convex, so Jensen predicts $9 \le 10$. It is true.
4. The gap is $10 - 9 = 1$. The variance of $S$ is $\frac{(2-3)^2 + (4-3)^2}{2} = 1$.

**Answer.** $E[S^2] = 10 \gt 9 = (E[S])^2$, and the gap is exactly $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 1$.

**Check.** For $f(x) = x^2$ the Jensen gap is always the variance, so it is 0 only when $S$ is constant. This matches the equality case.

### Example 2: KL Divergence Is Never Negative

**Problem.** Two probability distributions on the same two outcomes are $p = (0.5, 0.5)$ and $q = (0.9, 0.1)$. The **KL divergence** (a measure of how different $q$ is from $p$, used as a loss in machine learning) is $D(p \Vert q) = \sum_x p(x) \ln \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$. Compute it, and show with Jensen that it is never negative.

**Solution.**

1. Compute the value:

$$
D(p \Vert q) = 0.5 \ln \frac{0.5}{0.9} + 0.5 \ln \frac{0.5}{0.1} = 0.5(-0.5878) + 0.5(1.6094) \approx 0.5108
$$

2. For the proof, write $D(p \Vert q) = E_p\big[-\ln \frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\big]$, an average over $X \sim p$. The function $-\ln$ is convex, so Jensen gives:

$$
D(p \Vert q) \ge -\ln E_p\left[\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right] = -\ln \sum_x p(x) \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} = -\ln \sum_x q(x) = -\ln 1 = 0
$$

3. Check with the numbers: $\frac{q}{p}$ is $1.8$ and $0.2$, so $E_p\left[\frac{q}{p}\right] = 0.5(1.8) + 0.5(0.2) = 1$, and $-\ln 1 = 0 \le 0.5108$.

**Answer.** $D(p \Vert q) \approx 0.5108 \ge 0$. It is zero only when $q = p$ (strict convexity).

**Check.** The value $0.5108$ is positive because $q$ is quite different from $p$. This fact is why minimizing cross-entropy loss pushes a model towards the true distribution.

### Example 3: A Bet With a Good Average That Ruins You

**Problem.** Each round, your money is multiplied by $1.5$ (a gain of 50%) or by $0.6$ (a loss of 40%), each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, independently. You play 100 rounds. What is the expected final wealth, and what does a typical player end with?

**Solution.**

1. One round: $E[\text{factor}] = 0.5(1.5) + 0.5(0.6) = 1.05$. Rounds are independent, so the expected product is the product of the means: $E[\text{wealth after 100 rounds}] = 1.05^{100} \approx 131.5$ times the start.
2. Now take logs. $\ln$ is concave, so $E[\ln \text{factor}] \le \ln E[\text{factor}]$. The numbers are:

$$
E[\ln \text{factor}] = 0.5 \ln 1.5 + 0.5 \ln 0.6 = 0.5(0.4055) + 0.5(-0.5108) \approx -0.0527
$$

3. The log of wealth is a sum of 100 independent logs, so a typical (median) player has about $e^{100 \times (-0.0527)} = e^{-5.27} \approx 0.0052$ times the start. That is a loss of about 99.5%.
4. To end above the start, you need at least 56 gains out of 100 (since $56 \ln 1.5 + 44 \ln 0.6 \gt 0$ but 55 gains is not enough). The chance of 56 or more gains is about $0.136$.

**Answer.** The expected wealth is 131.5 times the start, but the typical wealth is 0.005 times the start. Only about 13.6% of players are above their starting money.

**Check.** There is no contradiction. The mean is pulled up by a few extremely lucky players. This is Jensen for the concave $\ln$: the average of the logs is far below the log of the average. The simulation below agrees. [22.12](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md) and [22.14](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md) return to this idea.

## Simulate It

We take $X \sim \text{Unif}(1, 5)$ (mean 3) and compare $f(E[X])$ with $E[f(X)]$ for five functions. Then we simulate the bet of Example 3.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# X ~ Unif(1, 5): mean 3. Jensen compares E[f(X)] with f(E[X]).
x = rng.uniform(1, 5, n_trials)
print("f(x)       f(E[X])   E[f(X)] simulated   E[f(X)] exact   shape")
rows = [
    ("x^2    ", 3.0**2, np.mean(x**2), 124 / 12, "convex"),
    ("1/x    ", 1 / 3.0, np.mean(1 / x), np.log(5) / 4, "convex"),
    ("exp(x) ", np.exp(3.0), np.mean(np.exp(x)), (np.exp(5) - np.exp(1)) / 4, "convex"),
    ("ln x   ", np.log(3.0), np.mean(np.log(x)), (5 * np.log(5) - 4) / 4, "concave"),
    ("sqrt(x)", np.sqrt(3.0), np.mean(np.sqrt(x)), (2 / 3) * (5**1.5 - 1) / 4, "concave"),
]
for name, f_of_mean, mean_of_f, exact, shape in rows:
    print(f"{name}   {f_of_mean:8.4f}   {mean_of_f:12.4f}   {exact:14.4f}      {shape}")

# Wealth is multiplied by 1.5 or by 0.6, each with probability 1/2, in 100 rounds.
ups = rng.binomial(100, 0.5, size=n_trials)            # number of rounds that gain
wealth = 1.5**ups * 0.6 ** (100 - ups)
print(f"mean of one round's factor: {0.5 * 1.5 + 0.5 * 0.6:.2f}, so exact mean wealth {1.05**100:.1f}")
print(f"median final wealth {np.median(wealth):.4f}, share of paths above 1: {np.mean(wealth > 1):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
f(x)       f(E[X])   E[f(X)] simulated   E[f(X)] exact   shape
x^2         9.0000        10.3336          10.3333      convex
1/x         0.3333         0.4023           0.4024      convex
exp(x)     20.0855        36.4260          36.4237      convex
ln x        1.0986         1.0119           1.0118      concave
sqrt(x)     1.7321         1.6968           1.6967      concave
mean of one round's factor: 1.05, so exact mean wealth 131.5
median final wealth 0.0052, share of paths above 1: 0.1355
```

For the three convex functions $E[f(X)]$ is above $f(E[X])$, and for the two concave functions it is below. The simulated and exact values agree to about 3 or 4 digits.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using $E[f(X)] = f(E[X])$.** This holds only for straight lines $f(x) = ax + b$ (or when $X$ is constant). For example $E[X^2] \ne (E[X])^2$ whenever $X$ has any spread.
- **Getting the direction wrong.** Convex (smile) gives $f(E[X]) \le E[f(X)]$. Concave (frown) gives the opposite. Check with $x^2$ if you forget.
- **Using it on a function that is not convex on the whole range of $X$.** The function $\frac{1}{x}$ is convex only for $x \gt 0$, so $E\left[\frac{1}{X}\right] \ge \frac{1}{E[X]}$ needs $X \gt 0$. Likewise $x^3$ is convex only for $x \ge 0$, and $\sin x$ is neither convex nor concave.
- **Thinking it is a strict inequality always.** For a constant $X$ there is equality. For a function that is straight on part of its graph, equality can also hold.
- **Forgetting the log is concave.** $E[\ln X] \le \ln E[X]$. Average log returns are smaller than the log of the average return, which is why "average gain" can be misleading.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** KL divergence and cross-entropy are never negative (Example 2). The evidence lower bound in variational inference and the EM algorithm are Jensen for $\ln$: $\ln E[\cdot] \ge E[\ln \cdot]$. Averaging the predictions of an ensemble never increases the expected loss when the loss is convex.
- **Probability and statistics:** variance is non-negative, and $E[e^{tX}] \ge e^{tE[X]}$. The Chernoff bound ([16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)) starts from the MGF. The log-normal mean is above the median ([9.8](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.8-Log-Normal.md)).
- **Finance and economics:** a concave utility function means risk aversion: you prefer a sure 100 taka to a coin flip between 0 and 200 taka. Growth of money over time is governed by the average log, not the average factor (Example 3).
- **Information theory:** entropy is at most $\ln n$ for $n$ outcomes, and mutual information is never negative.
- **Everyday life:** the average of a quantity and the average of its square, or its inverse, are not the same. The average of 60 km/h and 40 km/h is 50, but a trip of equal distances at those speeds has an average speed of $\frac{2}{1/60 + 1/40} = 48$ km/h, because $\frac{1}{x}$ is convex.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is 1 or 9 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Compute $\sqrt{E[X]}$ and $E[\sqrt{X}]$. Which is bigger, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $E[X] = 5$, so $\sqrt{E[X]} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$. Also:

$$E[\sqrt{X}] = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(3) = 2$$

So $\sqrt{E[X]} \gt E[\sqrt{X}]$. The square root is concave, so Jensen gives $E[\sqrt{X}] \le \sqrt{E[X]}$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For each function, say whether $E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X])$ or $\le$, for any random variable $X$: (a) $f(x) = e^{-x}$, (b) $f(x) = -x^2$, (c) $f(x) = \lvert x - 3 \rvert$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $f'' = e^{-x} \gt 0$, convex, so $E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X])$. (b) $f'' = -2 \lt 0$, concave, so $E[f(X)] \le f(E[X])$. (c) The absolute value of a straight line is convex, so $E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X])$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X$ be 1, 2 or 4 with probability $\frac{1}{3}$ each. Compute $E\left[\frac{1}{X}\right]$ and $\frac{1}{E[X]}$, and check Jensen.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $E[X] = \frac{1 + 2 + 4}{3} = \frac{7}{3}$, so $\frac{1}{E[X]} = \frac{3}{7} \approx 0.4286$. Also:

$$E\left[\frac{1}{X}\right] = \frac{1}{3}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{7}{4} = \frac{7}{12} \approx 0.5833$$

Since $0.5833 \ge 0.4286$, Jensen's inequality for the convex function $\frac{1}{x}$ holds.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Use Jensen with $f = \ln$ to prove that $\sqrt{ab} \le \frac{a + b}{2}$ for $a, b \gt 0$. Check it for $a = 2$, $b = 8$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $X = a$ or $b$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. The function $\ln$ is concave, so $E[\ln X] \le \ln E[X]$:

$$\frac{\ln a + \ln b}{2} \le \ln \frac{a + b}{2}$$

The left side is $\ln \sqrt{ab}$. Apply $\exp$ (which keeps order) to both sides: $\sqrt{ab} \le \frac{a + b}{2}$. For $a = 2$ and $b = 8$: $\sqrt{16} = 4 \le 5$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $f$ be convex and let $X$ take values in $[a, b]$. Prove that $E[f(X)] \le \frac{(b - E[X]) f(a) + (E[X] - a) f(b)}{b - a}$. (This reverse bound is used in the proof of Hoeffding's lemma in [16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md).)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $x$ in $[a, b]$ write $x = \lambda a + (1 - \lambda) b$ with $\lambda = \frac{b - x}{b - a}$. Convexity says the graph is below the chord:

$$f(x) \le \frac{b - x}{b - a} f(a) + \frac{x - a}{b - a} f(b)$$

This holds for every value of $X$, so take the expectation. The right side is a straight line in $x$, so $E[x]$ can replace $x$:

$$E[f(X)] \le \frac{b - E[X]}{b - a} f(a) + \frac{E[X] - a}{b - a} f(b)$$

This is the claim. Together with Jensen, $E[f(X)]$ is squeezed between the curve at the mean and the chord at the mean.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Let $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$. Estimate $E[\ln X]$ and $\ln E[X]$ by simulation and check Jensen. (The exact value of $E[\ln X]$ is $-\gamma \approx -0.5772$, where $\gamma$ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=1_000_000)       # X ~ Exp(1), E[X] = 1
print(f"E[ln X] simulated = {np.mean(np.log(x)):.4f}   (exact: -0.5772)")
print(f"ln E[X] simulated = {np.log(np.mean(x)):.4f}   (exact: 0)")
```

Output:

```text
E[ln X] simulated = -0.5771   (exact: -0.5772)
ln E[X] simulated = -0.0002   (exact: 0)
```

So $E[\ln X] \approx -0.577 \le 0 = \ln E[X]$, as Jensen says for the concave $\ln$.

</details>

## Summary

- Convex (smile-shaped) $f$: $f(E[X]) \le E[f(X)]$. Concave (frown-shaped) $f$: the inequality flips. Equality holds for constants (strictly convex $f$) or straight lines.
- The proof: a convex curve lies above its tangent line at $E[X]$, and averaging the tangent line gives exactly $f(E[X])$.
- Special cases: $\operatorname{Var}(X) \ge 0$, $E[\ln X] \le \ln E[X]$ (geometric mean $\le$ arithmetic mean), $M_X(t) \ge e^{tE[X]}$, and $D(p \Vert q) \ge 0$.
- The average of a gamble can be good while its typical result is bad: the average log is below the log of the average.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f \text{ convex} \implies f\big(E[X]\big) \le E\big[f(X)\big]}
$$
