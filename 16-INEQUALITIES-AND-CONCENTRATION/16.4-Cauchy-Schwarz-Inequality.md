# 16.4 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.4 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [16.3 Jensen Inequality](16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)

> **Big idea:** The average of a product is limited by the sizes of its two factors, $\left(E[XY]\right)^2 \le E[X^2] \thinspace E[Y^2]$, and this is the reason why a correlation can never be larger than 1.

---

## Why It Matters

In [11.2](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) we said that the correlation coefficient $\rho$ always lies between $-1$ and $1$, and we promised a proof later. This is it. **The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality** also gives a quick test for impossible numbers: if someone gives you a covariance that is too large for the two variances, something is wrong. And it supplies a lower bound for the chance that a non-negative variable is positive, which is a main tool for the second-moment method in randomized algorithms.

## Intuition

You know the dot product of two arrows in the plane: $u \cdot v = \lvert u \rvert \lvert v \rvert \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between them. Since $\cos\theta$ is never above 1 in size, we get $\lvert u \cdot v \rvert \le \lvert u \rvert \lvert v \rvert$. Two arrows can have a big dot product only when both are long **and** point in nearly the same direction.

Random variables behave in the same way. Think of $X$ and $Y$ as arrows, and define their "dot product" as the average of the product, $E[XY]$. The "length" of $X$ is $\sqrt{E[X^2]}$. Then the same rule must hold: the average of $XY$ cannot be bigger in size than the lengths multiplied. Two variables have a large $E[XY]$ only when both are large and move together.

Here is a quick feeling for it. Let $X$ be your daily study hours and $Y$ your daily exam-practice hours. If both are about 2 hours, then $E[XY]$ is about 4. It can never be 100: a product of two numbers around 2 is not going to average 100.

The inequality is an equality only when the two arrows are parallel (angle $0$ or $180$ degrees): one variable is a constant multiple of the other.

## Definition

**Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.** For any random variables $X$ and $Y$ with finite $E[X^2]$ and $E[Y^2]$:

$$
\big(E[XY]\big)^2 \le E[X^2] \thinspace E[Y^2]
$$

or, taking square roots, $\lvert E[XY] \rvert \le \sqrt{E[X^2] \thinspace E[Y^2]}$. Equality holds if and only if one variable is a constant multiple of the other: $P(X = cY) = 1$ for some number $c$, or $P(Y = 0) = 1$.

**In words:** the size of the average of $XY$ is at most the square root of the average of $X^2$ times the average of $Y^2$.

Three results follow by choosing $X$ and $Y$ in a clever way:

- **Covariance and correlation.** Apply it to the centered variables $X - \mu_X$ and $Y - \mu_Y$. Then $E[(X - \mu_X)^2] = \sigma_X^2$ and $E[(Y - \mu_Y)^2] = \sigma_Y^2$, so:

$$
\lvert \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \rvert \le \sigma_X \sigma_Y \qquad \text{and so} \qquad -1 \le \rho \le 1
$$

- **Mean absolute value.** Take $Y = 1$. Then $\big(E[\lvert X \rvert]\big)^2 \le E[X^2]$.
- **A positive chance.** If $X \ge 0$, then $P(X \gt 0) \ge \frac{(E[X])^2}{E[X^2]}$. (Proved below.)

## Why It Is True

**The quadratic proof.** For any real number $t$, the variable $(X - tY)^2$ is never negative, so its mean is never negative. Expand the square and use linearity:

$$
0 \le E\big[(X - tY)^2\big] = E[X^2] - 2t \thinspace E[XY] + t^2 E[Y^2] \qquad \text{for every real } t
$$

The right side is a quadratic in $t$ that never goes below 0. There are two cases.

- **Case $E[Y^2] = 0$.** Then $Y = 0$ with probability 1 ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md), Practice 4), so $XY = 0$, and both sides of the inequality are 0.
- **Case $E[Y^2] \gt 0$.** Choose the value of $t$ that makes the quadratic smallest, $t = \frac{E[XY]}{E[Y^2]}$. Substitute it:

$$
0 \le E[X^2] - 2 \frac{(E[XY])^2}{E[Y^2]} + \frac{(E[XY])^2}{E[Y^2]} = E[X^2] - \frac{(E[XY])^2}{E[Y^2]}
$$

Multiply by $E[Y^2] \gt 0$ and move the last term: $(E[XY])^2 \le E[X^2] E[Y^2]$. (Another way to say the same: a quadratic that is never negative has no two different roots, so its discriminant $4(E[XY])^2 - 4E[X^2]E[Y^2]$ is at most 0.)

**The equality case.** Equality means the quadratic touches 0 at $t = \frac{E[XY]}{E[Y^2]}$, so $E[(X - tY)^2] = 0$ for that $t$. A non-negative variable with mean 0 is 0 almost surely, so $X = tY$ with probability 1. Conversely, if $X = cY$ then both sides equal $c^2 (E[Y^2])^2$.

**Correlation.** For the centered variables the inequality gives $(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y))^2 \le \sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2$. Dividing by $\sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2$ (when both are positive) gives $\rho^2 \le 1$. Equality, $\rho = \pm 1$, holds exactly when $Y - \mu_Y$ is a multiple of $X - \mu_X$, which means $Y = aX + b$: a perfect straight-line relation.

**A bound on the sum.** Since $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)) and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \le \sigma_X \sigma_Y$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) \le (\sigma_X + \sigma_Y)^2, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \sigma_{X+Y} \le \sigma_X + \sigma_Y
$$

**The second-moment bound.** Let $X \ge 0$ and let $I$ be the indicator of the event $\lbrace X \gt 0 \rbrace$. Then $X = X \cdot I$ (when $X = 0$ both sides are 0), and $I^2 = I$. Apply Cauchy-Schwarz to $X$ and $I$:

$$
\big(E[X]\big)^2 = \big(E[X \cdot I]\big)^2 \le E[X^2] \thinspace E[I^2] = E[X^2] \thinspace P(X \gt 0)
$$

Divide by $E[X^2]$ to get $P(X \gt 0) \ge \frac{(E[X])^2}{E[X^2]}$.

**In words:** if the mean is big compared with the second moment, $X$ cannot be zero most of the time.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Die and Its Square

**Problem.** Let $X$ be the roll of a fair die and $Y = X^2$. Check the inequality $\left(E[XY]\right)^2 \le E[X^2]E[Y^2]$ and say how close it is to an equality.

**Solution.**

1. $E[XY] = E[X^3] = \frac{1 + 8 + 27 + 64 + 125 + 216}{6} = \frac{441}{6} = 73.5$.
2. $E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.167$.
3. $E[Y^2] = E[X^4] = \frac{1 + 16 + 81 + 256 + 625 + 1296}{6} = \frac{2275}{6} \approx 379.167$.
4. Compare the two sides:

$$
(E[XY])^2 = 73.5^2 = 5402.25 \qquad \text{and} \qquad E[X^2] E[Y^2] = \frac{91}{6} \cdot \frac{2275}{6} \approx 5750.69
$$

**Answer.** $5402.25 \le 5750.69$. The ratio is $0.939$, so the "cosine of the angle" is $\sqrt{0.939} \approx 0.969$.

**Check.** $Y = X^2$ is almost a straight-line function of $X$ on the numbers 1 to 6, so the angle between them is small and the inequality is nearly tight. If $Y$ were exactly $cX$ the two sides would be equal.

### Example 2: Is This Covariance Possible?

**Problem.** A data sheet says $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 9$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 7$. Can this be right? What if the covariance is $5$?

**Solution.**

1. The standard deviations are $\sigma_X = 2$ and $\sigma_Y = 3$, so Cauchy-Schwarz says $\lvert \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \rvert \le 2 \times 3 = 6$.
2. A covariance of $7$ is larger than $6$, so it is **impossible**. (The correlation would be $\frac{7}{6} \gt 1$.)
3. A covariance of $5$ is allowed. Then $\rho = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833$.

**Answer.** $7$ is impossible; $5$ is possible with $\rho \approx 0.833$.

**Check.** In terms of a covariance matrix ([11.5](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.5-Random-Vectors-and-Covariance-Matrix.md)), the matrix with rows $(4, 7)$ and $(7, 9)$ has determinant $4 \cdot 9 - 7^2 = -13 \lt 0$, which a covariance matrix can never have. The same test, $\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y) - \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)^2 \ge 0$, is Cauchy-Schwarz written out. It is a useful sanity check when you build a covariance matrix by hand or by a program.

### Example 3: Can a Count Be Zero Most of the Time?

**Problem.** Flip three fair coins and let $X$ be the number of heads. Use the second-moment bound to give a lower bound for $P(X \gt 0)$, and compare with the exact value.

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{Bin}(3, \frac{1}{2})$, so $E[X] = 1.5$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 0.75$.
2. The second moment is $E[X^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (E[X])^2 = 0.75 + 2.25 = 3$.
3. The bound:

$$
P(X \gt 0) \ge \frac{(E[X])^2}{E[X^2]} = \frac{2.25}{3} = 0.75
$$

4. The exact value: $P(X \gt 0) = 1 - P(\text{no heads}) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = 0.875$.

**Answer.** The bound says at least $0.75$, and the exact value is $0.875$.

**Check.** The bound used only $E[X]$ and $E[X^2]$, not the distribution. In large random structures (random graphs, random hash tables) these two numbers are often easy to compute even when the distribution is not, and then this bound proves that something happens with positive probability.

## Simulate It

We check the die example, try 2,000 random pairs of dependent variables to see that $\lvert \rho \rvert$ never goes above 1, and check the second-moment bound for $X \sim \text{Pois}(1)$ (rate 1, mean 1, and $E[X^2] = 2$).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# 1. A die X and its square Y = X^2. Cauchy-Schwarz: (E[XY])^2 <= E[X^2] E[Y^2].
x = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
y = x**2
print(f"(E[XY])^2 = {np.mean(x * y)**2:.1f}   E[X^2] E[Y^2] = {np.mean(x**2) * np.mean(y**2):.1f}")
print(f"exact:      {73.5**2:.1f}                     {91 / 6 * 2275 / 6:.1f}")

# 2. Correlation never leaves [-1, 1]: try many random pairs with different dependence.
worst = 0.0
for _ in range(2000):
    c = rng.uniform(-3, 3)
    a = rng.normal(size=500)
    b = c * a + rng.normal(size=500)                   # Y depends on X with strength c
    rho = np.corrcoef(a, b)[0, 1]
    worst = max(worst, abs(rho))
print(f"largest |rho| over 2000 random pairs: {worst:.4f}")
print(f"Y = 3X exactly: rho = {np.corrcoef(a, 3 * a)[0, 1]:.4f}")

# 3. Second-moment bound P(X > 0) >= (E[X])^2 / E[X^2] for X ~ Poisson(1).
pois = rng.poisson(1.0, size=n_trials)
print(f"P(X > 0) = {np.mean(pois > 0):.4f}, bound = {np.mean(pois)**2 / np.mean(pois**2):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
(E[XY])^2 = 5388.2   E[X^2] E[Y^2] = 5736.1
exact:      5402.2                     5750.7
largest |rho| over 2000 random pairs: 0.9557
Y = 3X exactly: rho = 1.0000
P(X > 0) = 0.6320, bound = 0.4999
```

The left side stays below the right side in the die example (the small differences from the exact numbers come from using 1,000,000 rolls). No pair gets $\lvert \rho \rvert$ above 1, and the perfectly linear pair $Y = 3X$ reaches exactly 1. For the Poisson, the true $P(X \gt 0) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$ is above the bound $0.5$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the squares.** The inequality is about $\left(E[XY]\right)^2$ and $E[X^2]E[Y^2]$. Writing $E[XY] \le E[X]E[Y]$ is a different, and usually false, statement (it is false for positively related variables).
- **Using $\operatorname{Cov} \le \sigma_X\sigma_Y$ without the absolute value.** The covariance can also be as low as $-\sigma_X\sigma_Y$. The correct statement is $\lvert \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \rvert \le \sigma_X\sigma_Y$.
- **Thinking $\rho = 0$ means the bound is tight.** The reverse: $\rho = \pm 1$ is the tight case. For independent variables $E[XY]$ is far below the bound.
- **Forgetting that the second moments must be finite.** If $E[X^2] = \infty$, the right side is infinite and the inequality says nothing.
- **Misusing the second-moment bound.** It needs $X \ge 0$. It gives only a lower bound for $P(X \gt 0)$, and the bound can be 0 or very weak when $E[X^2]$ is much larger than $(E[X])^2$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** cosine similarity between two vectors, used to compare word embeddings and in attention scores, always lies in $[-1, 1]$ because of Cauchy-Schwarz (the dot product of vectors is the same inequality). Correlation between features and labels is also limited to $[-1, 1]$.
- **Statistics:** sample correlations, regression and the $R^2$ score all rely on $\lvert \rho \rvert \le 1$. It is the basis of the check on covariance matrices in Example 2.
- **Computer science:** the second-moment method shows that a random graph or a random assignment has some property with positive probability, from the mean and variance of a count alone.
- **Finance:** the standard deviation of a portfolio is at most the weighted sum of the standard deviations of its parts, with equality only if all assets are perfectly correlated. Mixing different assets reduces risk.
- **Everyday life:** two things cannot move together more strongly than their own size of movement allows. A claim of a "correlation of 1.3" is always an error.

**1. (Easy)** $E[X^2] = 4$ and $E[Y^2] = 9$. What is the largest possible value of $\lvert E[XY] \rvert$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\lvert E[XY] \rvert \le \sqrt{4 \cdot 9} = 6$$

The value 6 is reached when $Y = \frac{3}{2} X$ (then $E[XY] = \frac{3}{2} \cdot 4 = 6$).

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 25$. (a) Is $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 12$ possible? (b) If $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -8$, what is $\rho$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $\sigma_X \sigma_Y = 2 \times 5 = 10$, and $12 \gt 10$, so it is impossible. (b) $\rho = \frac{-8}{10} = -0.8$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Use Cauchy-Schwarz with $Y = 1$ to show $\big(E[\lvert X \rvert]\big)^2 \le E[X^2]$. Check it for $X = 1$ or $3$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Apply the inequality to $\lvert X \rvert$ and $1$: $\big(E[\lvert X \rvert \cdot 1]\big)^2 \le E[\lvert X \rvert^2] E[1^2] = E[X^2]$. For the example, $E[\lvert X \rvert] = 2$ and $E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 9}{2} = 5$:

$$2^2 = 4 \le 5$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $\sigma_X = 3$ and $\sigma_Y = 4$. What are the smallest and largest possible values of the standard deviation of $X + Y$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = 9 + 16 + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 25 + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$, and $\operatorname{Cov}$ lies in $[-12, 12]$. So the variance lies in $[1, 49]$ and the standard deviation lies in $[1, 7]$. The largest value $7 = 3 + 4$ happens at $\rho = 1$, and the smallest value $1 = \lvert 4 - 3 \rvert$ happens at $\rho = -1$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X \ge 0$ with mean $\mu \gt 0$ and finite $E[X^2]$, and let $0 \le \theta \le 1$. Prove the Paley-Zygmund inequality $P(X \ge \theta\mu) \ge (1 - \theta)^2 \frac{\mu^2}{E[X^2]}$. Evaluate it for $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and $\theta = \frac{1}{2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Split the mean: $\mu = E[X \cdot I_{\lbrace X \lt \theta\mu \rbrace}] + E[X \cdot I_{\lbrace X \ge \theta\mu \rbrace}]$. The first part is at most $\theta\mu$, because $X \lt \theta\mu$ there. For the second part use Cauchy-Schwarz with the indicator:

$$E[X \cdot I_{\lbrace X \ge \theta\mu \rbrace}] \le \sqrt{E[X^2] \thinspace P(X \ge \theta\mu)}$$

So $\mu \le \theta\mu + \sqrt{E[X^2] P(X \ge \theta\mu)}$, that is $(1 - \theta)\mu \le \sqrt{E[X^2] P(X \ge \theta\mu)}$. Square both sides and divide by $E[X^2]$. For $\text{Exp}(1)$: $\mu = 1$ and $E[X^2] = 2$, so the bound is $\frac{(1/2)^2}{2} = 0.125$. The exact value is $P(X \ge 0.5) = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.607$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Let $X$ and $Y$ be standard normal with correlation $0.8$. Estimate $(E[XY])^2$ and $E[X^2] E[Y^2]$, and check that the first is smaller.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
rho = 0.8
x = rng.normal(size=n_trials)
y = rho * x + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.normal(size=n_trials)   # standard normal with correlation 0.8
lhs = np.mean(x * y) ** 2
rhs = np.mean(x**2) * np.mean(y**2)
print(f"(E[XY])^2 = {lhs:.4f}  (exact 0.64)")
print(f"E[X^2] E[Y^2] = {rhs:.4f}  (exact 1)")
print(f"ratio = {lhs / rhs:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
(E[XY])^2 = 0.6418  (exact 0.64)
E[X^2] E[Y^2] = 1.0016  (exact 1)
ratio = 0.6408
```

The ratio is $\rho^2 = 0.64$, which is below 1.

</details>

## Summary

- Cauchy-Schwarz: $\left(E[XY]\right)^2 \le E[X^2] E[Y^2]$, with equality only when one variable is a constant multiple of the other.
- The proof: $E[(X - tY)^2] \ge 0$ for all $t$, a quadratic in $t$ that cannot have two roots.
- Applied to centered variables, it gives $\lvert \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \rvert \le \sigma_X \sigma_Y$ and $-1 \le \rho \le 1$.
- Applied to $X$ and an indicator, it gives $P(X \gt 0) \ge \frac{(E[X])^2}{E[X^2]}$ for $X \ge 0$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\big(E[XY]\big)^2 \le E[X^2] \thinspace E[Y^2]}
$$
