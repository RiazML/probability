# 16.5 Union Bound Revisited

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.5 Union Bound Revisited

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [16.4 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality](16.4-Cauchy-Schwarz-Inequality.md)

> **Big idea:** To make many things go right at the same time, give each one a small failure budget: if there are $K$ bad events and each has probability at most $\frac{\delta}{K}$, then all go well with probability at least $1 - \delta$.

---

## Why It Matters

We met the union bound in [3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md): the chance that at least one of several events happens is at most the sum of their chances. Here we look at it again with the tools of this module. We see that it is Markov's inequality in disguise ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)), we measure exactly how much it over-counts, and we add a matching lower bound. Then we use it for the most important job it has in modern data science: combining a **tail bound** for one quantity with the union bound to control **many quantities at once**. This is how we trust a test of 100 models, 20 statistical tests, or 1,000 random measurements.

## Intuition

You run a website with 8 servers. Each one crashes during a day with probability at most $0.01$. You do not know whether the crashes are linked (one power cut could take down several). What is the chance that at least one server crashes?

If the servers crashed one at a time and never together, the answer would be exactly $8 \times 0.01 = 0.08$. If crashes come together, then bad days overlap, and the number of bad days is smaller. So the chance is **at most** $0.08$. You need to know nothing about the links.

Now turn it around, as an engineer does. You want the whole system to work on a given day with probability at least $0.99$. Then you can afford a total failure budget of $0.01$. Share it among the 8 servers: each may fail with probability at most $\frac{0.01}{8} = 0.00125$. That is the **error budget** idea: divide the total allowed failure among the parts.

## Picture

```text
   Outcomes where something goes wrong (areas = probabilities):

   +--------------------------------------------------+
   |   +-------+                      +------+        |
   |   |  A1   |                      |  A3  |        |
   |   |    +--+----+                 +------+        |
   |   +----+--+    |                                 |
   |        |  A2   |                                 |
   |        +-------+                                 |
   +--------------------------------------------------+
   area of A1 + A2 + A3  >=  area of the union
   (the overlap of A1 and A2 is counted twice on the left side)


   Error budget: total allowance delta, split among K = 5 bad events

   |<----------------- delta = 0.05 ------------------>|
   +----------+----------+----------+----------+-------+
   |   0.01   |   0.01   |   0.01   |   0.01   |  0.01 |
   +----------+----------+----------+----------+-------+
   each event may fail with probability delta / K = 0.01
```

The sum of the areas is never smaller than the union. If the sum of the failure budgets is $\delta$, the chance of any failure is at most $\delta$.

## Definition

**The union bound (Boole's inequality).** For any events $A_1, \dots, A_K$:

$$
P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{K} A_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{K} P(A_i)
$$

**The error budget form.** Suppose each $A_i$ is a bad event with $P(A_i) \le \frac{\delta}{K}$. Then

$$
P(\text{every } A_i \text{ fails to happen}) = P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{K} A_i^c \right) \ge 1 - \delta
$$

**In words:** if each of $K$ bad things has chance at most $\frac{\delta}{K}$, then with probability at least $1 - \delta$ none of them happens. No independence is needed.

**The matching lower bound (Bonferroni).** Taking the pairs into account gives a bound from below:

$$
\sum_{i} P(A_i) - \sum_{i \lt j} P(A_i \cap A_j) \le P\left( \bigcup_{i} A_i \right) \le \sum_{i} P(A_i)
$$

**In words:** the union is squeezed between "the sum" and "the sum minus the pair overlaps". When the overlaps are small, the squeeze is tight and the union bound is nearly exact.

**The recipe: tail bound plus union bound.** Suppose for one quantity $Z$ you have a tail bound $P(\lvert Z - E[Z] \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \delta_1$. For $K$ such quantities (not necessarily independent), the union bound gives

$$
P\big(\text{some } \lvert Z_k - E[Z_k] \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \le K \delta_1
$$

If the tail bound is like $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$ ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)), then $K$ appears only inside a logarithm when we solve for $n$: $n \ge \frac{\ln(2K/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2}$. This is why the union bound is so cheap for light-tailed variables.

## Why It Is True

**A new proof: it is Markov's inequality.** Let $I_i$ be the indicator of $A_i$, and let $N = I_1 + \cdots + I_K$ be the **number of events that happen**. Its mean is $E[N] = \sum_i P(A_i)$. The union happens exactly when $N \ge 1$. Markov's inequality with threshold $a = 1$ ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)) gives:

$$
P\left( \bigcup_i A_i \right) = P(N \ge 1) \le \frac{E[N]}{1} = \sum_{i=1}^{K} P(A_i)
$$

**How much does it over-count?** For a whole number $N \ge 0$ we have $N - I_{\lbrace N \ge 1 \rbrace} = (N - 1)^+$, where $(m)^+$ means $m$ if $m \gt 0$ and $0$ otherwise. (Check: $N = 0$ gives $0$; $N = 1$ gives $0$; $N = 3$ gives $3 - 1 = 2$.) Taking expectations:

$$
\sum_i P(A_i) - P\left( \bigcup_i A_i \right) = E\big[(N - 1)^+\big]
$$

**In words:** the amount by which the union bound is too big is the average number of **extra** events beyond the first. It is 0 exactly when two events never happen together, that is, when $P(N \ge 2) = 0$.

**The lower bound.** For every whole number $N \ge 0$ we have the inequality

$$
I_{\lbrace N \ge 1 \rbrace} \ge N - \binom{N}{2}
$$

Check it: for $N = 0$: $0 \ge 0$. For $N = 1$: $1 \ge 1$. For $N = 2$: $1 \ge 2 - 1 = 1$. For $N \ge 3$ the right side is $\frac{N(3 - N)}{2} \le 0 \lt 1$. Now take expectations. The mean of $\binom{N}{2}$ is the expected number of pairs of events that both happen, and it equals $\sum_{i \lt j} P(A_i \cap A_j)$. So $P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) \ge \sum_i P(A_i) - \sum_{i \lt j} P(A_i \cap A_j)$.

**The error budget form.** By De Morgan's law, $\bigcap_i A_i^c$ is the complement of $\bigcup_i A_i$. So $P(\bigcap_i A_i^c) = 1 - P(\bigcup_i A_i) \ge 1 - \sum_i P(A_i) \ge 1 - K \cdot \frac{\delta}{K} = 1 - \delta$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Squeezing the Birthday Problem

**Problem.** In a group of $n$ people, let $A_{ij}$ be the event that persons $i$ and $j$ share a birthday (365 equally likely days, independent people). Find the upper and lower bounds for $P(\text{some pair shares})$ for $n = 5$ and $n = 23$, and compare with the exact values.

**Solution.**

1. For each pair, $P(A_{ij}) = \frac{1}{365}$. There are $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs.
2. For two different pair-events, the chance that both happen is $\frac{1}{365^2}$. If the pairs are disjoint (four different people), the two matches are independent. If they share a person, such as $(i, j)$ and $(i, k)$, all three people have one common birthday, which is also $\frac{1}{365^2}$.
3. For $n = 5$: there are $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs and $\binom{10}{2} = 45$ pairs of pair-events.

$$
\text{upper} = \frac{10}{365} \approx 0.0274, \qquad \text{lower} = \frac{10}{365} - \frac{45}{365^2} \approx 0.0274 - 0.0003 = 0.0271
$$

4. For $n = 23$: there are $\binom{23}{2} = 253$ pairs and $\binom{253}{2} = 31{,}878$ pairs of pair-events.

$$
\text{upper} = \frac{253}{365} \approx 0.6932, \qquad \text{lower} = \frac{253}{365} - \frac{31878}{133225} \approx 0.6932 - 0.2393 = 0.4539
$$

| $n$ | Lower bound | Exact | Upper bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | $0.0271$ | $0.0271$ | $0.0274$ |
| 23 | $0.4539$ | $0.5073$ | $0.6932$ |

**Answer.** For 5 people the squeeze is almost perfect. For 23 people the exact value $0.507$ is inside $[0.454, 0.693]$.

**Check.** The simulation below also lies inside both bounds. The union bound is good when the total is small, and worse when overlaps are common.

### Example 2: Twenty Statistical Tests

**Problem.** A scientist runs 20 independent tests, and in truth none of the effects are real. Each test is run at significance level $0.05$, which means a false alarm has probability $0.05$. (a) What does the union bound say about the chance of at least one false alarm? (b) What level should each test use so that the chance of any false alarm is at most $0.05$?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The sum of the failure chances is $20 \times 0.05 = 1$. The union bound says "at most 1", which says nothing. The exact chance (using independence) is:

$$
1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.6415
$$

   So with 20 tests at the usual level, a false alarm is more likely than not.
2. (b) Use the error budget form with total $\delta = 0.05$ and $K = 20$. Each test gets level $\frac{0.05}{20} = 0.0025$. This is the **Bonferroni correction**.
3. With this level, the exact chance of any false alarm (for independent tests) is:

$$
1 - (1 - 0.0025)^{20} = 1 - 0.9975^{20} \approx 0.0488
$$

**Answer.** (a) The bound is useless and the true value is $0.64$. (b) Use level $0.0025$ per test; then the overall false alarm chance is at most $0.05$ (the exact value is $0.0488$).

**Check.** The corrected bound is almost exact ($0.0488$ against $0.05$), because with a tiny level for each test, overlaps are very rare. The simulation agrees.

### Example 3: Choosing Among 100 Models

**Problem.** You test $K = 100$ candidate models on a fresh set of $n = 10{,}000$ examples. For each model, the measured error rate is an average of $n$ independent 0-or-1 outcomes. You want all 100 measured error rates to be within $\varepsilon = 0.02$ of the true error rates. What can you promise with (a) Chebyshev, (b) Hoeffding, and how large must $n$ be to promise 95%?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Chebyshev for one model ([16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)), using $p(1-p) \le \frac{1}{4}$:

$$
P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge 0.02) \le \frac{1/4}{10000 \times 0.0004} = 0.0625
$$

   The union bound over 100 models gives $100 \times 0.0625 = 6.25$. That is more than 1, so nothing is promised.
2. (b) Hoeffding for one model ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md), proved there) says $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$. With the numbers:

$$
2e^{-2 \times 10000 \times 0.0004} = 2e^{-8} \approx 0.00067
$$

   The union bound over 100 models gives $100 \times 0.00067 = 0.067$. So with probability at least $0.933$ **all** 100 measurements are within $0.02$.
3. For 95% overall we need $100 \times 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} \le 0.05$, which is $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} \le 0.0005$. Take logs:

$$
n \ge \frac{\ln(2K/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\ln(200/0.05)}{2 \times 0.0004} = \frac{\ln 4000}{0.0008} = \frac{8.294}{0.0008} \approx 10{,}368
$$

   For a single model ($K = 1$) the answer would be $\frac{\ln 40}{0.0008} \approx 4{,}612$.

**Answer.** Chebyshev plus the union bound fails. Hoeffding plus the union bound works, and needs $n = 10{,}368$ examples.

**Check.** Having 100 times more models costs only about $2.25$ times more data, because $K$ sits inside the logarithm. With Chebyshev, $K$ would multiply the sample size by $K$ itself.

## Simulate It

We check the birthday squeeze, the 20-test Bonferroni correction, and the maximum of 1,000 standard normals (where the union bound is nearly exact because large values are rare).

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Birthdays: any two of n people share a day. Union bound and Bonferroni lower bound.
for n_people in [5, 23]:
    pairs = comb(n_people, 2)
    upper = pairs / 365                                # sum of P(A_ij)
    lower = upper - comb(pairs, 2) / 365**2            # minus sum of P(A_ij and A_kl)
    days = np.sort(rng.integers(0, 365, size=(200_000, n_people)), axis=1)
    shared = np.mean(np.any(days[:, 1:] == days[:, :-1], axis=1))
    print(f"n = {n_people:2d}: lower {lower:.4f} <= simulated {shared:.4f} <= upper {upper:.4f}")

# 2. Twenty independent tests with no real effect: p-values are uniform on (0, 1).
p_values = rng.random((200_000, 20))
for level in [0.05, 0.05 / 20]:
    any_alarm = np.mean(np.any(p_values < level, axis=1))
    print(f"each test at level {level:.4f}: P(some false alarm) = {any_alarm:.4f}, union bound {min(1, 20 * level):.4f}")

# 3. Maximum of 1000 standard normals: the union bound says P(max >= t) <= 1000 P(Z >= t).
t = norm.isf(0.05 / 1000)                              # t that makes the union bound equal 0.05
maxima = rng.normal(size=(20_000, 1000)).max(axis=1)
print(f"t = {t:.3f}: simulated P(max >= t) = {np.mean(maxima >= t):.4f}, union bound 0.0500")
print(f"median of the maximum: {np.median(maxima):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =  5: lower 0.0271 <= simulated 0.0274 <= upper 0.0274
n = 23: lower 0.4539 <= simulated 0.5072 <= upper 0.6932
each test at level 0.0500: P(some false alarm) = 0.6412, union bound 1.0000
each test at level 0.0025: P(some false alarm) = 0.0486, union bound 0.0500
t = 3.891: simulated P(max >= t) = 0.0481, union bound 0.0500
median of the maximum: 3.198
```

In all three parts the simulated value lies inside the bounds. When the individual chances are tiny, the union bound is almost exact: $0.0486$ against $0.0500$, and $0.0481$ against $0.0500$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading the bound as the answer.** For 23 people the bound is $0.693$ but the truth is $0.507$. The union bound is "at most".
- **Using it when the sum is above 1.** With 20 tests at level $0.05$ the sum is exactly 1 and tells you nothing. Shrink the individual chances first, as in the Bonferroni correction.
- **Thinking independence is needed.** It is not. That is its strength. Do not replace it by $1 - (1-p)^K$ when the events may be linked: that formula is exact only for independent events.
- **Using it for uncountably many events.** If the family of bad events is infinite and uncountable (all possible thresholds, all possible weight vectors), the sum is infinite. Then you need other tools, such as covering a continuous family by a finite grid, which is beyond this book.
- **Forgetting to count the events.** $n$ people have $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs, not $n$ and not $n^2$. If you leave out some of the events, you leave out a real source of failure.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** to prove that the best of $K$ models on a validation set is truly good, we need all $K$ estimates to be accurate at once (Example 3). The size of the data needed grows only like $\ln K$. This is the first step in learning theory.
- **Computer science:** analysis of randomized algorithms "with high probability": if each of $n$ steps fails with probability at most $\frac{1}{n^2}$, then some step fails with probability at most $\frac{1}{n}$. Also hashing, load balancing and sketching.
- **Statistics:** the Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (Example 2) controls the chance of any false discovery.
- **Maximum of many variables:** $P(\max_k Z_k \ge t) \le K \thinspace P(Z \ge t)$ shows that the maximum of 1,000 standard normals is rarely above about $3.9$. This is how large-scale experiments set thresholds.
- **Everyday life:** the chance that "something goes wrong" in a long list of small risks is at most the sum of the risks, so a long list of small risks is a real risk ([22.12](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A system has 8 parts. Each fails on a given day with probability at most $0.01$. Give a bound for the chance that at least one part fails. If the parts are independent, what is the exact chance when each fails with probability exactly $0.01$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The union bound gives $8 \times 0.01 = 0.08$. For independent parts with exactly $0.01$:

$$1 - 0.99^8 \approx 0.0773$$

which is just below the bound.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You run 50 tests and want the chance of any false alarm to be at most $0.05$. What level should each test use?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Divide the budget: $\frac{0.05}{50} = 0.001$ per test.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A randomized algorithm makes 1,000 random choices, and each choice is bad with probability at most $10^{-5}$. Give a lower bound for the chance that all choices are good. How small must the bad chance of each choice be to guarantee success with probability at least $0.999999$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The union bound gives a failure chance of at most $1000 \times 10^{-5} = 0.01$, so success has probability at least $0.99$. For success $0.999999$ the total failure budget is $10^{-6}$, so each choice may fail with probability at most:

$$\frac{10^{-6}}{1000} = 10^{-9}$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Three events each have probability $0.3$, and each pair of events has intersection probability $0.05$. Give the best bounds for $P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3)$ from this section.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Upper bound: $3 \times 0.3 = 0.9$. Lower bound: $0.9 - 3 \times 0.05 = 0.9 - 0.15 = 0.75$. So

$$0.75 \le P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3) \le 0.9$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $\sum_i P(A_i) - P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = E[(N-1)^+]$ with $N = \sum_i I_{A_i}$, and conclude that the union bound is an equality if and only if $P(N \ge 2) = 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a whole number $N \ge 0$: if $N = 0$ then $N - I_{\lbrace N \ge 1 \rbrace} = 0 = (N-1)^+$; if $N \ge 1$ then $N - 1 = (N-1)^+$. So $N - I_{\lbrace N \ge 1 \rbrace} = (N-1)^+$ for every outcome. Take expectations, with $E[N] = \sum_i P(A_i)$ and $E[I_{\lbrace N \ge 1 \rbrace}] = P(\bigcup_i A_i)$. This proves the identity. The variable $(N-1)^+$ is never negative and is positive exactly when $N \ge 2$. A non-negative variable has mean 0 only if it is 0 with probability 1 ([16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md), Practice 4). So the gap is 0 if and only if $P(N \ge 2) = 0$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 2: simulate 50 independent tests with uniform p-values and estimate the chance that at least one p-value is below $0.001$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

p_values = rng.random((200_000, 50))                   # 50 tests, no real effect: p-values uniform

```

```

</details>

## Summary

- The union bound $P(\bigcup A_i) \le \sum P(A_i)$ is Markov's inequality for $N$, the number of events that happen. The gap is $E[(N-1)^+]$.
- Bonferroni gives a lower bound: subtract the sum of the pair overlaps. The two bounds squeeze the truth when overlaps are small.
- Error budget: if each of $K$ bad events has probability at most $\frac{\delta}{K}$, all go well with probability at least $1 - \delta$, without independence.
- Union bound plus an exponential tail bound controls $K$ quantities at once, and the price of $K$ is only $\ln K$ in the sample size.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{K} A_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{K} P(A_i)}
$$
