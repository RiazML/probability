> **Big idea:** Each inequality trades knowledge for strength: the more you know about a variable (its mean, variance, range, MGF), the smaller the tail bound you can prove, and learning to pick the right one is the skill of this module.

## How to Use This Set

- Each problem says which topic it uses. If you get stuck, reread that topic first.
- Try every problem on paper before opening the answer. Write each step, as in the worked examples.
- **Warm-Up** problems check the basic formulas. **Core** problems mix two or three ideas. **Challenge** problems include proofs. **Simulation Projects** ask you to write code.
- A good habit: ask first "what do I know?" Only the mean and $X \ge 0$: Markov. Mean and variance: Chebyshev. Independent bounded summands: Hoeffding. The MGF: Chernoff. Many events at once: union bound.
- After solving a problem by hand, check the number with a short simulation.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy)** *Uses [16.1 Markov Inequality](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md).*
The average waiting time at a clinic is 12 minutes. Bound the probability that a patient waits 60 minutes or more. Can the true probability be $0.19$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Waiting times are not negative, so Markov applies:

$$P(X \ge 60) \le \frac{12}{60} = 0.2$$

The true probability can be $0.19$, because $0.19 \le 0.2$. The bound says "at most", not "equal".

</details>

**W2. (Easy)** *Uses [16.2 Chebyshev Inequality](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md).*
Daily sales have mean 500 and standard deviation 50. Give a lower bound for $P(400 \lt X \lt 600)$ and an upper bound for $P(\lvert X - 500 \rvert \ge 200)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A distance of 100 is $k = 2$ standard deviations:

$$P(400 \lt X \lt 600) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{2^2} = 0.75$$

A distance of 200 is $k = 4$:

$$P(\lvert X - 500 \rvert \ge 200) \le \frac{1}{4^2} = 0.0625$$

</details>

**W3. (Easy)** *Uses [16.3 Jensen Inequality](16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md).*
$X$ is 0 or 10 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Compute $E[e^{X/10}]$ and $e^{E[X]/10}$, and say which is bigger and why.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $E[X] = 5$, so $e^{E[X]/10} = e^{0.5} \approx 1.6487$. Also:

$$E[e^{X/10}] = \tfrac{1}{2}e^0 + \tfrac{1}{2}e^1 = \tfrac{1 + e}{2} \approx 1.8591$$

The first is bigger, because $e^{x/10}$ is convex, so $E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X])$.

</details>

**W4. (Easy)** *Uses [16.4 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality](16.4-Cauchy-Schwarz-Inequality.md).*
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 9$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 16$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -10$. Is this possible? If yes, find $\rho$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cauchy-Schwarz needs $\lvert \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \rvert \le \sigma_X \sigma_Y = 3 \times 4 = 12$. Since $10 \le 12$, it is possible, and:

$$\rho = \frac{-10}{12} \approx -0.833$$

</details>

**W5. (Easy)** *Uses [16.5 Union Bound Revisited](16.5-Union-Bound-Revisited.md).*
A pipeline has 12 stages, and each fails with probability at most $0.002$. Bound the chance that the pipeline fails. What failure budget per stage guarantees success with probability at least $0.999$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The union bound gives $12 \times 0.002 = 0.024$, so the pipeline works with probability at least $0.976$. For success $0.999$ the total budget is $0.001$, so each stage may fail with probability at most:

$$\frac{0.001}{12} \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-5}$$

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium)** *Uses [16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md) and [16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md).*
A non-negative variable has mean 20 and standard deviation 4. Bound $P(X \ge 32)$ with (a) Markov, (b) Chebyshev, (c) Cantelli's one-sided inequality. Which is best, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Markov: $\frac{20}{32} = 0.625$. (b) The distance is $12 = 3\sigma$, so Chebyshev (two tails together) gives $\frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$. (c) Cantelli with $\sigma^2 = 16$ and $a = 12$:

$$P(X - 20 \ge 12) \le \frac{16}{16 + 144} = 0.1$$

Cantelli is best because it uses the variance and also knows that we need only one tail. Markov uses only the mean.

</details>

**C2. (Medium)** *Uses [16.2 Chebyshev Inequality](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md).*
The response time of a service has standard deviation $\sigma = 10$ milliseconds (unknown mean, unknown shape). How many independent measurements guarantee, by Chebyshev, that their average is within 1 millisecond of the true mean with probability at least $0.99$? Compare with the normal approximation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Chebyshev for the sample mean: $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge 1) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n \cdot 1^2} = \frac{100}{n}$. We need $\frac{100}{n} \le 0.01$, so:

$$n \ge 10{,}000$$

The normal approximation (not a guarantee) needs $2.576 \cdot \frac{10}{\sqrt{n}} \le 1$, so $n \ge 663.6$, that is $n = 664$. Chebyshev pays about 15 times more for the safety of "any distribution". (Hoeffding cannot be used here, because the times are not known to be bounded.)

</details>

**C3. (Medium)** *Uses [16.3 Jensen Inequality](16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md).*
A "fair" bet multiplies your money by $1.2$ or by $0.8$, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, independently each round. (a) What is the expected wealth after 100 rounds, starting from 1? (b) What is $E[\ln \text{factor}]$ per round, and the typical (median) wealth after 100 rounds? (c) Roughly what share of players end above their start?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) One round has mean factor $0.5(1.2) + 0.5(0.8) = 1$, so the expected wealth stays 1. (b) Since $\ln$ is concave:

$$E[\ln \text{factor}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln 1.2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln 0.8 = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln 0.96 \approx -0.0204$$

The median wealth is about $e^{100 \times (-0.0204)} = e^{-2.04} \approx 0.13$. (c) With $k$ gains out of 100, the wealth is above 1 when $k \ln 1.2 + (100 - k)\ln 0.8 \gt 0$, which needs $k \ge 56$. For a fair coin $P(k \ge 56) \approx 0.136$. So the average is 1, but a typical player has lost about 87% and only 13.6% are ahead.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
ups = rng.binomial(100, 0.5, size=1_000_000)           # rounds with factor 1.2, out of 100
wealth = 1.2**ups * 0.8 ** (100 - ups)
print(f"median final wealth {np.median(wealth):.4f}  (exact e^(-2.04) = 0.1299)")
print(f"share of paths above the start: {np.mean(wealth > 1):.4f}  (exact 0.1356)")
```

Output:

```text
median final wealth 0.1299  (exact e^(-2.04) = 0.1299)
share of paths above the start: 0.1355  (exact 0.1356)
```

</details>

**C4. (Medium)** *Uses [16.4 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality](16.4-Cauchy-Schwarz-Inequality.md).*
A non-negative variable has $E[X] = 2$ and $E[X^2] = 10$. Give a lower bound for $P(X \gt 0)$, and use the Paley-Zygmund inequality ([16.4](16.4-Cauchy-Schwarz-Inequality.md), Practice 5) with $\theta = \frac{1}{2}$ to bound $P(X \ge 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The second-moment bound gives:

$$P(X \gt 0) \ge \frac{(E[X])^2}{E[X^2]} = \frac{4}{10} = 0.4$$

Paley-Zygmund with $\mu = 2$, $\theta = \frac{1}{2}$ (so $\theta\mu = 1$):

$$P(X \ge 1) \ge (1 - \tfrac{1}{2})^2 \cdot \frac{\mu^2}{E[X^2]} = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \tfrac{4}{10} = 0.1$$

</details>

**C5. (Medium)** *Uses [16.6 Chernoff Bounds](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md).*
Flip a fair coin 200 times. Bound $P(\text{heads} \ge 120)$ with the Chernoff bound $e^{-nD(a \Vert p)}$ and with Cantelli's inequality. The exact value is $0.00284$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $a = 0.6$ and $p = 0.5$:

$$D(0.6 \Vert 0.5) = 0.6\ln 1.2 + 0.4\ln 0.8 \approx 0.109393 - 0.089257 = 0.020136$$

The Chernoff bound is $e^{-200 \times 0.020136} = e^{-4.027} \approx 0.0178$. For Cantelli, the variance is $50$ and the distance is $20$:

$$\frac{50}{50 + 400} \approx 0.111$$

Chernoff is about 6 times above the exact value, and Cantelli is about 39 times above it.

</details>

**C6. (Medium)** *Uses [16.7 Hoeffding Inequality](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md).*
You test a button on a web page and want its click rate within $\pm 0.01$ with 99% confidence. (a) How many independent visitors do you need? (b) With only 10,000 visitors, what is the 99% half-width?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Range 1, $\delta = 0.01$, $\varepsilon = 0.01$:

$$n \ge \frac{\ln(2/0.01)}{2 \times 0.0001} = \frac{5.2983}{0.0002} \approx 26{,}491.6$$

So $n = 26{,}492$ visitors. (b) For $n = 10{,}000$:

$$\varepsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\ln 200}{2 \times 10000}} = \sqrt{0.00026492} \approx 0.0163$$

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [16.1 Markov Inequality](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md) (general form).*
Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent with $P(X_i = 1) = P(X_i = -1) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $S = \sum_i X_i$. (a) Show that $E[S^4] = 3n^2 - 2n$. (b) Deduce $P(\lvert S \rvert \ge s) \le \frac{3n^2 - 2n}{s^4}$. (c) For $n = 100$ and $s = 30$, compare with Chebyshev and with the exact value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Expand $S^4 = \sum_{i,j,k,l} X_i X_j X_k X_l$. A term has mean 0 if some index appears an odd number of times (independence, zero means). The terms that survive have all four indices equal, giving $n$ terms with value $X_i^4 = 1$, or two different indices, each twice. For two different indices there are $\binom{n}{2}$ choices of the pair and $\frac{4!}{2! \cdot 2!} = 6$ orders, which is $3n(n-1)$ terms, each with mean $E[X_i^2]E[X_j^2] = 1$. So:

$$E[S^4] = n + 3n(n-1) = 3n^2 - 2n$$

(b) The events $\lbrace \lvert S \rvert \ge s \rbrace$ and $\lbrace S^4 \ge s^4 \rbrace$ are the same, and $S^4 \ge 0$. Markov gives $P(\lvert S \rvert \ge s) \le \frac{E[S^4]}{s^4}$. (c) With $n = 100$, $s = 30$: the fourth-moment bound is $\frac{30000 - 200}{810000} \approx 0.0368$. Chebyshev with $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 100$ is $\frac{100}{900} \approx 0.111$. The exact value is $P(\lvert H - 50 \rvert \ge 15) \approx 0.00352$ for $H \sim \text{Bin}(100, \frac{1}{2})$. Using a higher moment gave a bound 3 times better than Chebyshev. The best choice of power is what the Chernoff bound automates.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md) and [16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md).*
Prove that a variable $X$ with values in $[a, b]$ has $\operatorname{Var}(X) \le \frac{(b-a)^2}{4}$. Use it to show that Chebyshev needs at most $n \ge \frac{(b-a)^2}{4\varepsilon^2\delta}$ samples for $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \delta$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For any constant $c$, $E[(X - c)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (E[X] - c)^2 \ge \operatorname{Var}(X)$. Take $c = \frac{a+b}{2}$, the middle of the interval. Every value of $X$ is within $\frac{b-a}{2}$ of $c$, so $(X - c)^2 \le \frac{(b-a)^2}{4}$, and:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) \le E[(X - c)^2] \le \frac{(b-a)^2}{4}$$

Chebyshev for the mean gives $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \le \frac{(b-a)^2}{4n\varepsilon^2}$. Setting this at most $\delta$ gives $n \ge \frac{(b-a)^2}{4\varepsilon^2\delta}$. For a coin with $\varepsilon = 0.02$, $\delta = 0.05$ this is $12{,}500$, as in 16.2. The dependence on $\delta$ is $\frac{1}{\delta}$, while Hoeffding gives only $\ln\frac{2}{\delta}$.

</details>

**H3. (Hard)** *Uses [16.5 Union Bound Revisited](16.5-Union-Bound-Revisited.md) and [16.7 Hoeffding Inequality](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md).*
You compare $K = 50$ hyperparameter settings, and each is evaluated on its own independent set of $n$ validation examples (0-1 loss). How large must $n$ be so that, with probability at least $0.95$, every one of the 50 measured accuracies is within $0.03$ of its true value? Why does the best measured score look better than the truth?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Hoeffding for one setting: failure at most $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$. The union bound over 50 settings multiplies by 50. We need $50 \times 2e^{-2n(0.03)^2} \le 0.05$:

$$n \ge \frac{\ln(2K/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\ln 2000}{0.0018} = \frac{7.6009}{0.0018} \approx 4222.7$$

So $n = 4{,}223$. For a single setting ($K = 1$) the answer is $n = 2{,}050$, so 50 times more settings cost only about twice the data. The best of 50 noisy scores is picked **because** its noise happened to be favorable. Each error is up to $0.03$, so the winner's measured accuracy can be about $0.03$ above its true accuracy. This is why a final, separate test set is used.

</details>

**H4. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [16.3 Jensen Inequality](16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md) and [15.1 Moment Generating Function](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md).*
Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_n$ be standard normal (not necessarily independent) and $M = \max_i Z_i$. Prove $E[M] \le \sqrt{2\ln n}$ for $n \ge 2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fix $t \gt 0$. The function $x \mapsto e^{tx}$ is convex, so Jensen gives $e^{tE[M]} \le E[e^{tM}]$. The exponential of the maximum is the maximum of the exponentials, and a maximum is at most a sum of non-negative terms:

$$e^{tE[M]} \le E\big[\max_i e^{tZ_i}\big] \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} E\big[e^{tZ_i}\big] = n e^{t^2/2}$$

The last step uses the normal MGF $e^{t^2/2}$. Take logs and divide by $t$: $E[M] \le \frac{\ln n}{t} + \frac{t}{2}$. Choose $t = \sqrt{2\ln n}$:

$$E[M] \le \frac{\ln n}{\sqrt{2\ln n}} + \frac{\sqrt{2\ln n}}{2} = \sqrt{2\ln n}$$

For $n = 1000$ this is $3.72$. The simulation below shows the true mean is about $3.24$, so the bound has the right size.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
print("      n   simulated E[max]   bound sqrt(2 ln n)")
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 10_000]:
    maxima = rng.normal(size=(2000, n)).max(axis=1)     # 2000 maxima of n standard normals
    print(f"{n:7d}   {maxima.mean():15.3f}   {np.sqrt(2 * np.log(n)):17.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
      n   simulated E[max]   bound sqrt(2 ln n)
     10             1.553               2.146
    100             2.520               3.035
   1000             3.244               3.717
  10000             3.859               4.292
```

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Project)** *Uses [16.1](16.1-Markov-Inequality.md), [16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md), [16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md) and [16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md).*
**The bound zoo.** For $X \sim \text{Bin}(200, 0.3)$ compute the exact tail $P(X \ge k)$ for $k = 70, 80, 90, 100$ and compare it with the Markov, Cantelli, Hoeffding and Chernoff ($e^{-nD}$) bounds. Which bound is best at each $k$? Why is Hoeffding worse than Chernoff here?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

n, p = 200, 0.3                                         # X ~ Bin(200, 0.3): mean 60, variance 42
mu, var = n * p, n * p * (1 - p)

def kl(a, p):
    # D(a || p): exponent of the Chernoff bound for a binomial tail
    return a * np.log(a / p) + (1 - a) * np.log((1 - a) / (1 - p))

print("   k     exact    Markov  Cantelli  Hoeffding  Chernoff")
for k in [70, 80, 90, 100]:
    a = k / n
    print(f"{k:4d}  {binom.sf(k - 1, n, p):.2e}  {mu / k:.4f}  {var / (var + (k - mu) ** 2):.4f}"
          f"  {np.exp(-2 * n * (a - p) ** 2):.2e}  {np.exp(-n * kl(a, p)):.2e}")
```

Output:

```text
   k     exact    Markov  Cantelli  Hoeffding  Chernoff
  70  7.28e-02  0.8571  0.2958  3.68e-01  3.15e-01
  80  1.64e-03  0.7500  0.0950  1.83e-02  1.09e-02
  90  5.42e-06  0.6667  0.0446  1.23e-04  4.71e-05
 100  2.60e-09  0.6000  0.0256  1.13e-07  2.68e-08
```

The exact tail falls from $0.073$ at $k = 70$ to $2.6 \times 10^{-9}$ at $k = 100$. Markov never gets below $0.6$. Cantelli (using the variance) is the best bound at $k = 70$ ($0.296$ against $0.315$ for Chernoff), but it falls only like $\frac{1}{k^2}$, so it is useless for rare events. Chernoff and Hoeffding fall exponentially, and Chernoff beats the others from $k = 80$ on (at $k = 90$ it gives $4.7 \times 10^{-5}$ against $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ for Hoeffding and $0.045$ for Cantelli). Hoeffding is worse because it assumes the worst case $p = \frac{1}{2}$ and ignores that here the variance $p(1-p) = 0.21$ is smaller. Chernoff's $D(a \Vert p)$ uses the real $p$.

</details>

**S2. (Project)** *Uses [16.7 Hoeffding Inequality](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md).*
**How conservative is the sample size?** For $\varepsilon = 0.05$ compute the exact failure probability $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - p \rvert \ge \varepsilon)$ for $n = 100, 385, 738$ and $p = 0.5, 0.1$, and compare with the Hoeffding bound $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

eps = 0.05
print("    n     p   exact failure   Hoeffding bound")
for n in [100, 385, 738]:
    k = np.arange(n + 1)
    for p in [0.5, 0.1]:
        far = np.abs(k - n * p) >= n * eps - 1e-9      # outcomes with |Xbar - p| >= eps
        exact = binom.pmf(k, n, p)[far].sum()
        print(f"{n:5d}  {p:4.1f}   {exact:13.5f}   {2 * np.exp(-2 * n * eps**2):15.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
    n     p   exact failure   Hoeffding bound
  100   0.5         0.36820           1.21306
  100   0.1         0.13015           1.21306
  385   0.5         0.05265           0.29175
  385   0.1         0.00136           0.29175
  738   0.5         0.00717           0.04994
  738   0.1         0.00001           0.04994
```

At $n = 100$ the Hoeffding bound is above 1 and says nothing. At $n = 738$ it is $0.0499$ and the real failure rate is $0.0072$ for the worst coin ($p = 0.5$) and almost 0 for $p = 0.1$. The normal approximation says $n = 385$ is enough for $p = 0.5$, and the exact value $0.053$ is just above $0.05$, so the approximation is about right. Hoeffding costs about twice as many samples and, in return, is a guarantee for every $p$ and every $n$.

</details>
