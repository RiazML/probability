# 16.8 Concentration of Measure

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](README.md) / 16.8 Concentration of Measure

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [16.7 Hoeffding Inequality](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)

> **Big idea:** A quantity that depends on many independent inputs, none of which matters much on its own, is almost constant, and in high dimensions this makes lengths, angles and volumes behave in surprising ways.

---

## Why It Matters

Hoeffding's inequality ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)) says that an **average** of many independent numbers sits very close to its mean. **Concentration of measure** is the big picture behind it: not only averages, but almost any "smooth" function of many independent inputs is nearly constant. This one idea explains why a random algorithm with many random choices behaves predictably, why the length of a random vector in 1,000 dimensions is almost fixed, why nearest neighbors stop being meaningful in high dimension, and why random projections can compress data. This topic is mostly about intuition, with proofs where they are short.

## Intuition

Flip a coin 10,000 times. The number of heads is between 4,900 and 5,100 almost every time. That is a window of only 2% around the mean, even though the number of possible outcomes is astronomically large ($2^{10000}$). The reason is not that the sequences are similar. It is that **almost all** sequences have about 5,000 heads.

Now think of a high-dimensional space. A point has 1,000 coordinates, each drawn independently from a bell curve. Its squared length is a sum of 1,000 squared numbers, each with mean 1. This is an average of many independent pieces, so it is almost exactly 1,000. The length of the point is almost exactly $\sqrt{1000} \approx 31.6$, for **every** random point. All the random points sit on a thin shell, like skin on an orange.

An orange has another strange property. In 3 dimensions about 14% of the volume of a ball lies in its outer 5%. In 100 dimensions it is 99.4%. If you peel a 100-dimensional orange, there is almost no fruit left. Both facts come from the same principle: **when many independent contributions add up, the total is nearly fixed.**

As the dimension $d$ grows, the length of a random point, measured in units of $\sqrt{d}$, collapses to the single value 1.

## Definition

The **concentration of measure phenomenon** is the following principle. If $f(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is a function of **independent** random variables, and $f$ does not change much when one input changes, then $f$ is very close to its mean (or its median) with very high probability. Here are three precise versions, from simple to general.

**1. Sums (Hoeffding).** For independent $X_i \in [a_i, b_i]$ and $S = \sum_i X_i$, the bound $P(\lvert S - E[S] \rvert \ge t) \le 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2}\right)$ holds ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)).

**2. Bounded differences (McDiarmid's inequality).** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent and let $f$ be a function with the following property: if you change only the $i$-th input, the value of $f$ changes by at most $c_i$. Then for every $t \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(\lvert f(X_1, \dots, X_n) - E[f] \rvert \ge t\big) \le 2\exp\left( -\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i^2} \right)
$$

**In words:** a function of independent inputs, where no single input has a big influence, is concentrated around its mean. For $f = \sum X_i$ we have $c_i = b_i - a_i$ and this is Hoeffding.

**3. Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions.** A function $f$ is **1-Lipschitz** if $\lvert f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{y}) \rvert \le \lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \rVert$ (it never changes faster than the distance moves). If $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_d)$ is a vector of $d$ independent standard normals, then for every $t \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(\lvert f(\mathbf{X}) - E[f(\mathbf{X})] \rvert \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-t^2/2}
$$

**In words:** a 1-Lipschitz function of a Gaussian vector stays within a few units of its mean, and the bound does not depend on the dimension $d$ at all.

> **Note:** We prove the facts we use for lengths, angles and volumes below. We state the Gaussian concentration inequality without proof (its proofs need more advanced tools). For McDiarmid's inequality we give the proof idea, and the full argument with martingales is in [20.8](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.8-Martingales.md).

## Why It Is True

### Length of a Gaussian vector

Let $\mathbf{X} = (Z_1, \dots, Z_d)$ with independent $Z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The squared length is $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2 = Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_d^2$. Each $Z_i^2$ has mean $E[Z_i^2] = 1$ and variance $E[Z_i^4] - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2$ (the fourth moment of a standard normal is 3, [6.7](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)). So the average $\frac{1}{d}\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2$ has mean 1 and variance $\frac{2}{d}$. Chebyshev ([16.2](16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)) gives, for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$:

$$
P\left( \left\lvert \frac{\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2}{d} - 1 \right\rvert \ge \varepsilon \right) \le \frac{2}{d\varepsilon^2}
$$

The Chernoff method ([16.6](16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)) gives something much stronger. The MGF of $Z^2$ is, for $t \lt \frac{1}{2}$,

$$
E\big[e^{tZ^2}\big] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(1-2t)z^2/2} \thinspace dz = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2t}}
$$

(the integrand is a normal density with variance $\frac{1}{1-2t}$, up to the factor $\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-2t}}$). By independence the MGF of the sum is $(1-2t)^{-d/2}$. For the upper tail take $t = \frac{\varepsilon}{2(1+\varepsilon)}$, so that $1 - 2t = \frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}$:

$$
P\big(\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2 \ge d(1+\varepsilon)\big) \le e^{-td(1+\varepsilon)} (1-2t)^{-d/2} = \exp\left( -\frac{d}{2}\big(\varepsilon - \ln(1+\varepsilon)\big) \right)
$$

For the lower tail use the same method with $e^{-sZ^2}$, $s = \frac{\varepsilon}{2(1-\varepsilon)}$ and $0 \lt \varepsilon \lt 1$:

$$
P\big(\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2 \le d(1-\varepsilon)\big) \le \exp\left( \frac{d}{2}\big(\varepsilon + \ln(1-\varepsilon)\big) \right)
$$

Both exponents are negative, and for small $\varepsilon$ they are about $-\frac{d\varepsilon^2}{4}$. So the probability of a relative error of $\varepsilon$ in the squared length falls **exponentially in $d$**.

### The volume of a ball lives in its skin

A ball of radius $r$ in $d$ dimensions has volume $V_d r^d$, where $V_d$ is a constant that depends only on $d$. The ball of radius $1 - \varepsilon$ has $(1-\varepsilon)^d$ times the volume of the unit ball. So the fraction of the unit ball that lies in the outer shell of thickness $\varepsilon$ is

$$
1 - (1 - \varepsilon)^d \ge 1 - e^{-\varepsilon d}
$$

using $1 - x \le e^{-x}$. This is close to 1 as soon as $\varepsilon d$ is large, for example when $d$ is much bigger than $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$.

### Random directions are nearly perpendicular

Let $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ be independent standard Gaussian vectors in $d$ dimensions, and let $\theta$ be the angle between them. The distribution of $\mathbf{X}$ is the same in every direction (it is rotation invariant, [12.5](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)). So we may rotate until $\mathbf{Y}$ points along the first axis. Then $\cos\theta = u_1$, the first coordinate of the unit vector $\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{X}}{\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert}$. The $d$ coordinates of $\mathbf{u}$ have the same distribution, and $u_1^2 + \cdots + u_d^2 = 1$. Taking the expectation:

$$
d \cdot E[u_1^2] = 1 \quad \implies \quad E[\cos^2\theta] = \frac{1}{d}
$$

**In words:** the cosine of the angle between two random directions is typically of size $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}$. In 10,000 dimensions it is about $0.01$, so the angle is within $0.6$ degrees of $90$ degrees.

### The idea of McDiarmid's proof

This is an outline, not a full proof. Reveal the inputs one at a time and let $W_k = E[f \mid X_1, \dots, X_k]$ be your best guess for $f$ after seeing $k$ inputs ([14.2](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md)). Then $W_0 = E[f]$ and $W_n = f$. Each step $W_k - W_{k-1}$ has mean 0 given the past, and it lies in an interval of length at most $c_k$, because changing $X_k$ changes $f$ by at most $c_k$. Hoeffding's lemma ([16.7](16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)) then bounds the MGF of each step by $e^{t^2c_k^2/8}$, and the Chernoff method for the total $W_n - W_0$ gives exactly the stated bound.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: How Sure Is the Length of a Vector in 1,000 Dimensions?

**Problem.** Let $\mathbf{X}$ be a standard Gaussian vector in $d = 1000$ dimensions. Bound the chance that $\frac{\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2}{d}$ is off from 1 by $0.2$ or more, with Chebyshev and with Chernoff. What does it say about $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$?

**Solution.**

1. Chebyshev with $\varepsilon = 0.2$: $\frac{2}{1000 \times 0.04} = 0.05$.
2. Chernoff upper tail: $\exp\left(-500(0.2 - \ln 1.2)\right) = \exp(-500 \times 0.017678) = e^{-8.84} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-4}$.
3. Chernoff lower tail: $\exp\left(500(0.2 + \ln 0.8)\right) = \exp(500 \times (-0.023144)) = e^{-11.57} \approx 9.4 \times 10^{-6}$.
4. Together the Chernoff bound is about $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$.
5. With probability at least $0.9998$ we have $0.8 \lt \frac{\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2}{1000} \lt 1.2$. Take square roots: $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$ is between $\sqrt{800} = 28.3$ and $\sqrt{1200} = 34.6$.

**Answer.** The squared length is within 20% of 1,000 with probability at least $0.9998$ (Chebyshev alone gives only $0.95$).

**Check.** The simulation below shows that the typical $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$ is $31.6$ with a spread of only about $0.7$, much tighter than the guaranteed range. Gaussian concentration says the spread of $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$ (a 1-Lipschitz function) is at most about 1, for any $d$.

### Example 2: Peeling a High-Dimensional Orange

**Problem.** What fraction of the volume of a unit ball in $d$ dimensions lies within the outer shell of thickness $0.05$ (radius between $0.95$ and $1$), for $d = 3, 10, 100, 1000$?

**Solution.**

1. The inner ball of radius $0.95$ has $0.95^d$ of the volume, so the shell has $1 - 0.95^d$.
2. Compute the powers: $0.95^3 = 0.857$, $0.95^{10} = 0.599$, $0.95^{100} = 0.00592$, and $0.95^{1000} \approx 5.3 \times 10^{-23}$.

| $d$ | Inner ball $0.95^d$ | Shell $1 - 0.95^d$ |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | $0.857$ | $0.143$ |
| 10 | $0.599$ | $0.401$ |
| 100 | $0.00592$ | $0.994$ |
| 1000 | $5.3 \times 10^{-23}$ | $1.000$ |

**Answer.** In 100 dimensions, 99.4% of the volume is in the outer 5% of the radius.

**Check.** The bound $1 - e^{-\varepsilon d} = 1 - e^{-5}$ for $d = 100$ gives $0.9933$, close to the exact $0.9941$. A uniformly random point in a high-dimensional ball is almost surely near its surface.

### Example 3: Empty Bins

**Problem.** Throw $m = 1000$ balls independently and uniformly into $n = 1000$ bins. Let $N$ be the number of empty bins. Find $E[N]$ and bound $P(\lvert N - E[N] \rvert \ge t)$ with McDiarmid's inequality.

**Solution.**

1. A given bin is empty if all 1,000 balls miss it, which has probability $\left(1 - \frac{1}{1000}\right)^{1000}$. By linearity ([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)):

$$
E[N] = 1000 \times 0.999^{1000} \approx 367.7
$$

2. The function $N$ depends on the 1,000 independent ball positions. Moving one ball changes at most two bins (one gains a ball, one loses one), so $N$ changes by at most 1. Hence $c_i = 1$ for all $i$ and $\sum c_i^2 = 1000$.
3. McDiarmid: $P(\lvert N - 367.7 \rvert \ge t) \le 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^2}{1000}\right)$.

| $t$ | McDiarmid bound | Simulated value |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | $0.899$ | $0.0422$ |
| 30 | $0.331$ | $0.0024$ |
| 40 | $0.0815$ | $0.00004$ |

**Answer.** $E[N] \approx 367.7$, and the number of empty bins is within about 40 of that with probability at least $0.91$.

**Check.** We got this without computing any variance and without handling the dependence between the bins (the bins are not independent, but the **balls** are). The bound is true but conservative: the real standard deviation is $9.9$, so a deviation of 40 is 4 standard deviations away. Simulated values are from the code below.

## Simulate It

We check three things: the length of Gaussian vectors in many dimensions, the spread of the cosine of the angle between random vectors, and the empty-bins example.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Length of a standard Gaussian vector in d dimensions: |X| / sqrt(d) tends to 1.
print("     d   mean |X|/sqrt(d)   sd of |X|     (1/sqrt(2) = 0.7071)")
for d in [1, 10, 100, 1000, 10_000]:
    norms = np.linalg.norm(rng.normal(size=(2000, d)), axis=1)
    print(f"{d:6d}   {norms.mean() / np.sqrt(d):15.4f}   {norms.std():9.4f}")

# 2. Angle between two independent random directions: cos(theta) has spread 1/sqrt(d).
print("     d   sd of cos(theta)   1/sqrt(d)")
for d in [10, 100, 1000, 10_000]:
    x, y = rng.normal(size=(2000, d)), rng.normal(size=(2000, d))
    cos = (x * y).sum(axis=1) / (np.linalg.norm(x, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(y, axis=1))
    print(f"{d:6d}   {cos.std():16.4f}   {1 / np.sqrt(d):9.4f}")

# 3. Balls and bins: 1000 balls into 1000 bins; N = number of empty bins. Moving one ball changes N by at most 1.
n_bins = n_balls = 1000
empty = np.array([np.sum(np.bincount(rng.integers(0, n_bins, n_balls), minlength=n_bins) == 0)
                  for _ in range(50_000)])
exact_mean = n_bins * (1 - 1 / n_bins) ** n_balls
print(f"mean of N: simulated {empty.mean():.2f}, exact {exact_mean:.2f}, sd {empty.std():.2f}")
for t in [20, 30, 40]:
    mcdiarmid = 2 * np.exp(-2 * t**2 / n_balls)         # bounded differences, every c_i = 1
    print(f"t = {t}: P(|N - mean| >= t) simulated {np.mean(np.abs(empty - exact_mean) >= t):.5f}, McDiarmid {mcdiarmid:.4f}")
```

Output:

     d   mean |X|/sqrt(d)   sd of |X|     (1/sqrt(2) = 0.7071)
     1            0.7947      0.6125
    10            0.9798      0.7001
   100            0.9991      0.6965
  1000            0.9996      0.6905
 10000            0.9997      0.7201
     d   sd of cos(theta)   1/sqrt(d)
    10             0.3137      0.3162
   100             0.0996      0.1000
  1000             0.0320      0.0316
 10000             0.0099      0.0100
mean of N: simulated 367.70, exact 367.70, sd 9.84
t = 20: P(|N - mean| >= t) simulated 0.04220, McDiarmid 0.8987

t = 40: P(|N - mean| >= t) simulated 0.00004, McDiarmid 0.0815

Part 1 is the heart of the topic: the **relative** length $\frac{\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert}{\sqrt{d}}$ goes to 1, while the **absolute** spread of $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$ stays near $0.71$, however large $d$ is. Part 2 matches $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}$. Part 3 matches Example 3.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking concentration means "all values are equal".** The absolute spread of $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$ stays near $0.71$ for every $d$. It is the spread **relative** to the size $\sqrt{d}$ that goes to 0.
- **Using it for dependent inputs.** McDiarmid and Hoeffding need independent inputs. Strongly dependent inputs (all equal to the same coin flip) do not concentrate.
- **Forgetting the "no single input matters much" condition.** The function $f = X_1$ depends on one input only, with $c_1$ as large as the range. The bound $2\exp(-2t^2/c_1^2)$ is then not small. Concentration needs many small influences.
- **Trusting 2-D and 3-D pictures in high dimension.** In 3 dimensions the outer 5% shell has 14% of the volume, so you may think the core matters. In 100 dimensions the core is empty. Pictures from low dimensions mislead.
- **Thinking "nearly orthogonal" means "exactly orthogonal".** Two random directions have $\lvert \cos\theta \rvert$ of about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}$, which is small but not zero. It is the many small dot products that add up.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the curse of dimensionality. In high dimension, the distances from a query to random data points are almost all equal (Practice 6), so "nearest neighbor" carries little information. Weight initialization (Xavier and He) keeps the length of activations about constant across layers; this works because of concentration of $\lVert W\mathbf{x} \rVert^2$.
- **Dimension reduction:** the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma says that $N$ points can be mapped by a random projection to about $\frac{\ln N}{\varepsilon^2}$ dimensions while all pairwise distances change by at most a factor $1 \pm \varepsilon$. It follows from the concentration of the length of a random vector, together with the union bound ([16.5](16.5-Union-Bound-Revisited.md)).
- **Computer science:** balls and bins, hashing and load balancing (Example 3): the number of empty slots, the maximum load and the number of collisions are all tightly concentrated. See [21.3.1 Randomized Algorithms](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.1-Randomized-Algorithms.md).
- **Learning theory:** the difference between training and test error is a function of many independent samples with small influence each; McDiarmid's inequality is the main step in many generalization bounds.
- **Physics and everyday life:** the pressure of a gas is steady because it is the sum of billions of tiny independent collisions. Insurance and casinos make steady profits for the same reason.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** What fraction of the volume of a 50-dimensional unit ball is within distance $0.9$ of the center? What fraction is in the outer shell of thickness $0.1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The inner ball has $0.9^{50}$ of the volume:

$$0.9^{50} \approx 0.00515$$

So the outer shell of thickness $0.1$ has about $1 - 0.00515 = 0.9948$, or 99.5%, of the volume.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A standard Gaussian vector in $d = 400$ dimensions has $\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert$ close to what number, and what is a rough spread?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert \approx \sqrt{400} = 20$, with a spread (standard deviation) of about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.71$. So the length is almost always between about $18.6$ and $21.4$ (two standard deviations on each side).

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For $d = 2000$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, give the Chebyshev bound and the Chernoff bound (both tails) for $P\left(\left\lvert \frac{\lVert \mathbf{X} \rVert^2}{d} - 1 \right\rvert \ge \varepsilon\right)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Chebyshev: $\frac{2}{d\varepsilon^2} = \frac{2}{2000 \times 0.01} = 0.1$. For Chernoff, the upper tail is $\exp\left(-1000(0.1 - \ln 1.1)\right) = e^{-4.69} \approx 0.0092$, and the lower tail is $\exp\left(1000(0.1 + \ln 0.9)\right) = e^{-5.36} \approx 0.0047$. Their sum is about $0.014$, seven times smaller than Chebyshev, and the gap grows with $d$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that McDiarmid's inequality contains Hoeffding's inequality, and use it for the number of heads in 100 fair coin flips, $t = 10$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \sum x_i$ with $x_i \in [a_i, b_i]$. Changing the $i$-th input changes $f$ by at most $c_i = b_i - a_i$. McDiarmid gives $2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum (b_i - a_i)^2}\right)$, which is Hoeffding's two-sided bound. For 100 flips, $c_i = 1$, so $\sum c_i^2 = 100$ and:

$$P(\lvert S - 50 \rvert \ge 10) \le 2e^{-2 \times 100/100} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.271$$

The exact value is $0.0569$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ be independent standard Gaussian vectors in $d$ dimensions. Prove that $E[(\mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y})^2] = d$, and deduce a Chebyshev bound for $P\left(\frac{\lvert \mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y} \rvert}{d} \ge \varepsilon\right)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $\mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y} = \sum_i X_i Y_i$. Squaring gives $\sum_i \sum_j X_i Y_i X_j Y_j$. For $i \ne j$, independence and zero means give $E[X_i X_j Y_i Y_j] = E[X_i]E[X_j]E[Y_i]E[Y_j] = 0$. For $i = j$, $E[X_i^2 Y_i^2] = E[X_i^2]E[Y_i^2] = 1$. So the total is $d$. The mean of $\mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y}$ is 0, so its variance is $d$. Chebyshev:

$$P\left( \frac{\lvert \mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y} \rvert}{d} \ge \varepsilon \right) = P\big(\lvert \mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y} \rvert \ge d\varepsilon\big) \le \frac{d}{d^2\varepsilon^2} = \frac{1}{d\varepsilon^2}$$

Since both lengths are about $\sqrt{d}$, $\cos\theta \approx \frac{\mathbf{X} \cdot \mathbf{Y}}{d}$ is small with high probability.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Draw 500 random points in $d$ dimensions and one query point (all standard Gaussian). Find the nearest and the farthest distance from the query for $d = 2, 10, 100, 1000, 10000$. What happens to the ratio?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_points = 500
print("     d   nearest   farthest   farthest / nearest")
for d in [2, 10, 100, 1000, 10_000]:
    query = rng.normal(size=d)
    points = rng.normal(size=(n_points, d))
    dist = np.linalg.norm(points - query, axis=1)      # distance from the query to 500 random points
    print(f"{d:6d}   {dist.min():7.2f}   {dist.max():8.2f}   {dist.max() / dist.min():12.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
     d   nearest   farthest   farthest / nearest
     2      0.04       4.30         104.36
    10      0.98       6.35           6.51
   100     12.11      17.36           1.43
  1000     42.31      47.24           1.12
 10000    138.68     144.31           1.04
```

In 2 dimensions the farthest point is 100 times farther than the nearest. In 10,000 dimensions it is only 4% farther: the points are all at nearly the same distance (about $\sqrt{2d}$), so the idea of a "nearest neighbor" almost disappears.

</details>

## Summary

- Concentration of measure: a function of many independent inputs, where no single input matters much, is very close to its mean. Averages (Hoeffding) are the simplest case, and McDiarmid's inequality covers bounded-difference functions.
- In $d$ dimensions, the length of a Gaussian vector is almost exactly $\sqrt{d}$ (proved with Chebyshev and Chernoff on a sum of $d$ squares), and random directions are nearly perpendicular ($\lvert \cos\theta \rvert \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}$).
- The volume of a ball lives in its skin: the fraction within radius $1 - \varepsilon$ is $(1-\varepsilon)^d$.
- Consequences: all distances between random points become alike, random projections preserve geometry, and sums over huge systems are steady.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\big(\lvert f - E[f] \rvert \ge t\big) \le 2\exp\left( -\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i} c_i^2} \right)}
$$
