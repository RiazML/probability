# 7.11 Relationships Between Distributions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.11 Relationships Between Distributions

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [7.10 Poisson Approximation to Binomial](7.10-Poisson-Approximation-to-Binomial.md)

> **Big idea:** The named discrete distributions are one family: they are built from each other by adding, waiting, splitting, conditioning and taking limits, and knowing these links lets you derive results instead of memorizing them.

---

## Why It Matters

You have met nine distributions in this module. Learned one by one, they are a list
of formulas to memorize. Learned as a family, they explain each other: the binomial
is a sum of Bernoullis, the Poisson is a limit of binomials, the negative binomial
is a sum of geometric waits. These links also answer practical questions fast. Two
servers send you Poisson traffic: what is the total? A filter keeps 20% of the
events: what is left? The answers come from the relationships on this page.

## Intuition

Two call centers get 3 and 5 calls per minute on average, independently. Think of
each minute as many tiny pieces of time. In each piece, center A has a tiny chance
of a call, and so does center B. The combined center has a tiny chance of a call
from A or B in each piece, and the average total is $3 + 5 = 8$. A count of rare,
independent events with average 8 is $\text{Pois}(8)$. So adding independent Poisson
counts gives a Poisson count.

Now go the other way. Emails arrive at 10 per hour, and each is spam with
probability 0.2, independently. Mark each email with a coin flip that comes up
"spam" 20% of the time. The spam emails are still rare, independent events, at
average rate $10 \times 0.2 = 2$ per hour. So **splitting** a Poisson count also gives
Poisson counts.

## Picture

```text
   HGeom(N, K, n)                          Cat(p1, ..., pk)
        |                                    |            |
        | N -> ∞ with K/N -> p       n draws |            | all pj = 1/k
        v                                    v            v
     Bin(n, p) <------ one count ------ Mult(n, p1..pk)  Unif{1, ..., k}
      ^    |
  sum |    | n -> ∞, p -> 0, np = λ
 of n |    v
      |   Pois(λ) --+-- add an independent Pois(μ):  Pois(λ + μ)
      |             +-- keep each event with prob p:  Pois(λp)
      |

      |
      | count the trials until the 1st success
      v
   Geom(p)
      |
      | add r independent waits, counting only failures
      v
   NegBin(r, p)
```

The family tree of discrete distributions. Each arrow is an operation that turns one
distribution into another.

## Definition

The table lists the main relationships. In every row, "independent" is required
unless the row says otherwise.

| # | Start from | Operation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(p)$ | add them | $\text{Bin}(n, p)$ |
| 2 | $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$, $Y \sim \text{Bin}(m, p)$ | add them | $\text{Bin}(n + m, p)$ |
| 3 | $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$, $Y \sim \text{Pois}(\mu)$ | add them | $\text{Pois}(\lambda + \mu)$ |
| 4 | $G_1, \dots, G_r \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Geom}(p)$ | add the $G_i - 1$ | $\text{NegBin}(r, p)$ |
| 5 | $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{\lambda}{n})$ | let $n \to \infty$ | $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ |
| 6 | $\text{HGeom}(N, K, n)$ | let $N \to \infty$, $\frac{K}{N} \to p$ | $\text{Bin}(n, p)$ |
| 7 | $N \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ events | keep each with prob. $p$ | $\text{Pois}(\lambda p)$ kept, $\text{Pois}(\lambda(1-p))$ dropped, independent |
| 8 | $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$, $Y \sim \text{Pois}(\mu)$ | condition on $X + Y = n$ | $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, \frac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu})$ |
| 9 | $\text{Mult}(n, p_1, \dots, p_k)$ | look at one count | $\text{Bin}(n, p_j)$ |

Some distributions are special cases of others:

- $\text{Bern}(p) = \text{Bin}(1, p)$, and a Bernoulli is a categorical with $k = 2$.
- A categorical is a multinomial with $n = 1$ (as a one-hot vector).
- $\text{Unif}\lbrace 1, \dots, k \rbrace$ is a categorical with all $p_j = \frac{1}{k}$.
- The number of failures before the first success, $\text{Geom}(p) - 1$, is $\text{NegBin}(1, p)$.

**In words:** sums of independent counts of the same kind stay in the family;
rare-event limits lead to the Poisson; and a Poisson count can be split into
independent Poisson counts, or rebuilt from them.

Links to continuous distributions, for later: the geometric is the discrete version
of the exponential ([9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)); in a
Poisson process the gaps between events are exponential
([18.5](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.5-Poisson-Process.md)); a Poisson count whose rate
is Gamma-distributed is negative binomial
([10.8](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)); and binomial and
Poisson counts with a large mean look normal
([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)).

## Why It Is True

Rows 1, 4, 5 and 9 were proved in [7.2](7.2-Binomial.md), [7.4](7.4-Negative-Binomial.md),
[7.10](7.10-Poisson-Approximation-to-Binomial.md) and [7.9](7.9-Multinomial.md).

**Row 2, by a story.** $X$ counts successes in $n$ independent trials and $Y$ in $m$ more
trials, all with the same $p$. Together they count successes in $n + m$ independent
trials with success probability $p$. That is $\text{Bin}(n + m, p)$. The story breaks if
the two $p$ values differ.

**Row 3, Poisson plus Poisson.** $X + Y = n$ happens when $X = k$ and $Y = n - k$ for some
$k$ from 0 to $n$. These cases are disjoint, and $X$ and $Y$ are independent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X + Y = n) &= \sum_{k=0}^{n} P(X = k) \thinspace P(Y = n - k) && \text{(split by } X \text{, independence)} \\
&= \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^{n-k}}{(n-k)!} && \text{(Poisson PMFs)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-(\lambda + \mu)}}{n!} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{n!}{k! \thinspace (n-k)!} \lambda^k \mu^{n-k} && \text{(multiply and divide by } n! \text{)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-(\lambda + \mu)} (\lambda + \mu)^n}{n!} && \text{(binomial theorem)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the $\text{Pois}(\lambda + \mu)$ PMF. Summing two independent variables like
this is called a **convolution** ([13.4](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md)).

**Row 6, hypergeometric to binomial.** Write the hypergeometric PMF as (number of
orders) times (probability of one order). The chance of a specific order with $k$
special items first and then $n - k$ others is a product of fractions, and there are
$\binom{n}{k}$ orders, all with the same chance:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{K}{N} \cdot \frac{K-1}{N-1} \cdots \frac{K-k+1}{N-k+1}}_{k \text{ factors, each} \to p} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{N-K}{N-k} \cdots \frac{N-K-n+k+1}{N-n+1}}_{n - k \text{ factors, each} \to 1 - p}
$$

With $n$ and $k$ fixed and $N \to \infty$, the small shifts like $-1$ or $-k$ stop
mattering, so $P(X = k) \to \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.

**Row 7, splitting (thinning) a Poisson.** Let $N \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ be the number of
events, and keep each one with probability $p$, independently; write $q = 1 - p$. Given
$N = n$, the number kept, $K$, is $\text{Bin}(n, p)$. By the law of total probability
([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(K = k) &= \sum_{n=k}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^n}{n!} \cdot \frac{n!}{k! \thinspace (n-k)!} p^k q^{n-k} && \text{(total probability)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-\lambda} (\lambda p)^k}{k!} \sum_{n=k}^{\infty} \frac{(\lambda q)^{n-k}}{(n-k)!} && \text{(cancel } n! \text{, split } \lambda^n = \lambda^k \lambda^{n-k} \text{)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-\lambda} (\lambda p)^k}{k!} \thinspace e^{\lambda q} = \frac{e^{-\lambda p} (\lambda p)^k}{k!} && \text{(series for } e^{\lambda q} \text{, and } \lambda - \lambda q = \lambda p \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $K \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda p)$. Practice problem 5 shows the surprising part: the kept
and dropped counts are **independent**.

**Row 8, Poisson given the total.** Use the definition of conditional probability
and the result of row 3 in the denominator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X = k \mid X + Y = n) &= \frac{P(X = k) \thinspace P(Y = n - k)}{P(X + Y = n)} && \text{(independence on top)} \\
&= \frac{\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}}{\frac{e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}(\lambda+\mu)^n}{n!}} = \binom{n}{k} \frac{\lambda^k \mu^{n-k}}{(\lambda + \mu)^n} && \text{(the } e \text{ terms cancel)} \\
&= \binom{n}{k} \left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}\right)^k \left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda + \mu}\right)^{n-k} && \text{(split } (\lambda+\mu)^n \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**In words:** given the total, each event came from the first source with
probability $\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}$, independently. Rows 7 and 8 are two sides of the
same picture.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Call Centers Merged

**Problem.** Center A gets $\text{Pois}(3)$ calls per minute and center B gets
$\text{Pois}(5)$, independently. All calls go to one queue. What is the probability of
exactly 10 calls in a minute?

**Solution.**

1. By row 3, the total is $\text{Pois}(3 + 5) = \text{Pois}(8)$.
2. $P(\text{total} = 10) = e^{-8} \frac{8^{10}}{10!} = 0.00033546 \times \frac{1073741824}{3628800}$.
3. $\frac{1073741824}{3628800} \approx 295.89$, so the answer is $0.00033546 \times 295.89 \approx 0.0993$.

**Answer.** About $0.099$.

**Check.** 10 is close to the mean 8, so a probability near the top of the PMF (about
0.14 at $k = 7$ and $8$) but a bit lower makes sense.

### Example 2: Which Center Did the Calls Come From

**Problem.** In Example 1, a minute had exactly 10 calls. What is the probability
that exactly 4 of them came from center A?

**Solution.**

1. By row 8, given the total of 10, the number from A is
   $\text{Bin}(10, \frac{3}{3+5}) = \text{Bin}(10, 0.375)$.
2. $P = \binom{10}{4} (0.375)^4 (0.625)^6 = 210 \times 0.019775 \times 0.059605$.
3. $210 \times 0.019775 = 4.1528$, and $4.1528 \times 0.059605 \approx 0.2475$.

**Answer.** About $0.248$.

**Check.** The expected number from A is $10 \times 0.375 = 3.75$, so 4 is the most likely
value, and a probability near 0.25 is reasonable.

### Example 3: Spam in the Inbox

**Problem.** Emails arrive as $\text{Pois}(10)$ per hour. Each is spam with probability
0.2, independently. What is the distribution of spam per hour? What is the
probability of an hour with no spam? Does a busy hour of real email tell you anything
about the spam count?

**Solution.**

1. By row 7, spam per hour is $\text{Pois}(10 \times 0.2) = \text{Pois}(2)$.
2. $P(\text{no spam}) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$.
3. Real email is $\text{Pois}(10 \times 0.8) = \text{Pois}(8)$, and by row 7 it is
   **independent** of the spam count.

**Answer.** Spam is $\text{Pois}(2)$, an hour without spam has probability about
$0.135$, and the real-email count tells you nothing about the spam count.

**Check.** The two means add up: $2 + 8 = 10$. Independence is special to Poisson
arrivals. If the total were fixed at 10, more real email would mean less spam.

### Example 4: A Large Batch Behaves Like Sampling With Replacement

**Problem.** A batch has 1,000 items, 100 of them faulty. You test 5 without
replacement. Compare $P(\text{no faulty})$ with the binomial value.

**Solution.**

1. Exact: $X \sim \text{HGeom}(1000, 100, 5)$, and
   $P(X = 0) = \frac{900}{1000} \cdot \frac{899}{999} \cdot \frac{898}{998} \cdot \frac{897}{997} \cdot \frac{896}{996} \approx 0.5898$.
2. Binomial (row 6): $\text{Bin}(5, 0.1)$ gives $0.9^5 = 0.5905$.

**Answer.** $0.5898$ against $0.5905$: almost the same.

**Check.** Here $n = 5$ is only 0.5% of $N = 1000$, so removing items barely changes
the batch. Compare Example 2 of [7.6](7.6-Hypergeometric.md), where $n$ was 20% of $N$
and the difference was large.

## Simulate It

One script checks three rows of the table: Poisson plus Poisson (row 3), splitting a
Poisson (row 7), and Poisson given the total (row 8), using the numbers from the
examples.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb, exp, factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 4_000_000

# Row 3: Pois(3) + Pois(5) should be Pois(8).
a, b = rng.poisson(3, n_trials), rng.poisson(5, n_trials)
total = a + b
print(f"P(A+B = 10):      sim {np.mean(total == 10):.4f}, "
      f"exact {exp(-8) * 8**10 / factorial(10):.4f}")

# Row 8: given A + B = 10, A should be Bin(10, 3/8).
a_given = a[total == 10]
print(f"P(A=4 | total=10): sim {np.mean(a_given == 4):.4f}, "
      f"exact {comb(10, 4) * 0.375**4 * 0.625**6:.4f}")

# Row 7: keep each of Pois(10) emails with probability 0.2 (spam).
emails = rng.poisson(10, n_trials)
spam = rng.binomial(emails, 0.2)
ham = emails - spam
print(f"spam: mean {spam.mean():.4f}, var {spam.var():.4f} (both 2 for Pois(2))")
print(f"corr(spam, ham): {np.corrcoef(spam, ham)[0, 1]:.4f} (0 if independent)")
```

Output:

```text
P(A+B = 10):      sim 0.0991, exact 0.0993
P(A=4 | total=10): sim 0.2473, exact 0.2475
spam: mean 1.9992, var 2.0001 (both 2 for Pois(2))
corr(spam, ham): 0.0007 (0 if independent)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding binomials with different $p$.** $\text{Bin}(10, 0.3) + \text{Bin}(10, 0.5)$ is
  **not** binomial. Its mean is $3 + 5 = 8$, but its distribution has no simple name.
- **Forgetting independence.** Two counts that depend on each other (calls to two
  centers on the same stormy day) do not add up to a Poisson.
- **Thinking a sum of geometrics is geometric.** It is negative binomial (after
  subtracting 1 from each). A sum of $r$ waits is not one wait.
- **Subtracting Poissons.** $X - Y$ can be negative, so it cannot be Poisson. Only sums
  stay in the family.
- **Using the binomial for a hypergeometric when the sample is a large part of the
  population.** Row 6 needs $n$ to be small compared with $N$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** a load balancer that merges traffic from several sources
  (row 3), and network monitoring that samples each packet with probability $p$
  (row 7) both keep Poisson traffic Poisson. Splitting requests at random across
  servers gives each server its own independent Poisson stream.
- **AI and machine learning:** row 8 is used in models of count data: when a total
  count is known, its split among sources is binomial or multinomial. Choosing between
  Poisson and negative binomial models depends on whether counts are overdispersed.
- **Statistics:** large-population surveys use the binomial instead of the
  hypergeometric (row 6), and very rare events use the Poisson (row 5).
- **Biology:** mutations from two independent sources add as Poissons; detecting each
  mutation with probability $p$ is thinning.
- **Everyday life:** goals by two teams in a match, rainy days across two seasons, and
  customers from two entrances of a shop.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Bin}(10, 0.3)$ and $Y \sim \text{Bin}(15, 0.3)$ are independent.
What is the distribution of $X + Y$, and its mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By row 2, $X + Y \sim \text{Bin}(25, 0.3)$, with mean $25 \times 0.3 = 7.5$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** In a football match, the home team scores $\text{Pois}(1.5)$ goals and the
away team $\text{Pois}(1.1)$, independently. What is the probability of a 0-0 draw?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The total is $\text{Pois}(2.6)$ by row 3, and 0-0 means a total of 0:

$$P(0\text{-}0) = e^{-2.6} \approx 0.0743$$

(Or directly: $e^{-1.5} \cdot e^{-1.1} = e^{-2.6}$.)

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In the match of problem 2, there were 3 goals in total. What is the
probability that the home team scored all 3?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By row 8, home goals given a total of 3 are $\text{Bin}(3, \frac{1.5}{2.6})$:

$$P = \left(\frac{1.5}{2.6}\right)^3 \approx 0.5769^3 \approx 0.1920$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X \sim \text{Geom}(p_1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Geom}(p_2)$ are independent. Show
that $\min(X, Y)$ is geometric, and find its parameter. Two people each roll a die
until they get a 6, rolling at the same time. What is the distribution of the
number of rounds until the first 6 appears?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $q_i = 1 - p_i$, the minimum is bigger than $k$ only when both are:

$$P(\min(X, Y) \gt k) = P(X \gt k) P(Y \gt k) = q_1^k q_2^k = (q_1 q_2)^k$$

That is the tail of $\text{Geom}(1 - q_1 q_2)$. For two dice, $q_1 q_2 = \frac{25}{36}$, so the
number of rounds is $\text{Geom}(\frac{11}{36})$, with mean $\frac{36}{11} \approx 3.27$ rounds.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** In row 7, let $K$ be the kept events and $D = N - K$ the dropped events.
Show that $P(K = k, D = d) = P(K = k) \thinspace P(D = d)$ for all $k, d \ge 0$, so $K$ and
$D$ are independent Poisson counts.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$K = k$ and $D = d$ happen exactly when $N = k + d$ and $k$ of those events are kept:

$$P(K = k, D = d) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^{k+d}}{(k+d)!} \cdot \frac{(k+d)!}{k! \thinspace d!} p^k q^d = \frac{e^{-\lambda} (\lambda p)^k (\lambda q)^d}{k! \thinspace d!}$$

Since $e^{-\lambda} = e^{-\lambda p} e^{-\lambda q}$, this splits into two factors:

$$P(K = k, D = d) = \frac{e^{-\lambda p} (\lambda p)^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{e^{-\lambda q} (\lambda q)^d}{d!}$$

The first factor is the $\text{Pois}(\lambda p)$ PMF and the second is the
$\text{Pois}(\lambda q)$ PMF. Summing over $d$ shows the first factor is $P(K = k)$, and
summing over $k$ shows the second is $P(D = d)$. So the joint PMF is the product of
the two, and $K$ and $D$ are independent.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 3 by simulating many matches and looking only at
those with exactly 3 goals.


```text

```

</details>

## Summary

- Sums of independent counts stay in the family: Bernoullis make a binomial, binomials
  with the same $p$ make a binomial, Poissons make a Poisson, geometric waits make a
  negative binomial.
- Limits connect the family: $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{\lambda}{n}) \to \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ and
  $\text{HGeom}(N, K, n) \to \text{Bin}(n, \frac{K}{N})$.
- A Poisson count can be split into independent Poisson counts, and given the total,
  the split is binomial.
- Special cases link the rest: $\text{Bern} = \text{Bin}(1, p)$, categorical with $k = 2$ is
  Bernoulli, multinomial counts are binomial one at a time.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda), \ Y \sim \text{Pois}(\mu) \text{ independent} \implies X + Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda + \mu)}
$$
