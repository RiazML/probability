# 7.4 Negative Binomial

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.4 Negative Binomial

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [7.3 Geometric](7.3-Geometric.md)

> **Big idea:** The negative binomial distribution counts the failures you see before the $r$-th success, and it is a sum of $r$ independent geometric waits.

---

## Why It Matters

The geometric distribution waits for **one** success. Often we need several: a
salesperson needs 5 sales to meet a target, a team needs 4 wins to take a series,
a test engineer needs 3 failures to stop a test. The **negative binomial** counts
how long such a wait takes. It is also the standard model for counts that vary
**more** than a Poisson count does, such as website visits per day or insurance
claims, so it appears often in data science and machine learning.

## Intuition

A basketball player makes each shot with probability 0.4. She keeps shooting until
she has made 3 shots. How many misses will she have along the way?

Think of the shots as a line of M (made) and X (missed). One possible line with 2
misses is:

```text
   X  M  X  M  M
   ^           ^
   misses can  the last shot is
   be anywhere always the 3rd make
```

The **last** shot must be a make: it is the one that ends the game. Before it there
are 4 shots, with exactly 2 makes and 2 misses, in any order. There are
$\binom{4}{2} = 6$ such orders. Each full line has 3 makes and 2 misses, so it has
probability $0.4^3 \times 0.6^2$. The answer is $6 \times 0.4^3 \times 0.6^2$.

There is a second way to see the same wait. The player waits for her first make
(a geometric wait), then starts fresh and waits for the second, then for the third.
The total wait is three independent geometric waits added together.

## Definition

Do independent trials, each a success with probability $p$, until you have $r$
successes. Let $X$ be the **number of failures** before the $r$-th success. Then $X$
has the **negative binomial distribution**, written $X \sim \text{NegBin}(r, p)$,
where $r = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ is the number of successes we wait for and $p$ is the
success probability of each trial. Its PMF is:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{k + r - 1}{k} p^r (1 - p)^k, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

where:

- $k + r$ is the total number of trials, and the last one is the $r$-th success;
- $\binom{k + r - 1}{k}$ counts the ways to place the $k$ failures among the first
  $k + r - 1$ trials;
- $p^r (1 - p)^k$ is the chance of one such line of $r$ successes and $k$ failures.

**In words:** the last trial is a success, and the trials before it hold the other
$r - 1$ successes and all $k$ failures, in any order.

The support is $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$. The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = \frac{r(1 - p)}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{r(1 - p)}{p^2}
$$

**In words:** each of the $r$ successes costs, on average, $\frac{1-p}{p}$ failures.

> **Warning:** Some books count the total **trials** $T = X + r$ instead, with
> support $\lbrace r, r+1, \dots \rbrace$ and mean $\frac{r}{p}$. In this book
> $\text{NegBin}(r, p)$ counts **failures**. numpy's `rng.negative_binomial(r, p)` and
> `scipy.stats.nbinom(r, p)` count failures too.

With $r = 1$ the negative binomial is the number of failures before the first
success, which is $\text{Geom}(p) - 1$ ([7.3](7.3-Geometric.md)).

## Why It Is True

Write $q = 1 - p$.

**The PMF.** The event $X = k$ happens exactly when trial number $k + r$ is a
success **and** the first $k + r - 1$ trials contain exactly $r - 1$ successes and
$k$ failures. There are $\binom{k+r-1}{k}$ ways to choose where the $k$ failures go.
Each complete line has $r$ successes and $k$ failures, so by independence it has
probability $p^r q^k$.

**The PMF sums to 1.** Each of the $r$ geometric waits below ends with
probability 1 ([7.3](7.3-Geometric.md)), so the $r$-th success comes eventually, and
the values $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ cover every way it can happen.

**Mean and variance, as a sum of geometrics.** Let $Y_1$ be the number of failures
before the first success, $Y_2$ the number of failures between the first and second
success, and so on up to $Y_r$. After each success the trials start fresh, so
$Y_1, \dots, Y_r$ are independent, and each one is "failures before one success",
that is $\text{Geom}(p) - 1$. From [7.3](7.3-Geometric.md):

$$
E[Y_i] = \frac{1}{p} - 1 = \frac{q}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \frac{q}{p^2}
$$

The total number of failures is $X = Y_1 + Y_2 + \dots + Y_r$. Linearity gives the mean:

$$
E[X] = E[Y_1] + \dots + E[Y_r] = r \cdot \frac{q}{p}
$$

For the variance we use one fact from a later module: the variance of a sum of
**independent** variables is the sum of their variances
([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)). So:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \operatorname{Var}(Y_1) + \dots + \operatorname{Var}(Y_r) = r \cdot \frac{q}{p^2}
$$

<details>
<summary>Full proof without the variance-of-a-sum rule (optional)</summary>

Use the identity $k \binom{k+r-1}{k} = r \binom{k+r-1}{k-1}$, which you can check by
writing both sides with factorials. Shift $j = k - 1$ and notice a
$\text{NegBin}(r+1, p)$ PMF, which sums to 1:

$$E[X] = \sum_{k \ge 1} r \binom{k+r-1}{k-1} p^r q^k = \frac{rq}{p} \sum_{j \ge 0} \binom{j+r}{j} p^{r+1} q^{j} = \frac{rq}{p}$$

In the same way, $k(k-1)\binom{k+r-1}{k} = r(r+1)\binom{k+r-1}{k-2}$, and a
$\text{NegBin}(r+2, p)$ PMF appears:

$$E[X(X-1)] = r(r+1) \frac{q^2}{p^2} \sum_{j \ge 0} \binom{j+r+1}{j} p^{r+2} q^j = \frac{r(r+1)q^2}{p^2}$$

Then, using $E[X^2] = E[X(X-1)] + E[X]$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{r(r+1)q^2}{p^2} + \frac{rq}{p} - \frac{r^2 q^2}{p^2} = \frac{rq^2}{p^2} + \frac{rq}{p} = \frac{rq(q + p)}{p^2} = \frac{rq}{p^2}$$

</details>

**More spread than a Poisson.** Write $\mu = E[X] = \frac{rq}{p}$. Then
$\frac{1}{p} = 1 + \frac{\mu}{r}$, and:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{rq}{p} \cdot \frac{1}{p} = \mu \left(1 + \frac{\mu}{r}\right) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{r}
$$

**In words:** the variance is always bigger than the mean, by $\frac{\mu^2}{r}$. A
Poisson count has variance equal to its mean ([7.5](7.5-Poisson.md)), so the
negative binomial is the natural model for **overdispersed** counts, which means
counts that vary more than a Poisson count would.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Three Made Shots

**Problem.** A player makes each shot with probability 0.4, independently. She
shoots until she has made 3 shots. Find the probability of exactly 2 misses, and
the mean and standard deviation of the number of misses. How many shots does she
take on average?

**Solution.**

1. Misses before the 3rd make: $X \sim \text{NegBin}(3, 0.4)$, so $r = 3$, $p = 0.4$,
   $q = 0.6$.
2. $P(X = 2) = \binom{4}{2} (0.4)^3 (0.6)^2 = 6 \times 0.064 \times 0.36 = 0.13824$.
3. $E[X] = \frac{3 \times 0.6}{0.4} = \frac{1.8}{0.4} = 4.5$ misses.
4. $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{3 \times 0.6}{0.4^2} = \frac{1.8}{0.16} = 11.25$, so
   $\sigma = \sqrt{11.25} \approx 3.35$.
5. Total shots: $E[X + 3] = 4.5 + 3 = 7.5$, which is also $\frac{r}{p} = \frac{3}{0.4}$.

**Answer.** $P(X = 2) \approx 0.138$. She misses 4.5 times on average (standard
deviation about 3.35) and takes 7.5 shots on average.

**Check.** One make takes $\frac{1}{0.4} = 2.5$ shots on average, so three makes
take $3 \times 2.5 = 7.5$ shots. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: A Best-of-Seven Series

**Problem.** Team A wins each game against team B with probability 0.6,
independently. The first team to win 4 games wins the series. What is the
probability that team A wins the series?

**Solution.**

1. Team A wins the series exactly when it gets its 4th win before its 4th loss.
   Let $X$ be A's losses before A's 4th win, if the teams kept playing forever. Then
   $X \sim \text{NegBin}(4, 0.6)$, and A wins the series when $X \le 3$.
2. Compute each term $\binom{k+3}{k} 0.6^4 \thinspace 0.4^k$, with $0.6^4 = 0.1296$:
   - $k = 0$: $\binom{3}{0} = 1$, term $= 0.1296 \times 1 = 0.1296$
   - $k = 1$: $\binom{4}{1} = 4$, term $= 0.1296 \times 4 \times 0.4 = 0.20736$
   - $k = 2$: $\binom{5}{2} = 10$, term $= 0.1296 \times 10 \times 0.16 = 0.20736$
   - $k = 3$: $\binom{6}{3} = 20$, term $= 0.1296 \times 20 \times 0.064 = 0.165888$
3. Add: $0.1296 + 0.20736 + 0.20736 + 0.165888 = 0.710208$.

**Answer.** Team A wins the series with probability about $0.710$.

**Check.** A best-of-seven series helps the better team: 0.6 per game becomes
0.71 per series. Imagining the extra games that are never played does not change
the answer, because the series is already decided before them.

### Example 3: Sales Calls to Meet a Target

**Problem.** A salesperson closes each call with probability 0.2, independently.
The target is 5 sales. Find the mean and standard deviation of the number of
failed calls, and the probability of reaching the target with at most 10 failed
calls.

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{NegBin}(5, 0.2)$, with $q = 0.8$.
2. $E[X] = \frac{5 \times 0.8}{0.2} = 20$ failed calls, so $25$ calls in total on average.
3. $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{5 \times 0.8}{0.04} = 100$, so $\sigma = 10$.
4. "At most 10 failures before the 5th sale" means "at least 5 sales in the first
   15 calls". So $P(X \le 10) = P(B \ge 5)$ with $B \sim \text{Bin}(15, 0.2)$.
   Summing 11 terms by hand is slow; with
   `scipy.stats.nbinom.cdf(10, 5, 0.2)` we get $0.1642$.

**Answer.** 20 failed calls on average, standard deviation 10, and only about a
16% chance of reaching the target with 10 or fewer failed calls.

**Check.** The overdispersion formula gives the same variance:
$\mu + \frac{\mu^2}{r} = 20 + \frac{400}{5} = 100$.

## Simulate It

We sample Example 1 in two ways: with numpy's negative binomial sampler, and as a
sum of 3 independent "geometric minus 1" waits. Both should match
$P(X = 2) = 0.13824$, mean 4.5 and variance 11.25.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
r, p, n_trials = 3, 0.4, 1_000_000

# Way 1: numpy counts failures before the r-th success.
x1 = rng.negative_binomial(r, p, size=n_trials)

# Way 2: add r independent waits; each is (trials until success) - 1.
x2 = (rng.geometric(p, size=(n_trials, r)) - 1).sum(axis=1)

for name, x in [("negative_binomial", x1), ("sum of geometrics", x2)]:
    print(f"{name:18s} P(X=2) {np.mean(x == 2):.4f}  "
          f"mean {x.mean():.4f}  var {x.var():.4f}")
print(f"{'Exact':18s} P(X=2) {0.13824:.4f}  mean {r * (1 - p) / p:.4f}  "
      f"var {r * (1 - p) / p**2:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
negative_binomial  P(X=2) 0.1380  mean 4.4973  var 11.2096
sum of geometrics  P(X=2) 0.1383  mean 4.4995  var 11.2642
Exact              P(X=2) 0.1382  mean 4.5000  var 11.2500
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up the conventions.** Failures (support from 0, mean $\frac{rq}{p}$) or
  trials (support from $r$, mean $\frac{r}{p}$)? State which one you use.
- **Using $\binom{k+r}{k}$ instead of $\binom{k+r-1}{k}$.** The last trial is fixed as
  a success, so only the first $k + r - 1$ trials can be arranged.
- **Confusing it with the binomial.** The binomial fixes the number of **trials**
  and counts successes. The negative binomial fixes the number of **successes** and
  counts failures.
- **Forgetting that the waits must be independent with the same $p$.** If a
  salesperson gets better with practice, $p$ changes and the formula fails.

## Where It Is Used

- **Data science and AI:** counts with more spread than a Poisson, such as daily
  visits to a web page, word counts in documents, and gene expression counts in
  biology, are modeled with a negative binomial. It can be seen as a Poisson whose
  rate is itself random ([10.8 Mixture Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)),
  and in that form $r$ may be any positive number, not only a whole number.
- **Insurance:** the number of claims per customer varies more than a Poisson
  allows, because some customers are riskier than others.
- **Sports:** "best of $n$ games" series, as in Example 2.
- **Testing and reliability:** run tests until $r$ failures are seen, then estimate
  the failure rate.
- **Everyday life:** how many rejections before 3 job offers, how many rainy days
  before 5 sunny ones.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Flip a fair coin until you get 2 heads. Let $X$ be the number of tails.
Find $P(X = 0)$, $P(X = 1)$ and $E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X \sim \text{NegBin}(2, 0.5)$.

$$P(X = 0) = \binom{1}{0} 0.5^2 = 0.25, \qquad P(X = 1) = \binom{2}{1} 0.5^2 \times 0.5 = 0.25$$

$$E[X] = \frac{2 \times 0.5}{0.5} = 2$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{NegBin}(r, p)$ has mean 6 and variance 12. Find $r$ and $p$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Divide: $\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{E[X]} = \frac{rq/p^2}{rq/p} = \frac{1}{p} = \frac{12}{6} = 2$, so $p = 0.5$.
Then $\frac{r \times 0.5}{0.5} = r = 6$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Explain in words why $P(X \le k) = P(B \ge r)$, where
$X \sim \text{NegBin}(r, p)$ and $B \sim \text{Bin}(k + r, p)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Imagine the trials go on forever. "At most $k$ failures before the $r$-th success"
means the $r$-th success happens by trial $k + r$. That is the same as "the first
$k + r$ trials contain at least $r$ successes". The number of successes in the first
$k + r$ trials is $B \sim \text{Bin}(k + r, p)$. So the two events are equal, and so
are their probabilities.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $T = X + r$ be the total number of trials needed for $r$
successes. Find the PMF of $T$ and its mean.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$T = t$ means $X = t - r$, so for $t = r, r+1, r+2, \dots$:

$$P(T = t) = \binom{t-1}{t-r} p^r q^{t-r} = \binom{t-1}{r-1} p^r q^{t-r}$$

The mean is $E[T] = E[X] + r = \frac{rq}{p} + r = \frac{rq + rp}{p} = \frac{r}{p}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Two equal teams ($p = 0.5$) play a best-of-seven series. Find the
probability that the series lasts exactly 4, 5, 6 and 7 games, and the expected
number of games.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The series ends after game $g$ when one team gets its 4th win in game $g$. For a
given team that is $\text{NegBin}(4, 0.5)$ with $g - 4$ losses, and either team can
be the winner, so we multiply by 2:

$$P(G = g) = 2 \binom{g-1}{g-4} 0.5^{g}$$

- $g = 4$: $2 \times 1 \times \frac{1}{16} = 0.125$
- $g = 5$: $2 \times 4 \times \frac{1}{32} = 0.25$
- $g = 6$: $2 \times 10 \times \frac{1}{64} = 0.3125$
- $g = 7$: $2 \times 20 \times \frac{1}{128} = 0.3125$

They add up to 1. The expected number of games is

$$E[G] = 4(0.125) + 5(0.25) + 6(0.3125) + 7(0.3125) = 0.5 + 1.25 + 1.875 + 2.1875 = 5.8125$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Example 2 (team A wins each game with probability 0.6)
by playing 7 games and checking whether A wins at least 4. Why does that give the
same answer as the real series?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
wins = rng.binomial(7, 0.6, size=1_000_000)     # play all 7 games every time
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(wins >= 4):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {0.710208:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.7101
Exact:      0.7102
```

Playing the unneeded games does not change who reaches 4 wins first: the team with
at least 4 of the 7 wins is the team that got to 4 first. So
$P(\text{NegBin}(4, 0.6) \le 3) = P(\text{Bin}(7, 0.6) \ge 4)$, as in problem 3.

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{NegBin}(r, p)$ counts the failures before the $r$-th success; its support is
  $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$.
- PMF: the last trial is a success, so $\binom{k+r-1}{k} p^r (1-p)^k$.
- It is a sum of $r$ independent "geometric minus 1" waits, which gives the mean
  $\frac{r(1-p)}{p}$ and the variance $\frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}$.
- Its variance $\mu + \frac{\mu^2}{r}$ is bigger than its mean, so it models
  overdispersed counts.
- In numpy, sample it with `rng.negative_binomial(r, p, size)`, which also counts
  failures.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X = k) = \binom{k + r - 1}{k} p^r (1 - p)^k, \quad E[X] = \frac{r(1-p)}{p}}
$$
