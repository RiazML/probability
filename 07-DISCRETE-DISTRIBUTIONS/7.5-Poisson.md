# 7.5 Poisson

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.5 Poisson

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [7.4 Negative Binomial](7.4-Negative-Binomial.md)

> **Big idea:** The Poisson distribution counts how many rare, independent events happen in a fixed window of time or space, and one number $\lambda$, the average count, fixes everything about it.

---

## Why It Matters

How many requests hit a server in one second? How many calls reach a call center in
one minute? How many typos are on a page? In each case there are a huge number of
moments or places where an event **could** happen, but each one has a tiny chance.
The **Poisson distribution** is the model for such counts. It needs only the
average count, so it is easy to use, and it is the starting point for queues and
the Poisson process ([18.5](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.5-Poisson-Process.md)).

## Intuition

A call center gets 3 calls per minute on average. Cut one minute into 60 seconds.
In each second a call comes with a small chance, about $\frac{3}{60} = 0.05$, and the
seconds behave independently. Then the number of calls in the minute is
$\text{Bin}(60, 0.05)$.

Now cut the minute into 6,000 hundredths of a second, each with chance
$\frac{3}{6000} = 0.0005$. That is $\text{Bin}(6000, 0.0005)$. As the pieces get
smaller, the binomial settles down to one fixed distribution. That limit is the
Poisson distribution with mean 3. The table shows $P(\text{no calls})$ as the
pieces shrink:

| Pieces $n$ | Chance per piece | $P(\text{0 calls}) = (1 - 3/n)^n$ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.3 | 0.0282 |
| 100 | 0.03 | 0.0476 |
| 1,000 | 0.003 | 0.0496 |
| limit | | $e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$ |

So we never need $n$ or $p$: only their product, the average count $\lambda = 3$.
The full proof is in [7.10 Poisson Approximation to Binomial](7.10-Poisson-Approximation-to-Binomial.md).

## Definition

A random variable $X$ has the **Poisson distribution** with **rate** $\lambda \gt 0$,
written $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$, if:

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

where:

- $\lambda$ is the average number of events in the window (here λ is the rate,
  and it is also the mean);
- $k!$ is $k$ factorial, with $0! = 1$;
- $e \approx 2.71828$, and $e^{-\lambda}$ is exactly the probability of zero events.

**In words:** the chance of $k$ events is $e^{-\lambda}$ times $\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}$, the
$k$-th term of the series for $e^{\lambda}$.

The support is $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$, with no upper limit. The mean and
variance are **both** $\lambda$:

$$
E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda
$$

**In words:** a Poisson count with average 9 has variance 9 and standard
deviation 3.

**Rate times length.** If events happen at an average rate of $r$ per unit of time
(or length, or area), the count in a window of size $t$ is $\text{Pois}(rt)$.
For example, 3 calls per minute gives $\text{Pois}(15)$ for a 5-minute window.

The Poisson model fits when the events are **rare** in each small piece of the
window, happen **independently** of each other, and happen at a **steady**
average rate.

## Why It Is True

We use one fact from calculus, the series for $e^{\lambda}$:

$$
e^{\lambda} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = 1 + \lambda + \frac{\lambda^2}{2!} + \frac{\lambda^3}{3!} + \dots
$$

**The PMF sums to 1.**

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda} = 1
$$

**The mean.** The $k = 0$ term is zero. For $k \ge 1$, cancel $k$ against $k!$ to
get $(k-1)!$, then set $j = k - 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k \thinspace \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} && \text{(the } k = 0 \text{ term is 0)} \\
&= \lambda e^{-\lambda} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} && \text{(since } k/k! = 1/(k-1)! \text{)} \\
&= \lambda e^{-\lambda} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^{j}}{j!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda} = \lambda && \text{(series for } e^{\lambda} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The variance.** It is easiest to find $E[X(X-1)]$ first, with the same trick
(cancel $k(k-1)$ against $k!$ and set $j = k - 2$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X(X-1)] &= \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k(k-1) \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda^2 e^{-\lambda} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^j}{j!} = \lambda^2 && \text{(same steps)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X(X-1)] + E[X] - (E[X])^2 && \text{(since } X^2 = X(X-1) + X \text{)} \\
&= \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda
\end{aligned}
$$

**The shape.** Divide neighbouring terms:

$$
\frac{P(X = k)}{P(X = k-1)} = \frac{\lambda^k / k!}{\lambda^{k-1} / (k-1)!} = \frac{\lambda}{k}
$$

So the bars rise while $k \lt \lambda$ and fall when $k \gt \lambda$. The mode is
$\lfloor \lambda \rfloor$. When $\lambda$ is a whole number, the bars at $\lambda - 1$
and $\lambda$ tie, as in the picture. For small $\lambda$ the PMF is skewed to the
right; for large $\lambda$ it looks like a bell centred at $\lambda$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Calls in One Minute

**Problem.** A call center gets 4 calls per minute on average. Find the probability
of no calls in a given minute, and of at most 2 calls.

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{Pois}(4)$, and $e^{-4} \approx 0.018316$.
2. $P(X = 0) = e^{-4} \frac{4^0}{0!} = e^{-4} \approx 0.0183$.
3. $P(X = 1) = e^{-4} \frac{4^1}{1!} = 4e^{-4}$ and $P(X = 2) = e^{-4} \frac{4^2}{2!} = 8e^{-4}$.
4. $P(X \le 2) = e^{-4}(1 + 4 + 8) = 13 e^{-4} = 13 \times 0.018316 \approx 0.2381$.

**Answer.** $P(X = 0) \approx 0.018$ and $P(X \le 2) \approx 0.238$.

**Check.** With an average of 4, a quiet minute with at most 2 calls should be
unusual but not rare. About 1 minute in 4 is that quiet.

### Example 2: Can the Server Keep Up

**Problem.** A server gets 3 requests per second on average and can handle at most 6
in one second. Requests arrive independently. In what fraction of seconds is the
server overloaded? About how many overloaded seconds are there in a day?

**Solution.**

1. Requests in one second: $X \sim \text{Pois}(3)$, and $e^{-3} \approx 0.049787$.
   Overload means $X \ge 7$, so compute $P(X \le 6)$ and subtract from 1.
2. The terms $e^{-3} \frac{3^k}{k!}$ for $k = 0, \dots, 6$ are:

| $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\frac{3^k}{k!}$ | 1 | 3 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 3.375 | 2.025 | 1.0125 |
| $P(X = k)$ | 0.0498 | 0.1494 | 0.2240 | 0.2240 | 0.1680 | 0.1008 | 0.0504 |

3. The sum of $\frac{3^k}{k!}$ is $19.4125$, so $P(X \le 6) = 19.4125 \times 0.049787 \approx 0.9665$.
4. $P(X \ge 7) = 1 - 0.9665 = 0.0335$.
5. A day has 86,400 seconds, so about $86400 \times 0.0335 \approx 2895$ seconds are
   overloaded.

**Answer.** About 3.4% of seconds, or roughly 2,900 seconds (48 minutes) per day.

**Check.** The capacity 6 is $\frac{6 - 3}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.7$ standard deviations above
the mean, so overload is not rare. Capacity planning must look at the tail, not
only at the average load.

### Example 3: Typos in a Report

**Problem.** A writer makes 0.5 typos per page on average. What is the probability
that a 10-page report has no typos at all?

**Solution.**

1. Use rate times length: $\lambda = 0.5 \times 10 = 5$ typos for the whole report.
2. $X \sim \text{Pois}(5)$, so $P(X = 0) = e^{-5} \approx 0.0067$.

**Answer.** About 0.7%, less than 1 report in 100.

**Check.** Per page, $P(\text{no typo}) = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065$. If pages are
independent, all 10 are clean with probability $(e^{-0.5})^{10} = e^{-5}$. Same answer.

## Simulate It

We sample $\text{Pois}(3)$ and compare the first few PMF values, the mean and the
variance with the exact values. Mean and variance should both be close to 3.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import exp, factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
lam, n_trials = 3, 1_000_000
x = rng.poisson(lam, size=n_trials)     # counts in 1,000,000 windows

for k in range(7):
    exact = exp(-lam) * lam**k / factorial(k)
    print(f"P(X={k}): sim {np.mean(x == k):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"mean {x.mean():.4f}, variance {x.var():.4f}, exact both {lam}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X=0): sim 0.0499, exact 0.0498
P(X=1): sim 0.1497, exact 0.1494
P(X=2): sim 0.2240, exact 0.2240
P(X=3): sim 0.2241, exact 0.2240
P(X=4): sim 0.1681, exact 0.1680
P(X=5): sim 0.1005, exact 0.1008
P(X=6): sim 0.0499, exact 0.0504
mean 2.9976, variance 2.9987, exact both 3
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the wrong window.** If $\lambda$ is "per hour" and the question is about
  15 minutes, use $\lambda = \frac{15}{60} \times \text{rate}$. Always match the
  rate to the window.
- **Using it when events come in clusters.** If one event makes more events likely
  (a viral post, a chain of server errors), the count varies more than a Poisson
  count. A sign is variance much bigger than the mean; then try the negative
  binomial ([7.4](7.4-Negative-Binomial.md)).
- **Thinking $\lambda$ must be a whole number.** $\lambda = 2.5$ goals per match is
  fine. Only the counts $k$ are whole numbers.
- **Forgetting $k = 0$.** "At least one event" is $1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - e^{-\lambda}$.
- **Using it for counts with a hard maximum and a large chance.** "Heads in 10
  fair flips" is binomial. Poisson works for many trials with small chances.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** requests to a web server, packets at a router, and jobs
  in a queue ([21.3.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
  In a hash table with load factor $\alpha$, the number of keys in one bucket is
  close to $\text{Pois}(\alpha)$.
- **AI and machine learning:** **Poisson regression** predicts counts (clicks,
  sales, visits) by letting $\lambda$ depend on features. Word counts in long
  documents are often modeled with Poisson or negative binomial counts.
- **Imaging and physics:** photons hitting a camera pixel, which causes "shot
  noise" in dark photos, and radioactive decays per second.
- **Biology and medicine:** mutations in a stretch of DNA, cases of a rare disease
  per year in a city.
- **Everyday life:** goals in a football match, emails per hour, cars passing a
  point on a quiet road.

<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X = 0) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353, \qquad P(X = 2) = e^{-2} \frac{2^2}{2!} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.2707$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A website gets 120 visits per hour on average. What is the
distribution of the number of visits in one minute? What is the chance of no
visits in a minute?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The rate is $\frac{120}{60} = 2$ visits per minute, so the count is $\text{Pois}(2)$ and

$$P(X = 0) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The number of goals in a football match is $\text{Pois}(2.5)$. What is
the probability of at least 3 goals?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the complement $X \le 2$:

$$P(X \le 2) = e^{-2.5}\left(1 + 2.5 + \frac{2.5^2}{2}\right) = 6.625 \thinspace e^{-2.5} \approx 6.625 \times 0.082085 \approx 0.5438$$

So $P(X \ge 3) \approx 1 - 0.5438 = 0.4562$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Find the mode of $\text{Pois}(3.5)$ and of $\text{Pois}(0.8)$. Explain
using the ratio $\frac{P(X = k)}{P(X = k - 1)} = \frac{\lambda}{k}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The bars rise while $\frac{\lambda}{k} \gt 1$, that is while $k \lt \lambda$. For
$\lambda = 3.5$ they rise up to $k = 3$ and then fall, so the mode is 3. For
$\lambda = 0.8$ the ratio is already below 1 at $k = 1$, so the bars fall from the
start and the mode is 0.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$. Show that
$E\left[\frac{1}{X + 1}\right] = \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use LOTUS ([6.3](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.3-LOTUS.md)) and note
$(k+1) \cdot k! = (k+1)!$:

$$E\left[\frac{1}{X+1}\right] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k+1} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} = \frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^{k+1}}{(k+1)!} = \frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda}\left(e^{\lambda} - 1\right) = \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda}$$

The sum is the series for $e^{\lambda}$ without its first term, 1.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 5 for $\lambda = 2$ with 1,000,000 samples.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
lam = 2
x = rng.poisson(lam, size=1_000_000)

print(f"Exact:      {(1 - np.exp(-lam)) / lam:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text

Exact:      0.4323
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ counts rare, independent events in a fixed window; λ is the
  average count.
- PMF: $\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, with no upper limit.
- Mean and variance are both $\lambda$; the proofs use the series for $e^{\lambda}$.
- Scale the rate to the window: rate $r$ for length $t$ gives $\text{Pois}(rt)$.
- In numpy, sample it with `rng.poisson(lam, size)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}, \quad E[X] = \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda}
$$
