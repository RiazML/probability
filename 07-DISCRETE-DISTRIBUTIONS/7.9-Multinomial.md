# 7.9 Multinomial

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.9 Multinomial

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [7.8 Categorical](7.8-Categorical.md)

> **Big idea:** The multinomial distribution counts how many times each of $k$ categories appears in $n$ independent categorical draws; it is the binomial with more than two outcomes.

---

## Why It Matters

The binomial counts one kind of success in $n$ trials. Often there are more than
two kinds: votes for 3 candidates in a poll of 1,000 people, the 6 faces in 60 rolls
of a die, the words of a vocabulary in a document. The **multinomial distribution**
gives the probability of every possible set of counts. It is the model behind
election polls, the "bag of words" view of text, and goodness-of-fit tests.

## Intuition

Ask 10 random voters which of three candidates they like. Candidate A has 50%
support, B has 30% and C has 20%. One possible result is "5 for A, 3 for B, 2 for C".
How likely is it?

Look at one specific order of answers, say AAAAABBBCC. Each answer is independent,
so this order has probability $0.5^5 \times 0.3^3 \times 0.2^2$. But the answers could
come in many other orders, such as ABACABABCA, and each order with 5 A's, 3 B's and
2 C's has the same probability. So we need to count those orders: that is the
**multinomial coefficient** $\frac{10!}{5! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 2!} = 2520$ from
[2.6](../02-COUNTING/2.6-Multinomial-Coefficients.md). The answer is (number of orders)
times (probability of one order), exactly as for the binomial.

## Picture

```text
   n = 10 independent draws, each one lands in one box:

        draw 1   draw 2   draw 3            draw 10
          |        |        |                 |
          v        v        v                 v
    +-----------------+-----------------+-----------------+
    |   box A         |   box B         |   box C         |
    |   p1 = 0.5      |   p2 = 0.3      |   p3 = 0.2      |
    |   o o o o o     |   o o o         |   o o           |
    +-----------------+-----------------+-----------------+
         X1 = 5            X2 = 3            X3 = 2

    The counts always add up to n:  X1 + X2 + X3 = 10
```

Each of the $n$ balls falls into box $j$ with probability $p_j$; the multinomial is
the distribution of the vector of box counts.

## Definition

Do $n$ independent draws from $\text{Cat}(p_1, \dots, p_k)$ ([7.8](7.8-Categorical.md)).
Let $X_j$ be the number of draws that land in category $j$. Then the vector
$(X_1, \dots, X_k)$ has the **multinomial distribution**, written
$(X_1, \dots, X_k) \sim \text{Mult}(n, p_1, \dots, p_k)$, where $n$ is the number of
draws and $p_1, \dots, p_k$ are the category probabilities (they add up to 1). Its
PMF is:

$$
P(X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_k = x_k) = \frac{n!}{x_1! \thinspace x_2! \cdots x_k!} \thinspace p_1^{x_1} p_2^{x_2} \cdots p_k^{x_k}
$$

for whole numbers $x_j \ge 0$ with $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_k = n$. Any other set of counts
has probability 0.

**In words:** the number of orders that give these counts, times the probability
of one such order.

Each single count is binomial, so:

$$
X_j \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_j), \qquad E[X_j] = n p_j, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_j) = n p_j (1 - p_j)
$$

**In words:** to study one category, call it "success" and lump all other
categories together as "failure".

**Special cases.** With $k = 2$, the vector is $(X_1, n - X_1)$ and $X_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_1)$.
With $n = 1$, the vector is the one-hot encoding of a single categorical draw.

## Why It Is True

**The PMF.** Fix one order of the $n$ answers with $x_1$ answers in category 1, $x_2$
in category 2, and so on. By independence its probability is
$p_1^{x_1} p_2^{x_2} \cdots p_k^{x_k}$. Every such order has the same probability. The
number of orders is the number of ways to arrange $n$ items of which $x_1$ are of
type 1, $x_2$ of type 2, and so on:

$$
\binom{n}{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k} = \frac{n!}{x_1! \thinspace x_2! \cdots x_k!}
$$

Different orders are disjoint events, so we add their probabilities.

**The PMF sums to 1.** By the multinomial theorem, which is the binomial theorem
with $k$ terms,

$$
\sum_{x_1 + \dots + x_k = n} \frac{n!}{x_1! \cdots x_k!} \thinspace p_1^{x_1} \cdots p_k^{x_k} = (p_1 + \dots + p_k)^n = 1^n = 1
$$

**Each count is binomial.** Each draw lands in category $j$ with probability $p_j$, or
not, independently of the other draws. So $X_j$ counts successes in $n$ independent
trials with success probability $p_j$, and $X_j \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_j)$
([7.2](7.2-Binomial.md)). The mean and variance follow.

**Merging categories.** By the same argument, $X_1 + X_2$ counts draws that land in
"category 1 or 2", so $X_1 + X_2 \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_1 + p_2)$. Merging categories in a
multinomial gives another multinomial with fewer categories.

**The counts are not independent.** They must add up to $n$. If A gets more votes,
B and C together must get fewer. So the counts are **negatively related**.

<details>
<summary>How strong is the negative relation (optional)</summary>

For $i \ne j$, write $X_i = \sum_a A_a$ and $X_j = \sum_b B_b$, where $A_a = 1$ if draw $a$
is in category $i$, and $B_b = 1$ if draw $b$ is in category $j$. One draw cannot be in
both categories, so $A_a B_a = 0$. For two different draws $a \ne b$, independence
gives $E[A_a B_b] = p_i p_j$, and there are $n(n-1)$ such ordered pairs. By linearity:

$$E[X_i X_j] = \sum_{a \ne b} E[A_a B_b] = n(n-1) \thinspace p_i p_j$$

The **covariance**, defined in [11.1](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.1-Covariance.md)
as $E[X_i X_j] - E[X_i] E[X_j]$, is then

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = n(n-1) p_i p_j - (n p_i)(n p_j) = -n \thinspace p_i p_j$$

It is negative, as the story says. The simulation below checks it.

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Every Face Once

**Problem.** Roll a fair die 6 times. What is the probability that each face appears
exactly once?

**Solution.**

1. The counts of faces 1 to 6 are $\text{Mult}(6, \frac{1}{6}, \dots, \frac{1}{6})$. We want all
   counts equal to 1.
2. Number of orders: $\frac{6!}{1! \thinspace 1! \thinspace 1! \thinspace 1! \thinspace 1! \thinspace 1!} = 720$.
3. Probability of one order: $\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^6 = \frac{1}{46656}$.
4. Multiply: $\frac{720}{46656} \approx 0.01543$.

**Answer.** About $0.0154$, roughly 1 time in 65.

**Check.** The 720 orders are the permutations of the 6 faces, and there are
$6^6 = 46656$ equally likely sequences. Counting gives the same $\frac{720}{46656}$.

### Example 2: A Small Election Poll

**Problem.** Candidates A, B and C have support 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. Ten random voters
are asked. Find the probability of exactly 5 votes for A, 3 for B and 2 for C, the
expected counts, and the probability that C gets at least 4 votes.

**Solution.**

1. $(X_A, X_B, X_C) \sim \text{Mult}(10, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$.
2. Number of orders: $\frac{10!}{5! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 2!} = \frac{3628800}{120 \times 6 \times 2} = \frac{3628800}{1440} = 2520$.
3. One order: $0.5^5 \times 0.3^3 \times 0.2^2 = 0.03125 \times 0.027 \times 0.04 = 0.00003375$.
4. $P(5, 3, 2) = 2520 \times 0.00003375 = 0.08505$.
5. Expected counts: $10 \times 0.5 = 5$, $10 \times 0.3 = 3$, $10 \times 0.2 = 2$.
6. For C alone, $X_C \sim \text{Bin}(10, 0.2)$. Use the complement $X_C \le 3$:
   - $P(X_C = 0) = 0.8^{10} \approx 0.10737$
   - $P(X_C = 1) = 10 \times 0.2 \times 0.8^9 \approx 0.26844$
   - $P(X_C = 2) = 45 \times 0.04 \times 0.8^8 \approx 0.30199$
   - $P(X_C = 3) = 120 \times 0.008 \times 0.8^7 \approx 0.20133$
7. Sum: $0.87913$, so $P(X_C \ge 4) \approx 1 - 0.87913 = 0.12087$.

**Answer.** $P(5, 3, 2) \approx 0.085$. The expected counts are 5, 3, 2. C gets at
least 4 of the 10 votes with probability about $0.121$.

**Check.** The single most likely result, "exactly the expected counts", still has
only about 8.5% probability. Small polls are noisy.

### Example 3: Genotypes of Offspring

**Problem.** Two parents both carry one copy of gene A and one of gene a. Each child
is AA, Aa or aa with probabilities $\frac{1}{4}$, $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{4}$,
independently. In a family of 4 children, what is the probability of exactly 1 AA,
2 Aa and 1 aa?

**Solution.**

1. The counts are $\text{Mult}(4, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4})$.
2. Number of orders: $\frac{4!}{1! \thinspace 2! \thinspace 1!} = \frac{24}{2} = 12$.
3. One order: $\frac{1}{4} \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{64}$.
4. Multiply: $\frac{12}{64} = 0.1875$.

**Answer.** $0.1875$.

**Check.** This is the "textbook" 1 : 2 : 1 ratio, yet it happens less than 1 time
in 5 for a family of 4. Ratios show up clearly only for large $n$.

## Simulate It

We simulate the poll of Example 2 many times with `rng.multinomial`. We check
$P(5, 3, 2)$, the binomial mean and variance of $X_A$, and the negative covariance
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_A, X_B) = -n p_A p_B = -10 \times 0.5 \times 0.3 = -1.5$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, p, n_trials = 10, [0.5, 0.3, 0.2], 1_000_000

counts = rng.multinomial(n, p, size=n_trials)      # each row: (A, B, C)
xa, xb, xc = counts[:, 0], counts[:, 1], counts[:, 2]

hit = (xa == 5) & (xb == 3) & (xc == 2)
print(f"P(5,3,2):     sim {hit.mean():.4f}, exact 0.0851")
print(f"E[X_A]:       sim {xa.mean():.4f}, exact {n * p[0]:.4f}")
print(f"Var(X_A):     sim {xa.var():.4f}, exact {n * p[0] * (1 - p[0]):.4f}")
cov = np.mean(xa * xb) - xa.mean() * xb.mean()
print(f"Cov(X_A,X_B): sim {cov:.4f}, exact {-n * p[0] * p[1]:.4f}")
print("every row adds to n:", bool(np.all(counts.sum(axis=1) == n)))
```

Output:

```text
P(5,3,2):     sim 0.0852, exact 0.0851
E[X_A]:       sim 5.0021, exact 5.0000
Var(X_A):     sim 2.5017, exact 2.5000
Cov(X_A,X_B): sim -1.5032, exact -1.5000
every row adds to n: True
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting that the counts add up to $n$.** A PMF value for counts that do not
  add up to $n$ is 0. You also only need $k - 1$ counts; the last one is fixed.
- **Treating the counts as independent.** $P(X_A = 5) \times P(X_B = 3) \times P(X_C = 2)$ is
  **not** $P(5, 3, 2)$. Use the joint PMF.
- **Mixing up categorical and multinomial.** One draw is categorical; the counts of
  $n$ draws are multinomial.
- **Using it for draws without replacement.** Dealing cards and counting suits is
  not multinomial, because draws are dependent. That is the multivariate version
  of the hypergeometric ([7.6](7.6-Hypergeometric.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** in the **bag of words** model, a document's word
  counts are multinomial, which is the basis of multinomial Naive Bayes for spam
  filtering and text classification
  ([21.1.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md)).
- **Statistics and data science:** the counts in the bins of a histogram are
  multinomial; goodness-of-fit tests compare observed counts with $n p_j$
  ([21.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Topic models:** when the probabilities $p_j$ are themselves uncertain, they are
  often given a Dirichlet distribution ([9.12](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.12-Dirichlet.md)).
- **Biology:** genotype counts, as in Example 3, and counts of species in a sample.
- **Everyday life:** election polls, survey answers with several options, and the
  number of each face in a game of dice.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Roll a fair die 12 times. What is the distribution of the number of 6s?
What is its mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Lump the faces 1 to 5 together as "not 6". The number of 6s is $\text{Bin}(12, \tfrac{1}{6})$,
with mean $12 \times \frac{1}{6} = 2$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** How many different orders of 7 votes contain exactly 3 votes for A,
2 for B and 2 for C?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\frac{7!}{3! \thinspace 2! \thinspace 2!} = \frac{5040}{6 \times 2 \times 2} = \frac{5040}{24} = 210$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Example 3 (probabilities $\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}$), what is the
probability that a family of 4 children has exactly 2 AA and 2 aa, and no Aa?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(2, 0, 2) = \frac{4!}{2! \thinspace 0! \thinspace 2!} \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^0 \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2 = 6 \times \frac{1}{256} = \frac{6}{256} \approx 0.0234$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A model sorts 20 support tickets into "bug", "billing" and "other"
with probabilities 0.25, 0.25, 0.5, independently. What are the mean and standard
deviation of the number of "other" tickets? What is the distribution of the number
of tickets that are **not** "other"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X_{\text{other}} \sim \text{Bin}(20, 0.5)$: mean $10$, variance $20 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 5$, standard
deviation $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$. The tickets that are not "other" are
$20 - X_{\text{other}} \sim \text{Bin}(20, 0.5)$ too (merging "bug" and "billing" gives
probability $0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $(X_1, X_2, X_3) \sim \text{Mult}(n, p_1, p_2, p_3)$. Show that, given
$X_1 = m$, the count $X_2$ is $\text{Bin}\left(n - m, \frac{p_2}{p_2 + p_3}\right)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the definition of conditional probability, with $X_3 = n - m - x$ forced:

$$P(X_2 = x \mid X_1 = m) = \frac{P(X_1 = m, X_2 = x, X_3 = n - m - x)}{P(X_1 = m)}$$

The top is $\frac{n!}{m! \thinspace x! \thinspace (n-m-x)!} p_1^m p_2^x p_3^{n-m-x}$. The bottom is
$\binom{n}{m} p_1^m (p_2 + p_3)^{n-m}$, because $X_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_1)$. Dividing, the
$p_1^m$ cancels and $\frac{n!}{m!}$ against $\binom{n}{m}$ leaves $(n-m)!$:

$$P(X_2 = x \mid X_1 = m) = \frac{(n-m)!}{x! \thinspace (n-m-x)!} \cdot \frac{p_2^x \thinspace p_3^{n-m-x}}{(p_2 + p_3)^{n-m}} = \binom{n-m}{x} \left(\frac{p_2}{p_2+p_3}\right)^x \left(\frac{p_3}{p_2+p_3}\right)^{n-m-x}$$

In words: the $n - m$ draws that did not land in category 1 are split between
categories 2 and 3 in the ratio $p_2 : p_3$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 5 with the poll of Example 2: among simulated polls
with $X_A = 6$, estimate $E[X_B]$ and compare with $(10 - 6) \times \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 2.4$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

```

Var(X_B | X_A = 6): sim 0.9607, exact 0.9600
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Mult}(n, p_1, \dots, p_k)$ counts how many of $n$ independent categorical draws
  land in each category.
- PMF: (multinomial coefficient) times (probability of one order):
  $\frac{n!}{x_1! \cdots x_k!} p_1^{x_1} \cdots p_k^{x_k}$, for counts that add up to $n$.
- Each count is $\text{Bin}(n, p_j)$, with mean $n p_j$ and variance $n p_j (1 - p_j)$.
- The counts are negatively related, because they share a fixed total $n$.
- In numpy, sample it with `rng.multinomial(n, p, size)`; each row is one vector of counts.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_k = x_k) = \frac{n!}{x_1! \cdots x_k!} \thinspace p_1^{x_1} \cdots p_k^{x_k}}
$$
