# 7.2 Binomial

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.2 Binomial

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [7.1 Bernoulli](7.1-Bernoulli.md)

> **Big idea:** The binomial distribution counts the successes in $n$ independent yes/no trials that all have the same success probability $p$.

---

## Why It Matters

Ten exam questions answered by guessing, 100 test images checked by a classifier,
1,000 visitors to a web page: each time we repeat the same yes/no trial and
**count the successes**. The binomial distribution answers every question of the
form "what is the chance of exactly (or at least) $k$ successes?". It is the most
used discrete distribution, and the Poisson and normal distributions both appear
as its limits.

## Intuition

You guess on a quiz with 3 true/false questions. Each guess is right with
probability 0.5, and the guesses do not affect each other. How many will you get
right?

There are $2^3 = 8$ equally likely patterns of right (R) and wrong (W). Group them
by the number of R's:

| Number right | Patterns | Count | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | WWW | 1 | $\frac{1}{8}$ |
| 1 | RWW, WRW, WWR | 3 | $\frac{3}{8}$ |
| 2 | RRW, RWR, WRR | 3 | $\frac{3}{8}$ |
| 3 | RRR | 1 | $\frac{1}{8}$ |

The counts 1, 3, 3, 1 are the numbers of ways to choose which questions are
right: $\binom{3}{0}, \binom{3}{1}, \binom{3}{2}, \binom{3}{3}$. If each guess were
right with probability 0.25 instead, each pattern with $k$ rights would have
probability $0.25^k \times 0.75^{3-k}$. That is the whole binomial formula: **(number of
patterns) times (probability of one pattern)**.

## Definition

Do $n$ trials. Assume:

1. each trial is a success or a failure;
2. every trial has the same success probability $p$;
3. the trials are **independent** ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)).

Let $X$ be the number of successes. Then $X$ has the **binomial distribution**,
written $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$, where $n$ is the number of trials and $p$ is the
success probability of each trial. Its PMF is:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n
$$

where:

- $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ is the number of ways to choose which $k$ of
  the $n$ trials are the successes ([2.3](../02-COUNTING/2.3-Combinations.md));
- $p^k$ is the chance that those $k$ trials succeed;
- $(1 - p)^{n - k}$ is the chance that the other $n - k$ trials fail.

**In words:** the chance of $k$ successes is the number of success patterns
times the chance of one pattern.

The support is $\lbrace 0, 1, \dots, n \rbrace$. The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1 - p)
$$

**In words:** you expect a fraction $p$ of the $n$ trials to succeed, and the
spread is $n$ times the spread of one trial.

With $n = 1$ the binomial is the Bernoulli: $\text{Bin}(1, p) = \text{Bern}(p)$.

## Why It Is True

**The PMF.** Fix one pattern with successes in $k$ chosen places and failures in
the other $n - k$, for example SSFF...F. By independence, its probability is the
product $p \cdot p \cdots (1-p) = p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$. Every pattern with $k$
successes has this same probability, there are $\binom{n}{k}$ of them, and no two
can happen at once. So we add $\binom{n}{k}$ equal terms.

**The PMF sums to 1.** By the binomial theorem
([2.7](../02-COUNTING/2.7-Binomial-Theorem-and-Pascals-Triangle.md)), with $q = 1 - p$:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} p^k q^{n-k} = (p + q)^n = 1^n = 1
$$

This is where the name "binomial" comes from.

**The mean.** Write $X$ as a sum of indicators. Let $I_j = 1$ if trial $j$ succeeds,
so $I_j \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ and $X = I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n$. By linearity
([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)):

$$
E[X] = E[I_1] + E[I_2] + \dots + E[I_n] = p + p + \dots + p = np
$$

**The variance.** First find $E[X(X-1)]$. If there are $x$ successes, the number of
**ordered pairs** $(i, j)$ of two different successful trials is $x(x - 1)$. So
$X(X - 1) = \sum_{i \ne j} I_i I_j$, a sum over all $n(n-1)$ ordered pairs of
different trials. Each term is 1 only if both trials succeed, which has
probability $p \cdot p = p^2$ by independence. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X(X-1)] &= \sum_{i \ne j} E[I_i I_j] = n(n-1) p^2 && \text{(linearity, } n(n-1) \text{ pairs)} \\
E[X^2] &= E[X(X-1)] + E[X] = n(n-1)p^2 + np && \text{(since } X^2 = X(X-1) + X \text{)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 && \text{(shortcut formula)} \\
&= n^2 p^2 - n p^2 + np - n^2 p^2 && \text{(expand)} \\
&= np - np^2 = np(1 - p)
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Note:** A faster route: the variance of a sum of **independent** variables is
> the sum of their variances, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) = n \cdot p(1-p)$. That rule is
> proved in [11.3 Variance of a Sum](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md).

**The shape.** The PMF rises and then falls. It is symmetric when $p = 0.5$, has a
long right tail when $p \lt 0.5$, and a long left tail when $p \gt 0.5$. Its highest
bar (the **mode**) is at $\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$; Practice problem 5 proves it.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Guessing on a Quiz

**Problem.** A quiz has 10 questions, each with 4 choices. You guess every answer
at random. You pass with 5 or more right. What is the chance you pass? How many do
you get right on average?

**Solution.**

1. Each guess is right with probability $p = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$, the guesses are
   independent, and $n = 10$. So $X \sim \text{Bin}(10, 0.25)$.
2. "5 or more" has 6 terms; its complement "4 or fewer" has 5. So compute
   $P(X \le 4)$ and subtract from 1.
3. Each term is $\binom{10}{k} 0.25^k \thinspace 0.75^{10-k}$:

| $k$ | $\binom{10}{k}$ | $0.25^k$ | $0.75^{10-k}$ | $P(X = k)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 0.05631 | 0.0563 |
| 1 | 10 | 0.25 | 0.07508 | 0.1877 |
| 2 | 45 | 0.0625 | 0.10011 | 0.2816 |
| 3 | 120 | 0.015625 | 0.13348 | 0.2503 |
| 4 | 210 | 0.00390625 | 0.17798 | 0.1460 |

4. Add: $P(X \le 4) = 0.0563 + 0.1877 + 0.2816 + 0.2503 + 0.1460 = 0.9219$.
5. So $P(X \ge 5) = 1 - 0.9219 = 0.0781$.
6. The mean is $np = 10 \times 0.25 = 2.5$, and the standard deviation is
   $\sqrt{10 \times 0.25 \times 0.75} = \sqrt{1.875} \approx 1.37$.

**Answer.** You pass with probability about $0.078$, about 1 time in 13. On
average you get 2.5 answers right.

**Check.** The pass mark 5 is about $(5 - 2.5)/1.37 \approx 1.8$ standard deviations
above the mean, so a small probability makes sense.

### Example 2: Will the Service Stay Up

**Problem.** A service runs on 12 servers. On a given day each server is down with
probability 0.1, independently of the others. The service works if at least 10
servers are up. What is the probability the service works?

**Solution.**

1. Count the servers that are **down**: $X \sim \text{Bin}(12, 0.1)$. "At least 10
   up" means "at most 2 down", that is $X \le 2$.
2. $P(X = 0) = 0.9^{12} = 0.2824$.
3. $P(X = 1) = 12 \times 0.1 \times 0.9^{11} = 1.2 \times 0.3138 = 0.3766$.
4. $P(X = 2) = 66 \times 0.01 \times 0.9^{10} = 0.66 \times 0.3487 = 0.2301$.
5. Add: $P(X \le 2) = 0.2824 + 0.3766 + 0.2301 = 0.8891$.

**Answer.** The service works on about 89% of days.

**Check.** On average $np = 1.2$ servers are down, which is below the limit of 2,
so "works" should be the likely outcome. It is.

### Example 3: How Noisy Is Test Accuracy

**Problem.** A classifier is right on 90% of all possible inputs. You test it on
100 independent examples. How many will it get right, on average, and how much
does that number vary?

**Solution.**

1. Each example is right with $p = 0.9$, so the number right is
   $X \sim \text{Bin}(100, 0.9)$.
2. Mean: $E[X] = 100 \times 0.9 = 90$.
3. Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 100 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 9$, so $\sigma = 3$.

**Answer.** On average 90 right, with a standard deviation of 3. A measured
accuracy of 87% or 93% is normal noise. In fact the exact probability of 85 or
fewer right is about $0.073$ (computed with `scipy.stats.binom.cdf(85, 100, 0.9)`).

**Check.** A test set of 100 examples cannot tell an 88% model from a 90% model.
That is why benchmark test sets are large.

## Simulate It

We build $\text{Bin}(10, 0.3)$ in two ways: by adding 10 Bernoulli trials, and with
`rng.binomial`. Both should match the exact PMF value $P(X = 3) \approx 0.2668$, the
mean 3 and the variance 2.1.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, n, p = 1_000_000, 10, 0.3

# Way 1: add n independent Bernoulli(p) trials.
x1 = (rng.random((n_trials, n)) < p).sum(axis=1)

# Way 2: numpy's binomial sampler.
x2 = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_trials)

exact = comb(n, 3) * p**3 * (1 - p) ** (n - 3)
for name, x in [("sum of Bernoullis", x1), ("rng.binomial", x2)]:
    print(f"{name:18s} P(X=3) {np.mean(x == 3):.4f}  "
          f"mean {x.mean():.4f}  var {x.var():.4f}")
print(f"{'Exact':18s} P(X=3) {exact:.4f}  mean {n * p:.4f}  "
      f"var {n * p * (1 - p):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
sum of Bernoullis  P(X=3) 0.2675  mean 3.0013  var 2.0983
rng.binomial       P(X=3) 0.2671  mean 3.0001  var 2.1014
Exact              P(X=3) 0.2668  mean 3.0000  var 2.1000
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the $\binom{n}{k}$.** The chance of 2 heads in 3 flips is not
  $0.5^2 \times 0.5$; there are 3 patterns, so it is $3 \times 0.125 = 0.375$.
- **Using the binomial when trials are not independent.** Drawing 5 cards from a
  deck **without replacement** changes the chance on each draw. That is the
  hypergeometric distribution ([7.6](7.6-Hypergeometric.md)).
- **Using it when $p$ changes from trial to trial.** If some servers are older
  and fail more often, the count of failures is not binomial.
- **Mixing up "exactly", "at least" and "at most".** "At least 1" is easiest as
  $1 - P(X = 0)$. Always write the event as a set of $k$ values first.
- **Swapping the exponents.** The power of $p$ is the number of successes $k$, and
  the power of $1 - p$ is the number of failures $n - k$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the number of correct predictions on a test set is
  binomial, so it tells you how much to trust an accuracy number. A majority vote
  of independent models is a binomial "at least half" question.
- **A/B testing:** the number of conversions among $n$ visitors is
  $\text{Bin}(n, p)$; comparing two such counts is the core of an A/B test.
- **Computer science:** replication and quorum systems ("at least 3 of 5 copies
  are alive"), error-correcting codes (how many bits flip), and the analysis of
  randomized algorithms.
- **Quality control and medicine:** defective items in a batch of $n$, patients
  who respond to a drug among $n$ treated.
- **Everyday life:** penalty kicks scored in a shootout, rainy days in a week.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Flip a fair coin 5 times. What is the probability of exactly 3 heads?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X \sim \text{Bin}(5, 0.5)$:

$$P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3} 0.5^3 \thinspace 0.5^2 = 10 \times \frac{1}{32} = \frac{10}{32} = 0.3125$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ has mean 6 and variance 2.4. Find $n$ and $p$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Divide the variance by the mean: $\frac{np(1-p)}{np} = 1 - p = \frac{2.4}{6} = 0.4$, so
$p = 0.6$. Then $n = \frac{6}{0.6} = 10$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Which is more likely: at least one six in 4 rolls of a die, or at
least one double six in 24 rolls of two dice? (This puzzle started the study of
probability in 1654.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the complement "zero successes" each time.

$$P(\text{at least one six in 4}) = 1 - \left(\tfrac{5}{6}\right)^4 \approx 1 - 0.4823 = 0.5177$$

$$P(\text{at least one double six in 24}) = 1 - \left(\tfrac{35}{36}\right)^{24} \approx 1 - 0.5086 = 0.4914$$

The first bet is better: it wins more than half the time, the second does not.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Five independent classifiers are each right with probability 0.7.
The ensemble outputs the majority answer. What is the probability that the
ensemble is right?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The number right is $X \sim \text{Bin}(5, 0.7)$. The majority is right when $X \ge 3$:

$$P(X \ge 3) = 10(0.7)^3(0.3)^2 + 5(0.7)^4(0.3) + (0.7)^5 = 0.3087 + 0.36015 + 0.16807 = 0.83692$$

The ensemble (about 84%) beats every single model (70%). This needs the models'
mistakes to be independent, which is rarely fully true in practice.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ with $0 \lt p \lt 1$. Show that

$$\frac{P(X = k+1)}{P(X = k)} = \frac{(n - k) \thinspace p}{(k + 1)(1 - p)}$$

and use it to show that the mode is $\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$ when $(n+1)p$ is not a
whole number.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write the two binomial coefficients with factorials. Their ratio is

$$\frac{\binom{n}{k+1}}{\binom{n}{k}} = \frac{k! \thinspace (n-k)!}{(k+1)! \thinspace (n-k-1)!} = \frac{n-k}{k+1}$$

and the powers give one more $p$ and one less $(1-p)$, so the ratio is as stated.

The PMF goes **up** from $k$ to $k+1$ exactly when the ratio is bigger than 1:

$$(n-k)p \gt (k+1)(1-p) \iff np - kp \gt k + 1 - kp - p \iff k + 1 \lt (n+1)p$$

So the bars rise while $k + 1 \lt (n+1)p$ and fall after that. The highest bar is
at the largest whole number below $(n+1)p$, which is $\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$. For
$\text{Bin}(10, 0.3)$: $(n+1)p = 3.3$, so the mode is 3, as the picture shows.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the answer to problem 4 by simulating 5 independent
classifiers on 1,000,000 inputs.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_inputs = 1_000_000
is_right = rng.random((n_inputs, 5)) < 0.7      # 5 independent models
majority_right = is_right.sum(axis=1) >= 3
print(f"Simulation: {majority_right.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {0.83692:.4f}")
```

Exact:      0.8369
```

</details>

## Summary

- The binomial counts successes in $n$ independent trials with the same $p$.
- Its PMF is (number of patterns) times (probability of one pattern):
  $\binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.
- Writing $X$ as a sum of $n$ Bernoulli indicators gives the mean $np$ and the
  variance $np(1-p)$.
- Use it only when the trials are independent and $p$ stays the same.
- In numpy, sample it with `rng.binomial(n, p, size)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \quad E[X] = np, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p)}
$$
