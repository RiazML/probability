# 7.10 Poisson Approximation to Binomial

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.10 Poisson Approximation to Binomial

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [7.9 Multinomial](7.9-Multinomial.md)

> **Big idea:** When there are many independent trials and each has a tiny chance of success, the binomial count is almost exactly Poisson with $\lambda = np$.

---

## Why It Matters

Binomial probabilities with large $n$ are hard to compute by hand: try
$\binom{1000}{3} (0.003)^3 (0.997)^{997}$. Worse, in real life we often do not know $n$
and $p$ separately. We know only the average count: "about 3 server errors per
day", "about 2 typos per page". The **Poisson approximation** replaces
$\text{Bin}(n, p)$ by $\text{Pois}(np)$. It explains why the Poisson distribution
([7.5](7.5-Poisson.md)) appears everywhere, and it comes with a simple bound on its error.

## Intuition

A company sends 1,000 emails, and each one bounces with probability 0.003,
independently. The number of bounces is $\text{Bin}(1000, 0.003)$, with mean 3.

Now think about a different company: 100,000 emails, each bouncing with probability
0.00003. The mean is again 3. Do the two companies see different distributions of
bounces? Hardly. In both cases there are many chances and each is tiny, so the only
thing that matters is the average, $\lambda = 3$. The table shows
$\text{Bin}(n, \frac{3}{n})$ for growing $n$, next to $\text{Pois}(3)$:

| $k$ | $n = 10$ | $n = 100$ | $n = 1000$ | $\text{Pois}(3)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0282 | 0.0476 | 0.0496 | 0.0498 |
| 1 | 0.1211 | 0.1471 | 0.1491 | 0.1494 |
| 2 | 0.2335 | 0.2252 | 0.2242 | 0.2240 |
| 3 | 0.2668 | 0.2275 | 0.2244 | 0.2240 |
| 4 | 0.2001 | 0.1706 | 0.1683 | 0.1680 |
| 5 | 0.1029 | 0.1013 | 0.1009 | 0.1008 |
| 6 | 0.0368 | 0.0496 | 0.0503 | 0.0504 |

With $n = 10$ and $p = 0.3$ the match is rough. With $n = 1000$ and $p = 0.003$ the two
agree to about 3 decimal places.

Same mean 3, but $p = 0.3$ is not small: the binomial is narrower (variance 2.1)
than the Poisson (variance 3). As $p$ shrinks, the binomial variance
$np(1-p)$ grows toward $np = 3$ and the bars become the same.

## Definition

Let $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ and set $\lambda = np$. The **Poisson approximation** is:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} \approx \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad \lambda = np
$$

**In words:** for many trials with a small success chance, count successes as if
they were Poisson with the same mean.

**The limit theorem.** Fix $\lambda \gt 0$ and let $p = \frac{\lambda}{n}$. Then for every
fixed $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \binom{n}{k} \left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^k \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n - k} = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}
$$

**How good is it?** A result called **Le Cam's inequality** says that for **every**
set $A$ of values,

$$
\big\lvert P(X \in A) - P(Y \in A) \big\rvert \le n p^2, \qquad X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p), \ Y \sim \text{Pois}(np)
$$

**In words:** any probability computed with the Poisson is off by at most $np^2$,
which is $\lambda$ times $p$. So the approximation is good when $p$ is small, even if
$\lambda$ is not small.

The same is true when the trials have **different** small probabilities
$p_1, \dots, p_n$: the number of successes is close to $\text{Pois}(p_1 + \dots + p_n)$,
with error at most $p_1^2 + \dots + p_n^2$. We use this inequality without proof; a
proof and the deeper "law of rare events" are in
[17.10](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md).

A common guide: the approximation is good when $n \ge 20$ and $p \le 0.05$, and very
good when $n \ge 100$ and $np \le 10$.

## Why It Is True

We need one fact from calculus: for any fixed number $\lambda$,

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^n = e^{-\lambda}
$$

(For example, $(1 - \frac{3}{1000})^{1000} \approx 0.0496$ and $e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$; see the
table in [7.5](7.5-Poisson.md).) Now split the binomial PMF into four pieces. Write
$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}$ and $p = \frac{\lambda}{n}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X = k) &= \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!} \cdot \frac{\lambda^k}{n^k} \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n} \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k} && \text{(rewrite the PMF)} \\
&= \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{n}{n} \cdot \frac{n-1}{n} \cdots \frac{n-k+1}{n}}_{\to 1} \cdot \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n}}_{\to e^{-\lambda}} \cdot \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k}}_{\to 1} && \text{(regroup)}
\end{aligned}
$$

As $n \to \infty$ with $k$ fixed:

1. Each of the $k$ fractions $\frac{n - i}{n} = 1 - \frac{i}{n}$ goes to 1, and there is a
   fixed number $k$ of them, so their product goes to 1.
2. $\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^n \to e^{-\lambda}$, by the calculus fact.
3. $\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k} \to 1^{-k} = 1$, because the power $k$ is fixed.

So $P(X = k) \to \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \cdot 1 \cdot e^{-\lambda} \cdot 1 = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}$.

**Why $p$ must be small, not only $n$ large.** The binomial variance is
$np(1-p) = \lambda(1-p)$, and the Poisson variance is $\lambda$. They agree only when
$1 - p$ is close to 1. With $n$ large but $p = 0.5$, the binomial is close to a
normal curve instead ([17.6](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Bit Errors in a Packet

**Problem.** A packet has 1,000 bits. Each bit is flipped by noise with probability
0.001, independently. Find the probability of no errors and of at most 2 errors,
both exactly and with the Poisson approximation.

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{Bin}(1000, 0.001)$, and $\lambda = 1000 \times 0.001 = 1$.
2. Exact: $P(X = 0) = 0.999^{1000} \approx 0.36770$.
3. Poisson: $P(Y = 0) = e^{-1} \approx 0.36788$.
4. Poisson, at most 2: $P(Y \le 2) = e^{-1}\left(1 + 1 + \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2.5 e^{-1} \approx 0.91970$.
5. Exact, at most 2 (with `scipy.stats.binom.cdf(2, 1000, 0.001)`): $0.91979$.
6. Le Cam's bound: $np^2 = 1000 \times 0.000001 = 0.001$.

**Answer.** $P(\text{no errors}) \approx 0.368$ and $P(\text{at most 2}) \approx 0.920$, by
either method.

**Check.** The two methods differ by less than $0.0002$, inside the bound $0.001$.

### Example 2: Birthdays on New Year's Day

**Problem.** A school has 500 students. Ignore leap years and assume birthdays are
uniform over 365 days. What is the probability that at least one student has a
birthday on January 1?

**Solution.**

1. Each student has the birthday with probability $p = \frac{1}{365}$, independently, so the
   count is $\text{Bin}(500, \frac{1}{365})$.
2. $\lambda = \frac{500}{365} \approx 1.3699$.
3. Poisson: $P(\text{at least one}) \approx 1 - e^{-1.3699} \approx 1 - 0.2541 = 0.7459$.
4. Exact: $1 - \left(\frac{364}{365}\right)^{500} \approx 1 - 0.2537 = 0.7463$.

**Answer.** About $0.746$.

**Check.** The error is $0.0005$, and the bound is
$np^2 = \frac{500}{365^2} \approx 0.0038$. The bound holds.

### Example 3: When the Approximation Fails

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin 10 times. Compare $P(X = 5)$ for $X \sim \text{Bin}(10, 0.5)$
with the Poisson value for $\lambda = 5$.

**Solution.**

1. Exact: $\binom{10}{5} 0.5^{10} = \frac{252}{1024} \approx 0.2461$.
2. Poisson: $e^{-5} \frac{5^5}{5!} = e^{-5} \times \frac{3125}{120} \approx 0.006738 \times 26.04 \approx 0.1755$.

**Answer.** $0.246$ against $0.175$: the Poisson value is about 29% too small.

**Check.** Here $p = 0.5$ is not small. The binomial variance is 2.5, but the Poisson
variance is 5, so the Poisson spreads its probability too widely. Le Cam's bound
$np^2 = 2.5$ is useless here, which is the warning sign.

## Simulate It

We simulate the email example from the Intuition, $\text{Bin}(1000, 0.003)$, and
compare its frequencies with the exact binomial PMF and the Poisson PMF.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, p, n_trials = 1000, 0.003, 1_000_000
x = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_trials)      # bounced emails per batch of 1,000

print(" k   simulated   binomial   Poisson(3)")
for k in range(7):
    print(f" {k}   {np.mean(x == k):.4f}      "
          f"{stats.binom.pmf(k, n, p):.4f}     {stats.poisson.pmf(k, n * p):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
 k   simulated   binomial   Poisson(3)
 0   0.0494      0.0496     0.0498
 1   0.1492      0.1491     0.1494
 2   0.2242      0.2242     0.2240
 3   0.2246      0.2244     0.2240
 4   0.1680      0.1683     0.1680
 5   0.1011      0.1009     0.1008
 6   0.0502      0.0503     0.0504
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using it when $p$ is not small.** Large $n$ alone is not enough. With $p = 0.5$ use
  the exact binomial or the normal approximation.
- **Forgetting to set $\lambda = np$.** The Poisson must have the same mean as the
  binomial. Using $\lambda = n$ or $\lambda = p$ is a frequent slip.
- **Using it for a few trials.** With $n = 5$ and $p = 0.01$, just compute the binomial;
  it is easy and exact.
- **Ignoring strong dependence.** The trials may be weakly dependent (as in the
  birthday problem), but if one success makes others much more likely, the count
  can be far from Poisson.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** hash collisions and the birthday problem
  ([2.11.1](../02-COUNTING/2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md)):
  the number of colliding pairs among many keys is close to Poisson. Also bit errors,
  packet loss and rare server failures.
- **AI and machine learning:** rare events in large datasets, such as fraud among
  millions of transactions, or how often a rare token appears in a large corpus.
- **Quality control and reliability:** defects among thousands of items, each with
  a tiny defect rate.
- **Insurance and medicine:** claims in a large group of customers, cases of a rare
  disease in a large population.
- **Everyday life:** winners among lottery tickets sold, typos in a long book.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** 2,000 lottery tickets are sold, and each wins a prize with probability
0.001, independently. Use the Poisson approximation to find the probability that no
ticket wins.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\lambda = 2000 \times 0.001 = 2$, so

$$P(\text{no winner}) \approx e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$$

The exact value is $0.999^{2000} \approx 0.1352$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For which of these is the Poisson approximation good:
$\text{Bin}(500, 0.002)$, $\text{Bin}(20, 0.4)$, $\text{Bin}(10^6, 10^{-6})$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\text{Bin}(500, 0.002)$: yes, $\lambda = 1$ and $np^2 = 0.002$.
$\text{Bin}(20, 0.4)$: no, $p$ is large ($np^2 = 3.2$).
$\text{Bin}(10^6, 10^{-6})$: yes, $\lambda = 1$ and $np^2 = 10^{-6}$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Example 1 (1,000 bits, flip probability 0.001), use Le Cam's
inequality to say how far the Poisson value of $P(X \ge 3)$ can be from the exact
value. Then compute the Poisson value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The bound is $np^2 = 1000 \times 10^{-6} = 0.001$ for every event. The Poisson value is

$$P(Y \ge 3) = 1 - P(Y \le 2) = 1 - 2.5e^{-1} \approx 1 - 0.9197 = 0.0803$$

so the exact value is between about $0.0793$ and $0.0813$. (It is $0.0802$.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** You put 1,000 keys into a hash table with 100,000 buckets, each key in
a uniformly random bucket. Each of the $\binom{1000}{2}$ pairs of keys collides with
probability $\frac{1}{100000}$. Use a Poisson approximation for the number of
colliding pairs to estimate the probability of no collision at all.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The expected number of colliding pairs is

$$\lambda = \binom{1000}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{100000} = \frac{499500}{100000} = 4.995$$

The pairs are not quite independent, but the dependence is weak, so the count is close
to $\text{Pois}(4.995)$ (this "Poisson paradigm" is explained in
[17.10](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md)). So

$$P(\text{no collision}) \approx e^{-4.995} \approx 0.0068$$

The exact value, $\prod_{i=0}^{999} \left(1 - \frac{i}{100000}\right)$, is about $0.0067$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $1 - p \le e^{-p}$ for $0 \le p \le 1$. Conclude that the Poisson
approximation never underestimates the chance of zero successes:
$(1 - p)^n \le e^{-np}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the series $e^{-p} = 1 - p + \frac{p^2}{2!} - \frac{p^3}{3!} + \frac{p^4}{4!} - \dots$ and
group the terms after $1 - p$ in pairs:

$$e^{-p} - (1 - p) = \left(\frac{p^2}{2!} - \frac{p^3}{3!}\right) + \left(\frac{p^4}{4!} - \frac{p^5}{5!}\right) + \dots$$

Each pair is $\frac{p^{m}}{m!}\left(1 - \frac{p}{m+1}\right) \ge 0$ when $0 \le p \le 1$. So
$e^{-p} \ge 1 - p$. Both sides are non-negative, so raising them to the power $n$ keeps
the order: $(1 - p)^n \le (e^{-p})^n = e^{-np}$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 4: throw 1,000 keys into 100,000 buckets 20,000
times, and estimate the probability of no collision.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, n_keys, n_buckets = 20_000, 1000, 100_000
buckets = rng.integers(0, n_buckets, size=(n_trials, n_keys))
buckets.sort(axis=1)
no_collision = np.all(np.diff(buckets, axis=1) != 0, axis=1)
print(f"Simulation: {no_collision.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Poisson:    {np.exp(-4.995):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.0067
Poisson:    0.0068
```

</details>

## Summary

- For large $n$ and small $p$, $\text{Bin}(n, p) \approx \text{Pois}(np)$.
- The proof splits the PMF into pieces and uses $(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n})^n \to e^{-\lambda}$.
- Le Cam's inequality bounds the error of every probability by $np^2$, so small $p$ is
  what matters.
- It also works for different small $p_i$, with $\lambda = \sum p_i$, and for weakly
  dependent rare events.
- It fails when $p$ is not small: the binomial is then narrower than the Poisson.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} \approx \frac{e^{-np} (np)^k}{k!}, \quad \text{error} \le np^2}
$$
