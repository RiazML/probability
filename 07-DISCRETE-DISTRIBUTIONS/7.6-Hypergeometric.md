# 7.6 Hypergeometric

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.6 Hypergeometric

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [7.5 Poisson](7.5-Poisson.md)

> **Big idea:** The hypergeometric distribution counts the "special" items in a sample drawn **without replacement**, so every draw changes what is left.

---

## Why It Matters

When you deal a poker hand, the same card cannot come twice. When a factory
inspects 10 chips from a box of 50, it does not put a chip back and test it again.
These are draws **without replacement**, and the binomial distribution is then
not exact, because the chance of a "special" item changes after every draw. The
**hypergeometric distribution** gives the exact answer. It is used for card games,
lotteries, quality control, and tests in biology and statistics.

## Intuition

A box holds 10 phones: 3 are broken and 7 work. You pick 2 phones at random, without
putting the first one back. What is the chance both are broken?

- The first phone is broken with chance $\frac{3}{10}$.
- If it was broken, 2 broken phones are left among 9, so the second is broken with
  chance $\frac{2}{9}$.
- Both broken: $\frac{3}{10} \times \frac{2}{9} = \frac{6}{90} = \frac{1}{15} \approx 0.067$.

With replacement the answer would be $0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$. The difference comes
from the shrinking box. We can also get $\frac{1}{15}$ by counting: there are
$\binom{10}{2} = 45$ equally likely pairs, and $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ of them are
"both broken", so the chance is $\frac{3}{45} = \frac{1}{15}$. The hypergeometric
formula is this counting idea, in general.

## Definition

A population has $N$ items. $K$ of them are **special** ("successes") and $N - K$ are
not. Draw $n$ items at random **without replacement**, so that every set of $n$
items is equally likely. Let $X$ be the number of special items in the sample.
Then $X$ has the **hypergeometric distribution**, written $X \sim \text{HGeom}(N, K, n)$,
where $N$ is the population size, $K$ is the number of special items, and $n$ is the
sample size. Its PMF is:

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k} \binom{N - K}{n - k}}{\binom{N}{n}}
$$

where:

- $\binom{N}{n}$ is the number of possible samples, all equally likely;
- $\binom{K}{k}$ is the number of ways to choose which $k$ special items you get;
- $\binom{N-K}{n-k}$ is the number of ways to choose the other $n - k$ items.

**In words:** count the samples with exactly $k$ special items, and divide by the
number of all samples.

The **support** is every whole number $k$ with
$\max(0, \thinspace n - (N - K)) \le k \le \min(n, K)$. You cannot get more special
items than you draw or than exist, and if you draw more items than there are
"others", some special items must appear.

The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = n \frac{K}{N}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = n \frac{K}{N} \left(1 - \frac{K}{N}\right) \frac{N - n}{N - 1}
$$

**In words:** the mean is the same as for a binomial with $p = \frac{K}{N}$, but the
variance is smaller by the factor $\frac{N-n}{N-1}$, called the **finite population
correction**.

## Why It Is True

**The PMF.** All $\binom{N}{n}$ samples are equally likely, so we can use the
counting rule ([3.7](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.7-Equally-Likely-Outcomes.md)). A
sample with exactly $k$ special items is a choice of $k$ special items **and** a
choice of $n - k$ other items. By the multiplication rule there are
$\binom{K}{k}\binom{N-K}{n-k}$ such samples.

**The PMF sums to 1.** Every sample has some number of special items, so adding the
counts over all $k$ gives all samples:

$$
\sum_{k} \binom{K}{k} \binom{N - K}{n - k} = \binom{N}{n}
$$

This is **Vandermonde's identity**, and the sentence above is its story proof
([2.10 Story Proofs](../02-COUNTING/2.10-Story-Proofs.md)).

**The mean, with indicators.** Think of the sample as $n$ draws in order. Let
$I_j = 1$ if draw $j$ is special. Before you look at any draw, draw $j$ is equally
likely to be any of the $N$ items, so $P(I_j = 1) = \frac{K}{N}$. This is true for every
$j$, even though the draws depend on each other. So, by linearity
([6.4 Indicator Trick](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.4-Indicator-Trick.md)):

$$
E[X] = E[I_1] + \dots + E[I_n] = n \frac{K}{N}
$$

**The variance.** As for the binomial ([7.2](7.2-Binomial.md)), $X(X-1)$ is the
number of ordered pairs $(i, j)$ of different draws that are both special. For two
different draws, the chance both are special is $\frac{K}{N} \cdot \frac{K-1}{N-1}$ (the
second draw has one fewer special item and one fewer item in total). There are
$n(n-1)$ ordered pairs, so:

$$
E[X(X-1)] = n(n-1) \frac{K(K-1)}{N(N-1)}
$$

Now use $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X(X-1)] + E[X] - (E[X])^2$ and take out the factor
$\frac{nK}{N}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= \frac{nK}{N} \left[ \frac{(n-1)(K-1)}{N-1} + 1 - \frac{nK}{N} \right] && \text{(factor out } nK/N \text{)} \\
&= \frac{nK}{N} \cdot \frac{(n-1)(K-1)N + N(N-1) - nK(N-1)}{N(N-1)} && \text{(common denominator)} \\
&= \frac{nK}{N} \cdot \frac{N^2 - nN - KN + nK}{N(N-1)} && \text{(expand and cancel)} \\
&= \frac{nK}{N} \cdot \frac{(N-K)(N-n)}{N(N-1)} = n \frac{K}{N} \cdot \frac{N-K}{N} \cdot \frac{N-n}{N-1} && \text{(factor)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why the correction makes sense.** If $n = 1$, the factor $\frac{N-n}{N-1}$ is 1: one
draw cannot "remember" anything. If $n = N$, you take the whole population, so
$X = K$ for sure and the variance is 0. If $N$ is huge compared with $n$, the factor is
close to 1, and $\text{HGeom}(N, K, n)$ is close to $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{K}{N})$: taking a few
items out of a huge population barely changes it
([7.11](7.11-Relationships-Between-Distributions.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Hearts in a Poker Hand

**Problem.** You are dealt 5 cards from a well-shuffled deck of 52. What is the
probability of exactly 2 hearts? What are the mean and variance of the number of
hearts?

**Solution.**

1. $N = 52$, $K = 13$ hearts, $n = 5$, so $X \sim \text{HGeom}(52, 13, 5)$.
2. $\binom{13}{2} = 78$ ways to pick the 2 hearts.
3. $\binom{39}{3} = \frac{39 \times 38 \times 37}{6} = 9139$ ways to pick the other 3 cards.
4. $\binom{52}{5} = 2598960$ hands in total.
5. $P(X = 2) = \frac{78 \times 9139}{2598960} = \frac{712842}{2598960} \approx 0.2743$.
6. Mean: $5 \times \frac{13}{52} = 1.25$.
7. Variance: $5 \times \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{47}{51} = 0.9375 \times 0.9216 \approx 0.8640$.

**Answer.** $P(X = 2) \approx 0.274$, $E[X] = 1.25$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) \approx 0.864$.

**Check.** A binomial $\text{Bin}(5, 0.25)$ would give variance $0.9375$; the real
variance is a bit smaller, as the correction factor $\frac{47}{51}$ says.

### Example 2: Accepting a Box of Chips

**Problem.** A box has 50 chips, and 5 of them are faulty. The buyer tests 10 chips
chosen at random and accepts the box only if none of them is faulty. What is the
probability the box is accepted?

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{HGeom}(50, 5, 10)$, and we want $P(X = 0)$.
2. $P(X = 0) = \frac{\binom{5}{0}\binom{45}{10}}{\binom{50}{10}} = \frac{\binom{45}{10}}{\binom{50}{10}}$.
3. Write both with factorials. Most factors cancel, and what is left is

$$
\frac{\binom{45}{10}}{\binom{50}{10}} = \frac{45! \thinspace 40!}{35! \thinspace 50!} = \frac{40 \times 39 \times 38 \times 37 \times 36}{50 \times 49 \times 48 \times 47 \times 46} = \frac{78960960}{254251200} \approx 0.3106
$$

**Answer.** The box is accepted with probability about $0.311$, even though 10% of
its chips are faulty.

**Check.** The binomial guess $0.9^{10} \approx 0.349$ is too high. Without
replacement, each good chip you draw makes the faulty ones a bigger share of what
is left, so finding no faulty chip is a little harder.

### Example 3: Matching Three Lottery Numbers

**Problem.** A lottery draws 6 numbers from 1 to 49. Your ticket has 6 numbers. What
is the probability that exactly 3 of your numbers are drawn?

**Solution.**

1. Think of your 6 numbers as the "special" items: $N = 49$, $K = 6$, and the draw
   is a sample of $n = 6$. So $X \sim \text{HGeom}(49, 6, 6)$.
2. $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ and $\binom{43}{3} = \frac{43 \times 42 \times 41}{6} = 12341$.
3. $\binom{49}{6} = 13983816$.
4. $P(X = 3) = \frac{20 \times 12341}{13983816} = \frac{246820}{13983816} \approx 0.01765$.

**Answer.** About $0.0177$, roughly 1 ticket in 57.

**Check.** The expected number of matches is $6 \times \frac{6}{49} \approx 0.73$, so most
tickets match 0 or 1 number, and 3 matches should be rare. It is.

## Simulate It

We deal 5-card hands with numpy's hypergeometric sampler and compare with the exact
PMF. Note the numpy arguments: number of special items, number of other items,
sample size.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N, K, n, n_trials = 52, 13, 5, 1_000_000

# ngood = K hearts, nbad = N - K other cards, nsample = n cards.
x = rng.hypergeometric(K, N - K, n, size=n_trials)

for k in range(n + 1):
    exact = comb(K, k) * comb(N - K, n - k) / comb(N, n)
    print(f"P(X={k}): sim {np.mean(x == k):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
var_exact = n * (K / N) * (1 - K / N) * (N - n) / (N - 1)
print(f"mean {x.mean():.4f} (exact {n * K / N:.4f}), "
      f"var {x.var():.4f} (exact {var_exact:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
P(X=0): sim 0.2222, exact 0.2215
P(X=1): sim 0.4115, exact 0.4114
P(X=2): sim 0.2741, exact 0.2743
P(X=3): sim 0.0811, exact 0.0815
P(X=4): sim 0.0106, exact 0.0107
P(X=5): sim 0.0005, exact 0.0005
mean 1.2477 (exact 1.2500), var 0.8624 (exact 0.8640)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the binomial for small populations.** Without replacement from a small
  population, draws are dependent. Use the hypergeometric, unless $n$ is tiny
  compared with $N$ (a common guide: $n$ below 5% of $N$).
- **Mixing up the parameters in software.** numpy uses
  `rng.hypergeometric(ngood, nbad, nsample)`. scipy uses
  `scipy.stats.hypergeom(M, n, N)` with $M$ = population, $n$ = special items, and
  $N$ = sample size, which is **not** the letters of this book. Check with the mean.
- **Ignoring the support.** With $N = 10$, $K = 7$, $n = 5$, you must get at least 2
  special items, so $P(X = 0) = P(X = 1) = 0$.
- **Thinking the draws are independent because each has chance $\frac{K}{N}$.** Each
  draw alone has chance $\frac{K}{N}$, but the draws are dependent. That is why the
  mean matches the binomial but the variance does not.

## Where It Is Used

- **Games and lotteries:** card hands in poker and bridge, lottery prizes, and
  drawing tiles in board games.
- **Quality control:** **acceptance sampling**, as in Example 2, decides whether to
  accept a batch from a small test sample.
- **Ecology:** **capture-recapture**. Tag $K$ fish, release them, then catch $n$. The
  number of tagged fish caught is hypergeometric, which lets you estimate the
  unknown population size $N$.
- **Biology and data science:** **Fisher's exact test** and **gene set enrichment**
  ask whether a sample has "too many" special items, using hypergeometric tail
  probabilities.
- **Computer science and AI:** if a search engine returns $n$ documents at random from
  a collection of $N$ with $K$ relevant ones, the number of relevant results is
  $\text{HGeom}(N, K, n)$. This is the "random baseline" for precision at $n$.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A committee of 4 is chosen at random from 6 women and 4 men. What is
the probability that it has exactly 2 women?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X \sim \text{HGeom}(10, 6, 4)$:

$$P(X = 2) = \frac{\binom{6}{2}\binom{4}{2}}{\binom{10}{4}} = \frac{15 \times 6}{210} = \frac{90}{210} = \frac{3}{7} \approx 0.4286$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** In Example 2 (50 chips, 5 faulty, test 10), find the mean and
variance of the number of faulty chips found.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 10 \times \frac{5}{50} = 1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = 10 \times 0.1 \times 0.9 \times \frac{40}{49} \approx 0.9 \times 0.8163 \approx 0.7347$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A lake has 200 fish, and 20 of them are tagged. You catch 10 fish.
What is the probability that you catch no tagged fish? How many tagged fish do you
expect?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X \sim \text{HGeom}(200, 20, 10)$. The expected number is $10 \times \frac{20}{200} = 1$.

$$P(X = 0) = \frac{\binom{180}{10}}{\binom{200}{10}} = \frac{180 \times 179 \times \dots \times 171}{200 \times 199 \times \dots \times 191} \approx 0.3398$$

(The product has 10 factors on top and 10 below; a calculator gives $0.3398$.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X \sim \text{HGeom}(10, 7, 5)$. What is the support of $X$? Explain in
words why $X = 1$ is impossible.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The support is $\max(0, 5 - 3) \le k \le \min(5, 7)$, that is $\lbrace 2, 3, 4, 5 \rbrace$.
There are only $N - K = 3$ non-special items. A sample of 5 can contain at most 3
of them, so it must contain at least 2 special items.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $\text{HGeom}(N, K, n)$ and $\text{HGeom}(N, n, K)$ have the same
PMF: the roles of "number of special items" and "sample size" can be swapped. Give
a story proof.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Algebra: write everything with factorials. Both sides become

$$\frac{K! \thinspace (N-K)! \thinspace n! \thinspace (N-n)!}{k! \thinspace (K-k)! \thinspace (n-k)! \thinspace (N-K-n+k)! \thinspace N!}$$

Story: line up $N$ balls. Mark $K$ of them "special" and, independently and at
random, mark $n$ of them "sampled". $X$ is the number of balls with **both** marks.
Since the marks are placed at random and independently, it does not matter which
mark we call "special" and which we call "sampled". Swapping the names swaps $K$ and
$n$ but does not change the number of doubly marked balls.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 3 by really shuffling: for each trial, pick 10 of the
200 fish without replacement (fish 0 to 19 are tagged) and count the tagged ones.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
# A random ordering of 200 fish per trial; the first 10 are the catch.

tagged = (catch < 20).sum(axis=1)

print(f"mean tagged:  sim {tagged.mean():.4f}, exact 1.0000")
```

Output:

```text

mean tagged:  sim 0.9967, exact 1.0000
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{HGeom}(N, K, n)$ counts special items in a sample of $n$ drawn without
  replacement from $N$ items, $K$ of which are special.
- PMF by counting: $\frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{N-K}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}$.
- Mean $n\frac{K}{N}$, the same as the binomial, by indicators.
- Variance $n\frac{K}{N}(1-\frac{K}{N})\frac{N-n}{N-1}$: smaller than the binomial's, by the
  finite population correction.
- In numpy, sample it with `rng.hypergeometric(K, N - K, n, size)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X = k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k} \binom{N - K}{n - k}}{\binom{N}{n}}, \quad E[X] = n \frac{K}{N}}
$$
