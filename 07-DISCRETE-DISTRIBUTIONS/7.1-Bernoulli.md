# 7.1 Bernoulli

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 07: Discrete Distributions](README.md) / 7.1 Bernoulli

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 30 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/README.md)

> **Big idea:** A Bernoulli random variable is one yes/no trial written as a number: 1 for "yes" with probability $p$, and 0 for "no" with probability $1 - p$.

---

## Why It Matters

Many questions have only two answers. Did the user click? Is the email spam? Did
the packet arrive? The **Bernoulli distribution** is the model for one such
question. It is the smallest building block in this module: the binomial,
geometric and negative binomial distributions are all built by repeating
Bernoulli trials.

## Intuition

A basketball player makes 80% of her free throws. She takes one shot. Write 1 if
she scores and 0 if she misses. Then the number is 1 with probability 0.8 and 0
with probability 0.2.

What is the "average" result of one shot? If she shoots 1,000 times, she scores
about 800 times. The average of 800 ones and 200 zeros is
$\frac{800}{1000} = 0.8$. So the mean of a 0/1 number is just the chance of a 1.

How much does the result vary? If she made 100% of her shots, the result would
always be 1, with no variation at all. The same is true at 0%. The result is most
unpredictable in the middle, near 50%, when you really cannot guess the next shot.
The variance formula below shows exactly this shape.

## Picture

```text
  P(X = k)
   0.7 |   ###
   0.6 |   ###
   0.5 |   ###
   0.4 |   ###
   0.3 |   ###        ###
   0.2 |   ###        ###
   0.1 |   ###        ###
       +---+-+--------+-+------
           0          1        k        X ~ Bern(0.3)
        "failure"  "success"
```

A Bernoulli PMF has only two bars: height $1 - p$ at 0 and height $p$ at 1.

## Definition

A **Bernoulli trial** is one experiment with exactly two outcomes, called
**success** and **failure**. "Success" is only a name for the outcome we count. It
does not have to be a good thing: a server crash can be the "success".

A random variable $X$ has the **Bernoulli distribution** with parameter $p$,
written $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$, if it takes the value 1 with probability $p$ and
the value 0 with probability $1 - p$. Here $p$ is the success probability, and
$0 \le p \le 1$. Its PMF ([5.3](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.3-Probability-Mass-Function.md)) is:

$$
p_X(k) = P(X = k) =
\begin{cases}
p & \text{if } k = 1 \\
1 - p & \text{if } k = 0
\end{cases}
$$

The same PMF in one line, which is handy in machine learning:

$$
p_X(k) = p^k (1 - p)^{1 - k}, \qquad k \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace
$$

**In words:** put $k = 1$ and you get $p^1 (1-p)^0 = p$; put $k = 0$ and you get
$p^0 (1-p)^1 = 1 - p$.

The **support** (the set of values with positive probability, see
[5.8](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.8-Support.md)) is $\lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$
when $0 \lt p \lt 1$. We often write $q = 1 - p$ for the failure probability.

The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1 - p)
$$

**In words:** the average of a 0/1 variable is the chance of a 1, and its spread is
largest when success and failure are equally likely.

> **Note:** Every indicator variable is a Bernoulli variable. If $A$ is an event,
> then $I_A \sim \text{Bern}(P(A))$. See
> [5.5 Indicator Random Variables](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.5-Indicator-Random-Variables.md).

## Why It Is True

**The PMF sums to 1.** The two probabilities are $p$ and $1 - p$, and
$p + (1 - p) = 1$.

**The mean.** Use the definition of expected value from
[6.1](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.1-Expected-Value.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \sum_{k} k \thinspace p_X(k) && \text{(definition of expectation)} \\
&= 0 \cdot (1 - p) + 1 \cdot p && \text{(only two values)} \\
&= p
\end{aligned}
$$

**The variance.** Here is a useful trick. Since $X$ is 0 or 1, and $0^2 = 0$ and
$1^2 = 1$, we have $X^2 = X$. So $E[X^2] = E[X] = p$. Now use the shortcut formula
from [6.5](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 && \text{(shortcut formula)} \\
&= p - p^2 && \text{(because } X^2 = X \text{)} \\
&= p(1 - p)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Where the variance is largest.** Complete the square:

$$
p(1 - p) = p - p^2 = \frac{1}{4} - \left(p - \frac{1}{2}\right)^2
$$

The square is never negative, so $p(1 - p) \le \frac{1}{4}$. The largest value,
$\frac{1}{4}$, happens exactly at $p = \frac{1}{2}$. The variance is 0 at $p = 0$
and at $p = 1$, when there is no randomness at all.

```text
  Var(X) = p(1-p)
   0.25 |                 .  '  '  .
        |            . '              ' .
   0.16 |        . '                      ' .
        |     .'                              '.
   0.09 |   .'                                  '.
        | .'                                      '.
      0 +------------------------------------------- p
        0        0.2          0.5         0.8      1
```

The variance of one yes/no trial is a hill: 0 at the ends, highest at $p = 0.5$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: One Free Throw

**Problem.** A player makes a free throw with probability 0.8. Let $X = 1$ if she
scores. Find $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and the standard deviation.

**Solution.**

1. The shot is one yes/no trial, so $X \sim \text{Bern}(0.8)$ with $p = 0.8$.
2. Mean: $E[X] = p = 0.8$.
3. Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1 - p) = 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$.
4. Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.4$.

**Answer.** $E[X] = 0.8$, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.16$, $\sigma = 0.4$.

**Check.** The variance is below the maximum $0.25$, as it must be. A 50% shooter
would have the largest variance, $0.25$.

### Example 2: Did the User Click the Ad

**Problem.** An online ad is clicked by 2% of the people who see it. One person
sees it. Let $X = 1$ if they click. Find the mean and the standard deviation.

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{Bern}(0.02)$, so $p = 0.02$ and $1 - p = 0.98$.
2. Mean: $E[X] = 0.02$.
3. Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.02 \times 0.98 = 0.0196$.
4. Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.0196} = 0.14$.

**Answer.** $E[X] = 0.02$ and $\sigma = 0.14$.

**Check.** The standard deviation is 7 times the mean. For rare events, one trial
tells you very little, which is why A/B tests need many visitors.

### Example 3: A Bet With Two Outcomes

**Problem.** A game pays 10 taka with probability 0.4 and makes you lose 5 taka
with probability 0.6. Let $Y$ be your gain. Find $E[Y]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$.

**Solution.**

1. $Y$ takes the values 10 and $-5$, not 1 and 0, so $Y$ itself is not Bernoulli.
   But we can build it from $X \sim \text{Bern}(0.4)$, which is 1 when you win:

$$
Y = -5 + 15X
$$

Check the two cases: if $X = 1$ then $Y = -5 + 15 = 10$; if $X = 0$ then $Y = -5$.

2. Mean, by linearity ([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)):
   $E[Y] = -5 + 15 \times 0.4 = -5 + 6 = 1$.
3. Variance, by the rule $\operatorname{Var}(a + bX) = b^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$
   ([6.6](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)):
   $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 15^2 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 = 225 \times 0.24 = 54$.

**Answer.** $E[Y] = 1$ taka and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 54$, so the standard
deviation is $\sqrt{54} \approx 7.35$ taka.

**Check.** Directly: $E[Y] = 10 \times 0.4 + (-5) \times 0.6 = 4 - 3 = 1$. It matches.
Every two-valued variable is a shifted and scaled Bernoulli.

## Simulate It

There are two common ways to sample a Bernoulli in numpy: compare a uniform number
with $p$, or ask for a binomial with one trial. Both should give mean $p$ and
variance $p(1 - p)$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
p = 0.3

# Way 1: a uniform number in [0, 1) is below p with probability p.
x1 = (rng.random(n_trials) < p).astype(int)

# Way 2: a binomial with n = 1 is a Bernoulli.
x2 = rng.binomial(1, p, size=n_trials)

for name, x in [("random() < p", x1), ("binomial(1, p)", x2)]:
    print(f"{name:15s} mean {x.mean():.4f}  var {x.var():.4f}")
print(f"{'Exact':15s} mean {p:.4f}  var {p * (1 - p):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
random() < p    mean 0.2999  var 0.2099
binomial(1, p)  mean 0.2994  var 0.2098
Exact           mean 0.3000  var 0.2100
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up which outcome is the success.** If "success" means "the email is
  spam" with $p = 0.1$, then $P(X = 0) = 0.9$. Write down what $X = 1$ means before
  you compute anything.
- **Thinking the variance grows with $p$.** It does not. $p(1 - p)$ is the same for
  $p = 0.1$ and $p = 0.9$, and it is largest at $p = 0.5$.
- **Calling any two-valued variable Bernoulli.** A Bernoulli variable takes the
  values 0 and 1 only. For other values, write $Y = a + (b - a)X$ as in Example 3.
- **Using `rng.integers(0, 2)` for a biased coin.** That gives $p = 0.5$ only. For
  any other $p$, use `rng.random() < p` or `rng.binomial(1, p)`.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a binary classifier, such as logistic regression,
  predicts the $p$ of a Bernoulli label. Taking $-\log$ of the PMF
  $p^k (1 - p)^{1 - k}$ gives the **binary cross-entropy loss**
  ([21.1.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)).
- **Deep learning:** **dropout** keeps each neuron with some probability during
  training, so each neuron gets its own Bernoulli "keep" switch.
- **Computer science:** one bit of a Bloom filter, one packet that is lost or not,
  one run of a randomized algorithm that succeeds or not.
- **A/B testing:** each visitor converts (1) or not (0). The conversion rate is
  the $p$ of a Bernoulli.
- **Everyday life:** will it rain tomorrow, will the bus be late, will this
  exam question be on the test.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A fair die is rolled once. Let $X = 1$ if it shows a 6, and 0
otherwise. What is the distribution of $X$? Find its mean and variance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X \sim \text{Bern}(\tfrac{1}{6})$.

$$E[X] = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{5}{6} = \frac{5}{36} \approx 0.139$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For which value of $p$ is the variance of a Bernoulli largest? What
is that largest variance?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

At $p = \frac{1}{2}$, because $p(1 - p) = \frac{1}{4} - (p - \frac{1}{2})^2$. The largest
variance is $\frac{1}{4}$, and the standard deviation is then $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ has variance $0.21$. What can $p$ be?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $p(1 - p) = 0.21$, that is $p^2 - p + 0.21 = 0$:

$$p = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 0.84}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm 0.4}{2}$$

So $p = 0.7$ or $p = 0.3$. The variance cannot tell you which outcome is the more
likely one.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$. Show that $E[X^k] = p$ for every
$k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ What is $E[(1 - X)^2]$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X^k$ is $0^k = 0$ or $1^k = 1$, so $X^k = X$ and $E[X^k] = E[X] = p$.

$1 - X$ is 1 exactly when $X = 0$, so $1 - X \sim \text{Bern}(1 - p)$. Then
$(1 - X)^2 = 1 - X$ and

$$E[(1 - X)^2] = E[1 - X] = 1 - p$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ and $Y \sim \text{Bern}(r)$ are independent.
Show that $Z = XY$ is Bernoulli and find its parameter. Then show that
$W = X + Y - XY$ is also Bernoulli, and say in words what $W$ is.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$XY$ is 1 only when both are 1, and 0 otherwise. So it is a 0/1 variable with

$$P(Z = 1) = P(X = 1, Y = 1) = P(X = 1) P(Y = 1) = pr$$

by independence. So $Z \sim \text{Bern}(pr)$: it is the indicator of "both".

$W$ is 0 only when $X = Y = 0$. Check the 4 cases: $0+0-0 = 0$, $1+0-0 = 1$,
$0+1-0 = 1$, $1+1-1 = 1$. So $W$ is the indicator of "at least one", and

$$P(W = 1) = 1 - P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 1 - (1 - p)(1 - r)$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate the variance of $\text{Bern}(p)$ for
$p = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9$ with 100,000 samples each, and compare with $p(1 - p)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for p in [0.1, 0.5, 0.9]:
    x = rng.random(100_000) < p
    print(f"p = {p}: simulated var {x.var():.4f}, exact {p * (1 - p):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
p = 0.1: simulated var 0.0893, exact 0.0900
p = 0.5: simulated var 0.2500, exact 0.2500
p = 0.9: simulated var 0.0896, exact 0.0900
```

The values for $p = 0.1$ and $p = 0.9$ agree with each other, as the formula says.

</details>

## Summary

- A Bernoulli variable is one yes/no trial: 1 with probability $p$, 0 with
  probability $1 - p$.
- Its PMF can be written in one line as $p^k (1 - p)^{1 - k}$ for $k \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$.
- Because $X^2 = X$, the mean is $p$ and the variance is $p(1 - p)$, which is
  largest at $p = 0.5$.
- Every indicator $I_A$ is $\text{Bern}(P(A))$, and every two-valued variable is a
  shifted and scaled Bernoulli.
- In numpy, sample it with `rng.random(n) < p` or `rng.binomial(1, p, n)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{X \sim \text{Bern}(p) \implies E[X] = p, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1 - p)}
$$
