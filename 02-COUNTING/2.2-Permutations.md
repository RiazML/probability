# 2.2 Permutations

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.2 Permutations

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [2.1 Multiplication and Addition Rules](2.1-Multiplication-and-Addition-Rules.md)

> **Big idea:** The number of ways to put $k$ of $n$ different things in order, with no repeats, is $n (n-1) \cdots (n-k+1)$, and for all $n$ things it is $n!$.

---

## Why It Matters

Many questions ask "in how many orders?": the finishing order of a race, the order
of songs in a playlist, the order of cards after a shuffle. These are
**permutations**. They give the size of the sample space whenever order matters and
nothing repeats. They are also the first step to
[2.3 Combinations](2.3-Combinations.md), where we remove the order again.

## Intuition

Five friends stand in a line for a photo. Who stands first? Any of the 5. Once that
person is placed, 4 people are left for the second spot, then 3, then 2, then 1. By
the multiplication rule there are $5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 120$ different
photos.

Now only 3 of the 8 runners in a race win medals: gold, silver and bronze. Gold can
go to any of 8 runners, silver to any of the 7 left, bronze to any of the 6 left. That
is $8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336$ possible podiums. We stop after 3 steps, because only 3
spots exist.

The pattern: fill the spots one at a time, and each spot has one fewer choice than
the spot before it, because nothing can be used twice.

## Picture

```text
   n = 8 runners, k = 3 medals

     Gold        Silver       Bronze
   +------+     +------+     +------+
   |  8   |  x  |  7   |  x  |  6   |   =  336 ordered podiums
   +------+     +------+     +------+
   any of 8     any of the   any of the
   runners      7 left       6 left
```

Each slot has one fewer choice than the slot before it; multiply the slot counts.

## Definition

The **factorial** of a whole number $n$, written $n!$ and read "n factorial", is the
product of all whole numbers from 1 to $n$:

$$
n! = n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1, \qquad 0! = 1
$$

A **permutation** of $n$ different objects is an arrangement of all of them in a row.
A **$k$-permutation** of $n$ objects is an ordered list of $k$ of them, with no object
used twice. Their numbers are:

$$
\text{all } n \text{ in order: } n! \qquad\qquad \text{ordered } k \text{ of } n \text{: } \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} = \underbrace{n (n-1) \cdots (n-k+1)}_{k \text{ factors}}
$$

where $0 \le k \le n$. Some books write $P(n, k)$ or $n^{\underline{k}}$ for the second
count; in this book we write it out.

**In words:** to order $k$ of $n$ different things, multiply $k$ numbers, starting
at $n$ and going down by one each time.

**Why is $0! = 1$?** There is exactly one way to arrange nothing: do nothing. It also
makes the formula work for $k = n$: $\frac{n!}{(n-n)!} = \frac{n!}{0!} = n!$.

## Why It Is True

Fill $k$ ordered slots, one at a time, from $n$ different objects:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{slot 1} &: n \text{ choices} && \text{(any object)} \\
\text{slot 2} &: n - 1 \text{ choices} && \text{(any object except the one in slot 1)} \\
\text{slot } i &: n - (i-1) \text{ choices} && \text{(}i - 1\text{ objects are already used)} \\
\text{slot } k &: n - k + 1 \text{ choices}
\end{aligned}
$$

The number of choices at each slot does not depend on **which** objects were used,
only on **how many**. So the multiplication rule from
[2.1 Multiplication and Addition Rules](2.1-Multiplication-and-Addition-Rules.md)
applies:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n (n-1) \cdots (n-k+1) &= \frac{n (n-1) \cdots (n-k+1) \cdot (n-k)!}{(n-k)!} && \text{(multiply and divide by } (n-k)! \text{)} \\
&= \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} && \text{(the top is now } n \cdot (n-1) \cdots 1 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $k = n$ every object gets a slot, and the count is $n!$.

### Stirling's Approximation

Factorials grow very fast: $10! = 3628800$, and $52!$ has 68 digits. To estimate
them we use **Stirling's approximation**:

$$
n! \approx \sqrt{2 \pi n} \left( \frac{n}{e} \right)^n
$$

**In words:** $n!$ is close to $(n/e)^n$ times a small correction $\sqrt{2 \pi n}$. The
ratio of the two sides goes to 1 as $n$ grows (it is already above 0.99 for
$n = 10$, as the simulation below shows).

<details>
<summary>Where the main part comes from (optional)</summary>

Take logarithms: $\ln n! = \ln 1 + \ln 2 + \dots + \ln n$. This sum is close to the
area under the curve $\ln x$ from 1 to $n$, which is $n \ln n - n + 1$. So
$n!$ is roughly $e^{n \ln n - n} = (n/e)^n$. The extra factor $\sqrt{2 \pi n}$ needs a
more careful estimate of the gap between the sum and the area. Calculus starts in
[Module 08](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/README.md), so we do not prove it here.

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Guessing the Podium

**Problem.** Eight runners are equally good, so every finishing order is equally
likely. You guess the gold, silver and bronze winners in order. What is the
probability that your guess is exactly right?

**Solution.**

1. The outcome we care about is the ordered list (gold, silver, bronze): a
   3-permutation of 8 runners.
2. Count them:

$$
\frac{8!}{(8-3)!} = \frac{40320}{120} = 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336
$$

3. All 336 podiums are equally likely, and exactly one matches your guess.

**Answer.** $P(\text{exact podium}) = \frac{1}{336} \approx 0.003$.

**Check.** The chance that gold alone is right is $\frac{1}{8}$. Adding silver and bronze
must make it smaller, and $\frac{1}{336}$ is much smaller than $\frac{1}{8}$.

### Example 2: Three Friends Sitting Together

**Problem.** Eight people, including Ana, Ben and Chen, sit in a row of 8 seats in a
random order. What is the probability that Ana, Ben and Chen sit next to each other
(in any order)?

**Solution.**

1. All arrangements: $8! = 40320$, all equally likely.
2. Glue the three friends into one **block**. Now arrange 6 items (the block and the
   5 other people): $6! = 720$ ways.
3. Inside the block, the three friends can be in $3! = 6$ orders.
4. By the multiplication rule, favorable arrangements: $720 \cdot 6 = 4320$.

$$
P(\text{together}) = \frac{6! \cdot 3!}{8!} = \frac{4320}{40320} = \frac{3}{28} \approx 0.107
$$

**Answer.** About $0.107$, a little more than 1 in 10.

**Check.** Another way: the block can start at seat 1 to 6 (6 places), the friends have
$3!$ orders, and the other 5 people have $5!$ orders: $6 \cdot 6 \cdot 120 = 4320$. Same
count. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 3: How Many Ways to Shuffle a Deck

**Problem.** How many orders can a deck of 52 cards be in? Use Stirling's
approximation and compare with the exact value.

**Solution.**

1. Every order is a permutation of 52 different cards: $52!$ orders.
2. Stirling: $\sqrt{2 \pi \cdot 52} \approx 18.08$ and $(52/e)^{52} \approx 4.455 \times 10^{66}$.
   Their product is about $8.053 \times 10^{67}$.
3. The exact value is $52! \approx 8.066 \times 10^{67}$.

**Answer.** About $8 \times 10^{67}$ orders. Stirling is off by only about 0.16%.

**Check.** This number is so large that a well-shuffled deck is almost surely in an
order that no deck in history has ever been in before.

## Simulate It

The code shuffles 8 people many times to check Example 2, then tests Stirling's
approximation.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# People 0, 1, 2 are Ana, Ben and Chen. Shuffle all 8 into seats.
seats = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(8), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)
pos = np.argsort(seats, axis=1)[:, :3]          # seat number of each friend
together = pos.max(axis=1) - pos.min(axis=1) == 2
print(f"P(together): simulation {together.mean():.4f}, exact {3 / 28:.4f}")

# Stirling's approximation: the ratio should approach 1.
for n in [1, 5, 10, 20, 52, 100]:
    stirling = math.sqrt(2 * math.pi * n) * (n / math.e) ** n
    print(f"n = {n:>3}: n! = {math.factorial(n):.4e}, "
          f"Stirling = {stirling:.4e}, ratio = {stirling / math.factorial(n):.4f}")
```

Output:

## Common Mistakes

- **Using $n!$ when only $k$ spots exist.** A podium has 3 spots, so the count is
  $8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6$, not $8!$. Stop multiplying when the slots run out.
- **Using permutations when order does not matter.** A committee of 3 is the same
  committee in any order. Then divide by $3!$, as in [2.3 Combinations](2.3-Combinations.md).
- **Forgetting the order inside a block.** In Example 2 the friends can sit as ABC,
  ACB, BAC, and so on. Multiply by $3!$.
- **Thinking $0! = 0$.** By definition $0! = 1$; otherwise $\frac{n!}{0!}$ would divide
  by zero.
- **Using permutations when objects can repeat.** A 4-digit PIN can reuse digits, so it
  has $10^4$ values, not $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7$. See
  [2.4 Sampling With Replacement](2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** a sorting algorithm must handle all $n!$ input orders. Since
  each comparison has 2 results, it needs about $\log_2 n!$ comparisons in the worst
  case, and Stirling turns this into the famous $n \log_2 n$ lower bound.
- **Brute-force search:** the travelling salesman visiting $n$ cities in every
  possible order checks $n!$ routes; at $n = 20$ that is about $2.4 \times 10^{18}$.
- **AI and machine learning:** random permutations shuffle training data each epoch,
  and permutation tests compare a model's score to its score on shuffled labels.
- **Cards and games:** $52!$ deck orders; the probability of any particular order
  after a fair shuffle is $\frac{1}{52!}$.
- **Everyday life:** seating plans, schedules and rankings.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** How many different orders can the letters of the word MATH be written in?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Four different letters, all used once:

$$4! = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A club of 10 members elects a president, a vice president and a
secretary (three different people). How many results are possible?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The roles are different, so order matters:

$$10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = \frac{10!}{7!} = 720$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Five people, including Ana and Ben, stand in a random line. What is the
probability that Ana and Ben stand next to each other?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Glue Ana and Ben into a block: $4!$ arrangements of 4 items, times $2!$ orders inside
the block, out of $5!$:

$$P(\text{adjacent}) = \frac{4! \cdot 2!}{5!} = \frac{48}{120} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Six people sit around a round table. Two seatings count as the same if
everyone has the same left and right neighbors (one is a rotation of the other). How
many seatings are there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are $6!$ seatings in numbered chairs. Each round seating appears 6 times, once
for each rotation. So:

$$\frac{6!}{6} = 5! = 120$$

In general, $n$ people around a table give $(n-1)!$ seatings.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Use Stirling's approximation to estimate how many digits $100!$ has.
(Hint: a number $x \ge 1$ has $\lfloor \log_{10} x \rfloor + 1$ digits.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $\log_{10}$ of Stirling's formula:

$$\log_{10} 100! \approx \log_{10} \sqrt{2 \pi \cdot 100} + 100 \log_{10} \frac{100}{e} \approx 1.399 + 156.571 = 157.970$$

So $100! \approx 10^{157.97} \approx 9.33 \times 10^{157}$, which has
$157 + 1 = 158$ digits. The exact value of $100!$ does have 158 digits.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

## Summary

- A permutation is an ordered arrangement with no repeats.
- $n$ different objects can be put in a row in $n!$ ways, and $0! = 1$.
- Ordered selections of $k$ out of $n$: multiply $k$ numbers going down from $n$.
- For "these items must be together", glue them into a block and remember the
  order inside the block.
- Stirling's approximation $n! \approx \sqrt{2 \pi n} (n/e)^n$ estimates huge
  factorials with tiny relative error.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{ordered } k \text{ of } n \text{ without replacement} = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} = n (n-1) \cdots (n-k+1)}
$$
