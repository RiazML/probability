# 2.4 Sampling With Replacement

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.4 Sampling With Replacement

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [2.3 Combinations](2.3-Combinations.md)

> **Big idea:** When the same item may be picked again, ordered samples number $n^k$ and unordered samples number $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$, but only the ordered samples are equally likely.

---

## Why It Matters

Rolling dice, drawing digits for a PIN, hashing keys into buckets and building a
**bootstrap** sample in machine learning all pick from the same pool again and again.
This is **sampling with replacement**. Together with the last two topics it completes
the **counting table**: four formulas that cover every way to choose $k$ objects from $n$.
It also holds the most common trap in counting-based probability: outcomes that are
easy to count but **not** equally likely.

## Intuition

An urn holds 3 balls numbered 1, 2, 3. You draw a ball, write its number, **put it
back**, and draw again. Because the ball goes back, every draw has all 3 choices. Two
draws give $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ ordered results: 11, 12, 13, 21, 22, 23, 31, 32, 33.

Now suppose you only care **which** numbers you got, not the order: "12" and "21" are
both "one 1 and one 2". Then the results are the 6 groups {1,1}, {1,2}, {1,3},
{2,2}, {2,3}, {3,3}. Such a group, where repeats are allowed, is called a
**multiset**.

Here is the trap. The 9 ordered results are equally likely, each with probability
$\frac{1}{9}$. The 6 multisets are **not**: {1,1} comes only from "11" (probability
$\frac{1}{9}$), but {1,2} comes from "12" and "21" (probability $\frac{2}{9}$). Counting
multisets is fine for questions like "how many menus?", but for probability you
usually need the ordered outcomes.

## Picture

## Definition

**Sampling with replacement** means each chosen object is put back before the next
choice, so the same object can be chosen again. Choose $k$ times from $n$ different
objects.

**Ordered** (a sequence, such as a PIN):

$$
\text{number of sequences} = n^k
$$

**In words:** each of the $k$ draws has all $n$ choices, so multiply $n$ by itself $k$
times.

**Unordered** (a multiset, such as a bag of donuts):

$$
\text{number of multisets} = \binom{n+k-1}{k}
$$

where $n$ is the number of kinds and $k$ is the number of picks.

**In words:** choosing $k$ items from $n$ kinds, with repeats allowed and order
ignored, is like choosing $k$ items from $n + k - 1$ different items with no repeats.

> **Warning:** If the $k$ draws are random and independent, all $n^k$ **sequences** are
> equally likely, but the $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ **multisets** are not. A multiset with all
> items different comes from $k!$ sequences; one with every item the same comes from
> only 1. Use sequences when you compute probabilities.

## Why It Is True

**Ordered.** Draw 1 has $n$ choices. The object goes back, so draw 2 also has $n$
choices, whatever draw 1 was, and so on. By the multiplication rule from
[2.1](2.1-Multiplication-and-Addition-Rules.md) the count is $n \cdot n \cdots n = n^k$.

**Unordered.** Write the kinds as numbers $1, 2, \dots, n$. A multiset of size $k$ can
be written in sorted order, $a_1 \le a_2 \le \dots \le a_k$, in exactly one way. Now
**shift** each entry by its position:

$$
b_i = a_i + (i - 1), \qquad i = 1, \dots, k
$$

Here is why this turns multisets into ordinary subsets:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_{i+1} - b_i &= (a_{i+1} - a_i) + 1 \ge 1 && \text{(since } a_{i+1} \ge a_i \text{), so the } b_i \text{ are all different} \\
b_1 &= a_1 \ge 1 && \text{(smallest value)} \\
b_k &= a_k + k - 1 \le n + k - 1 && \text{(largest value)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\lbrace b_1, \dots, b_k \rbrace$ is a set of $k$ different numbers from
$\lbrace 1, \dots, n+k-1 \rbrace$. The shift can be undone with $a_i = b_i - (i - 1)$, and
any $k$-subset, sorted, undoes to a valid sorted multiset. So the shift is a
**bijection**: a perfect one-to-one matching between the two collections. Matched
collections have the same size, so the number of multisets is $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$.

For $n = 3$ and $k = 2$, the 6 multisets {1,1}, {1,2}, {1,3}, {2,2}, {2,3}, {3,3}
shift to the 6 subsets {1,2}, {1,3}, {1,4}, {2,3}, {2,4}, {3,4} of
$\lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4 \rbrace$, and $\binom{4}{2} = 6$. A second proof, with a picture of
stars and bars, is in [2.5 Stars and Bars](2.5-Stars-and-Bars.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Four Dice All Different

**Problem.** You roll 4 fair dice. What is the probability that all four show
different numbers?

**Solution.**

1. Think of the dice as rolled in order: die 1, die 2, die 3, die 4. This is ordered
   sampling with replacement from 6 faces, and all sequences are equally likely:
   $6^4 = 1296$ outcomes.
2. "All different" is ordered sampling **without** replacement:
   $6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 360$ outcomes.

$$
P(\text{all different}) = \frac{6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3}{6^4} = \frac{360}{1296} = \frac{5}{18} \approx 0.278
$$

**Answer.** $\frac{5}{18} \approx 0.278$.

**Check.** The same "without over with" pattern drives the
[birthday problem](2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md), with 365
days instead of 6 faces.

### Example 2: The Three-Dice Trap

**Problem.** You roll 3 dice and look only at the set of numbers, ignoring order. A
friend says: "There are $\binom{6+3-1}{3} = \binom{8}{3} = 56$ possible results, and 6 of them
are triples like (2, 2, 2). So $P(\text{triple}) = \frac{6}{56}$." Is this right?

**Solution.**

1. The count of 56 multisets is correct. But they are not equally likely, so
   "favorable over total" does not apply to them.
2. Use the equally likely ordered outcomes instead: $6^3 = 216$ sequences.
3. A triple is one of the 6 sequences (1,1,1), (2,2,2), ..., (6,6,6):

$$
P(\text{triple}) = \frac{6}{216} = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.028
$$

**Answer.** The friend is wrong: the true probability is $\frac{1}{36} \approx 0.028$, not
$\frac{6}{56} \approx 0.107$.

**Check.** The multiset {1, 2, 3} comes from $3! = 6$ sequences, while {2, 2, 2} comes
from only 1. The friend gave them equal weight. The simulation below shows the true
value.

### Example 3: Bootstrap Samples in Machine Learning

**Problem.** A **bootstrap sample** is made by drawing $n$ points, with replacement,
from a data set of $n$ points. It is used in bagging and random forests. With $n = 5$
points: (a) how many ordered bootstrap samples are there, and how many different
multisets? (b) What is the probability that a given point is **never** drawn?

**Solution.**

1. Ordered: $5^5 = 3125$ sequences, all equally likely.
2. Multisets: $\binom{5+5-1}{5} = \binom{9}{5} = 126$ different bootstrap samples.
3. A given point is missed by one draw with probability $\frac{4}{5}$. Count sequences
   that avoid it: each of the 5 draws has 4 choices, so $4^5 = 1024$ sequences.

$$
P(\text{point left out}) = \frac{4^5}{5^5} = \left( \frac{4}{5} \right)^5 = \frac{1024}{3125} \approx 0.328
$$

**Answer.** 3125 sequences, 126 multisets, and each point is left out with probability
about $0.328$.

**Check.** For $n$ points the same count gives $\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$, which for large $n$
is close to $\frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$ (for $n = 100$ it is $0.366$). So about 37% of the data is
**out of bag** for each bootstrap sample, and random forests use these points as a
free test set.

## Simulate It

The code checks the three-dice trap and the bootstrap "left out" probability.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Example 2: three dice, all equal?
dice = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(n_trials, 3))
triple = (dice[:, 0] == dice[:, 1]) & (dice[:, 1] == dice[:, 2])
print(f"P(triple): simulation {triple.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 36:.4f}, "
      f"wrong multiset answer {6 / 56:.4f}")

# Example 3: bootstrap samples of size n from n points. Is point 0 left out?
for n in [5, 100]:
    n_boot = 100_000
    samples = rng.integers(0, n, size=(n_boot, n))
    left_out = ~np.any(samples == 0, axis=1)
    print(f"n = {n:>3}: P(left out) simulation {left_out.mean():.4f}, "
          f"exact {(1 - 1 / n) ** n:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(triple): simulation 0.0278, exact 0.0278, wrong multiset answer 0.1071
n =   5: P(left out) simulation 0.3278, exact 0.3277
n = 100: P(left out) simulation 0.3647, exact 0.3660
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating multisets as equally likely.** This is the trap of Example 2. For
  probability, count ordered sequences, even when the question ignores order.
- **Using $n!/(n-k)!$ when repeats are allowed.** A PIN can be 7777, so there are
  $10^4$ PINs, not $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7$.
- **Swapping $n$ and $k$ in $n^k$.** The base is the number of choices per draw; the
  exponent is the number of draws. Three letters from 26 gives $26^3$, not $3^{26}$.
- **Forgetting that $k$ may be larger than $n$.** With replacement you can draw 10
  times from 3 objects: $3^{10}$ sequences and $\binom{12}{10} = 66$ multisets. Without
  replacement this would be impossible.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** bootstrap samples, bagging and random forests sample
  with replacement; mini-batches are often drawn with replacement too.
- **Computer science:** hashing $k$ keys into $n$ buckets is ordered sampling with
  replacement, with $n^k$ equally likely assignments when the hash is uniform. See
  [21.3.2 Hashing and Bloom Filters](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.2-Hashing-and-Bloom-Filters.md).
- **Statistics:** the bootstrap estimates the uncertainty of any statistic by
  resampling with replacement.
- **Physics:** the two ways of counting give two physical models. Distinguishable
  particles follow the $n^k$ count (Maxwell-Boltzmann); photons, which cannot be told
  apart, follow the multiset count (Bose-Einstein).
- **Everyday life:** dice games, PINs, and choosing several scoops of ice cream.

**1. (Easy)** How many 3-letter strings (like "aab" or "cat") can be made from the 26
lowercase letters if letters may repeat?

Ordered, with replacement:

$$26^3 = 17576$$

**2. (Easy)** An ice cream shop has 3 flavors. You order a cup with 4 scoops; repeats
are allowed and the order of scoops does not matter. How many different cups are
possible?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Unordered, with replacement, $n = 3$ kinds and $k = 4$ picks:

$$\binom{3+4-1}{4} = \binom{6}{4} = 15$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You draw 3 times, with replacement, from 10 balls numbered 0 to 9.
What is the probability that the 3 numbers are all different?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{all different}) = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{10^3} = \frac{720}{1000} = 0.72$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Two dice are rolled. List how many unordered results there are, and
give the probability of each kind of unordered result. Check that the probabilities
add to 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are $\binom{6+2-1}{2} = \binom{7}{2} = 21$ unordered results: 6 doubles and 15 pairs
with two different numbers. A double such as {3, 3} comes from 1 of the 36 ordered
outcomes, so it has probability $\frac{1}{36}$. A pair such as {2, 5} comes from 2 ordered
outcomes, (2, 5) and (5, 2), so it has probability $\frac{2}{36}$. Total:

$$6 \cdot \frac{1}{36} + 15 \cdot \frac{2}{36} = \frac{6}{36} + \frac{30}{36} = 1$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** How many **different** bootstrap samples (as multisets) can be made from
a data set of $n$ points? Compute the answer for $n = 10$, and compare it with the
number of ordered samples.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A bootstrap sample picks $k = n$ times from $n$ kinds, with repeats and without order:

$$\binom{n+n-1}{n} = \binom{2n-1}{n}$$

For $n = 10$ this is $\binom{19}{10} = 92378$ multisets, while there are
$10^{10}$ ordered samples. Most multisets are very unlikely; the single most likely
one is "every point exactly once", which comes from $10! = 3628800$ sequences.

</details>

import numpy as np

## Summary

- With replacement, an object can be chosen again, so each draw has all $n$ choices.
- Ordered samples: $n^k$. Unordered samples (multisets): $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$.
- The four cases (order or not, replacement or not) make the counting table.
- Random draws make the ordered sequences equally likely, not the multisets. For
  probabilities, count sequences.
- Each point is left out of a bootstrap sample with probability
  $\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx \frac{1}{e}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{with replacement: ordered } n^k, \qquad \text{unordered } \binom{n+k-1}{k}}
$$
