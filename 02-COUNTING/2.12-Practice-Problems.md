# 2.12 Practice Problems

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 3 hours · **Before this:** [2.11.3 Poker Hands](2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.3-Poker-Hands.md)

> **Big idea:** Counting is a skill you build by doing: for every problem, decide whether order matters and whether repeats are allowed, choose the tool, and check the answer a second way.

---

## How to Use This Set

- Work on paper first. Look at the answer only after you have a number.
- Each problem says which topic it uses, in brackets, so you can go back and reread it.
- Before you count, answer two questions: **does order matter?** and **can an item be
  chosen twice?** Then use the counting table from
  [2.4 Sampling With Replacement](2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md).
- Check every answer a second way: a complement, a small case you can list, or a
  simulation.
- The Warm-Up takes about 30 minutes, the Core about an hour, and the Challenge and
  the Simulation Projects about an hour and a half.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy) [2.1 Multiplication and Addition Rules]** A cafe offers 4 starters, 6
mains and 3 desserts. A meal is one starter and one main, with or without one dessert.
How many different meals are there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Dessert has $3 + 1 = 4$ options (one of the 3 desserts, or none; addition rule). Then
multiply:

$$4 \cdot 6 \cdot 4 = 96$$

</details>

**W2. (Easy) [2.2 Permutations]** Six different books are put on a shelf in a random
order. What is the probability that the two math books end up at the two ends?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

All orders: $6! = 720$. Favorable: the math books take the two ends in $2! = 2$ orders,
and the other 4 books fill the middle in $4! = 24$ orders:

$$P = \frac{2 \cdot 24}{720} = \frac{48}{720} = \frac{1}{15} \approx 0.067$$

Check: the left end is a math book with probability $\frac{2}{6}$, and then the right end is
the other math book with probability $\frac{1}{5}$; $\frac{2}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{5} = \frac{1}{15}$.

</details>

**W3. (Easy) [2.3 Combinations]** A teacher picks 3 of her 12 students for a trip. How
many groups are possible? If Ana is one of the 12, what is the probability that she is
picked?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\binom{12}{3} = \frac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10}{6} = 220$$

Groups with Ana: choose her 2 companions from the other 11, $\binom{11}{2} = 55$:

$$P(\text{Ana picked}) = \frac{55}{220} = \frac{1}{4}$$

This makes sense: 3 of 12 places, so each student has a $\frac{3}{12}$ chance.

</details>

**W4. (Easy) [2.4 Sampling With Replacement]** A coupon code has 5 characters, each an
uppercase letter or a digit, and characters may repeat. How many codes are there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each character has $26 + 10 = 36$ choices, and order matters:

$$36^5 = 60466176$$

</details>

**W5. (Easy) [2.9 Pigeonhole Principle]** What is the smallest number of people that
guarantees two of them share a birthday? (Count February 29 as a possible birthday.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are 366 possible birthdays (boxes). With 366 people everyone could still have a
different day; with $366 + 1 = 367$ people, two must share. Answer: 367.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium) [2.11.3 Poker Hands]** How many 5-card hands are a full house? Among all
full houses, what fraction have three aces?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rank of the three (13), its suits ($\binom{4}{3} = 4$), rank of the pair (12), its suits
($\binom{4}{2} = 6$):

$$13 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 = 3744$$

With three aces the first rank is fixed: $1 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 = 288$. The fraction is
$\frac{288}{3744} = \frac{1}{13}$, which makes sense: each of the 13 ranks is equally likely to be the
three of a kind.

</details>

**C2. (Medium) [2.11.1 Birthday Problem]** On a planet with a 100-day year (all days
equally likely), what is the smallest group for which a shared birthday has probability
above one half? Compare with the approximation $n \approx \sqrt{2 N \ln 2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Compute $1 - \frac{100 \cdot 99 \cdots (100 - n + 1)}{100^n}$ for growing $n$:

$$n = 12: \ 0.4968, \qquad n = 13: \ 0.5572$$

So the answer is 13. The approximation gives $\sqrt{200 \ln 2} \approx 11.8$, so it suggests
12. For small $N$ the approximation is a little too optimistic about "no match" (see
Practice 5 of [2.11.1](2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md)); always
confirm with the exact formula.

</details>

**C3. (Medium) [2.5 Stars and Bars]** How many solutions does $a + b + c + d = 12$ have in
non-negative integers with $a \le 3$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

All solutions: $\binom{12+3}{3} = \binom{15}{3} = 455$. Bad solutions have $a \ge 4$; put
$a' = a - 4$, so $a' + b + c + d = 8$: $\binom{11}{3} = 165$.

$$455 - 165 = 290$$

</details>

**C4. (Medium) [2.6 Multinomial Coefficients]** How many different arrangements of the
letters of STATISTICS are there? If one is chosen at random, what is the probability
that it starts with S?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Letters: S three times, T three times, I twice, A once, C once ($3 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 10$):

$$\frac{10!}{3! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 2! \thinspace 1! \thinspace 1!} = \frac{3628800}{72} = 50400$$

Starting with S: the other 9 letters have S twice, T three times, I twice, A, C:
$\frac{9!}{2! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 2!} = \frac{362880}{24} = 15120$. So

$$P(\text{starts with S}) = \frac{15120}{50400} = \frac{3}{10}$$

Check: 3 of the 10 letter tiles are S, and the first tile is equally likely to be any of
them.

</details>

**C5. (Medium) [2.8 Inclusion-Exclusion]** How many whole numbers from 1 to 1000 are
divisible by **none** of 2, 3, 5 and 7?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Singles: $500 + 333 + 200 + 142 = 1175$. Pairs (by 6, 10, 14, 15, 21, 35):
$166 + 100 + 71 + 66 + 47 + 28 = 478$. Triples (by 30, 42, 70, 105): $33 + 23 + 14 + 9 = 79$.
All four (by 210): $4$.

$$\lvert \text{union} \rvert = 1175 - 478 + 79 - 4 = 772$$

$$\text{none} = 1000 - 772 = 228$$

</details>

**C6. (Medium) [2.7 Binomial Theorem and Pascal's Triangle]** A fair coin is flipped 8
times. What is the probability of at least 6 heads?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{at least 6}) = \frac{\binom{8}{6} + \binom{8}{7} + \binom{8}{8}}{2^8} = \frac{28 + 8 + 1}{256} = \frac{37}{256} \approx 0.145$$

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard) [2.11.2 Derangements and Matching]** Show that the probability that nobody
gets their own hat back among $n$ people tends to $\frac{1}{e}$. Then find the probability
that **exactly 2** of 6 people get their own hats.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $A_i$ be "person $i$ gets their own hat". Any $k$ of these happen together in
$(n-k)!$ permutations, and there are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways to choose the $k$ people. By
inclusion-exclusion, the number of permutations with no match is

$$D_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n-k)! = n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$$

Dividing by $n!$ gives $P(\text{no match}) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$. This is the start of the
series $e^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$, and the error is less than $\frac{1}{(n+1)!}$, which
goes to 0. So the probability tends to $\frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$.

Exactly 2 of 6: choose the 2 lucky people ($\binom{6}{2} = 15$) and derange the other 4
($D_4 = 9$):

$$P = \frac{15 \cdot 9}{720} = \frac{135}{720} = 0.1875$$

</details>

**H2. (Hard) [2.10 Story Proofs]** Prove that $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}$ with a
story.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A class has $n$ girls and $n$ boys. The right side counts the committees of $n$ people
from all $2n$. Count them again by the number $k$ of girls: choose $k$ girls
($\binom{n}{k}$ ways) and $n - k$ boys ($\binom{n}{n-k}$ ways). Since $\binom{n}{n-k} = \binom{n}{k}$, the
case with $k$ girls has $\binom{n}{k}^2$ committees. The cases $k = 0, \dots, n$ are disjoint and
cover every committee, so adding them gives $\binom{2n}{n}$. For $n = 3$:
$1 + 9 + 9 + 1 = 20 = \binom{6}{3}$.

</details>

**H3. (Hard) [2.5 Stars and Bars]** Four fair dice are rolled. What is the probability
that the sum is 14?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Put $y_i = x_i - 1$, so $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 + y_4 = 10$ with $0 \le y_i \le 5$. All non-negative
solutions: $\binom{13}{3} = 286$. Bad ones have some $y_i \ge 6$; for a given $i$, put
$y_i' = y_i - 6$, leaving a sum of 4: $\binom{7}{3} = 35$ each, so $4 \cdot 35 = 140$. Two
variables cannot both be $\ge 6$ (that needs at least 12). So there are $286 - 140 = 146$
outcomes:

$$P(\text{sum} = 14) = \frac{146}{6^4} = \frac{146}{1296} \approx 0.113$$

</details>

**H4. (Hard) [2.8 Inclusion-Exclusion]** A die is rolled 10 times. What is the
probability that all six faces appear at least once?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $A_f$ be "face $f$ never appears". If $j$ given faces are missing, each roll has
$6 - j$ choices: $(6 - j)^{10}$ sequences. By inclusion-exclusion, the number of
sequences that use every face is

$$\sum_{j=0}^{6} (-1)^j \binom{6}{j} (6-j)^{10} = 60466176 - 58593750 + 15728640 - 1180980 + 15360 - 6 + 0 = 16435440$$

$$P(\text{all faces}) = \frac{16435440}{6^{10}} = \frac{16435440}{60466176} \approx 0.272$$

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. [2.11.1 Birthday Problem]** Simulate the birthday problem for group sizes 1 to 60.
For each size, estimate the probability of a shared birthday, compare it with the exact
formula, and find where it first passes one half.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
first_above_half = None
for n in range(1, 61):
    days = np.sort(rng.integers(0, 365, size=(n_trials, n)), axis=1)
    sim = np.mean(np.any(days[:, 1:] == days[:, :-1], axis=1))
    exact = 1 - np.prod((365 - np.arange(n)) / 365)
    if first_above_half is None and exact > 0.5:
        first_above_half = n
    if n in (10, 20, 22, 23, 30, 40, 50, 60):
        print(f"n = {n:>2}: simulation {sim:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"First n with exact probability above 0.5: {first_above_half}")
```

Output:

```text
n = 10: simulation 0.1170, exact 0.1169
n = 20: simulation 0.4097, exact 0.4114
n = 22: simulation 0.4740, exact 0.4757
n = 23: simulation 0.5092, exact 0.5073
n = 30: simulation 0.7056, exact 0.7063
n = 40: simulation 0.8923, exact 0.8912
n = 50: simulation 0.9710, exact 0.9704
n = 60: simulation 0.9941, exact 0.9941
First n with exact probability above 0.5: 23
```

</details>

**S2. [2.11.2 Derangements and Matching]** Simulate the hat-check problem for
$n = 3, 5, 10$ and $50$ people. Estimate the probability that nobody gets their own hat,
and compare it with $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$ and with $\frac{1}{e}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from math import factorial, e

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000
for n in [3, 5, 10, 50]:
    perms = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(n), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)
    no_match = np.mean(np.all(perms != np.arange(n), axis=1))
    exact = sum((-1) ** k / factorial(k) for k in range(n + 1))
    print(f"n = {n:>2}: simulation {no_match:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}, 1/e {1 / e:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =  3: simulation 0.3332, exact 0.3333, 1/e 0.3679
n =  5: simulation 0.3661, exact 0.3667, 1/e 0.3679
n = 10: simulation 0.3679, exact 0.3679, 1/e 0.3679
n = 50: simulation 0.3684, exact 0.3679, 1/e 0.3679
```

</details>
