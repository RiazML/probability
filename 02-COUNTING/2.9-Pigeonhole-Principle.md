# 2.9 Pigeonhole Principle

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.9 Pigeonhole Principle

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [2.8 Inclusion-Exclusion](2.8-Inclusion-Exclusion.md)

> **Big idea:** If you put more objects than boxes, some box must hold at least two objects; more generally, some box holds at least the average, rounded up.

---

## Why It Matters

Most of this module counts outcomes to find a probability. The **pigeonhole
principle** is different: it proves that something is **certain** (probability 1),
with no calculation at all. It tells you when hash collisions cannot be avoided, why
no compression program can shrink every file, and why the birthday problem stops
being a question at 367 people. It also marks the edge where probability starts: below
the pigeonhole limit, collisions are only likely, and
[2.11.1 Birthday Problem](2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md)
computes how likely.

## Intuition

Ten pigeons fly into nine holes. Can every hole have at most one pigeon? No: nine holes
with at most one pigeon each hold at most nine pigeons, but there are ten. So some
hole has two or more. We do not know **which** hole, and we do not need to.

A drawer holds black and white socks, mixed up, and the room is dark. How many socks
must you take to be sure of a matching pair? Two socks might be one black and one
white. But with three socks and only two colors, two of them must share a color.
Three socks are enough, whatever the drawer holds.

The same idea scales up. If 10,000 keys go into 1,000 hash buckets, the buckets hold 10
keys on average, so at least one bucket holds 10 or more. Some box always gets at least
the average.

## Picture

```text
   9 boxes, 10 objects:

   +---+---+---+---+---+---+---+---+---+
   | o | o | o | o | o | o | o | o |o o|   <- some box must get 2
   +---+---+---+---+---+---+---+---+---+

   General rule:  N objects in k boxes  ->  some box holds at least
                  ceil(N / k) objects      (here ceil(10 / 9) = 2)
```

If every box held fewer than $\lceil N/k \rceil$ objects, the boxes together would hold
fewer than $N$ objects.

## Definition

The **ceiling** of a number $x$, written $\lceil x \rceil$, is the smallest whole number
that is $\ge x$. For example, $\lceil 2.1 \rceil = 3$ and $\lceil 4 \rceil = 4$.

**Pigeonhole principle.** If $n + 1$ or more objects are placed into $n$ boxes, then at
least one box contains at least 2 objects.

**Generalized pigeonhole principle.** If $N$ objects are placed into $k$ boxes, then at
least one box contains at least

$$
\left\lceil \frac{N}{k} \right\rceil
$$

objects, where $N$ is the number of objects and $k \ge 1$ is the number of boxes.

**In words:** some box always gets at least the average number of objects, rounded up.

In the language of functions: if $f$ maps a finite set $A$ into a finite set $B$ and
$\lvert A \rvert \gt \lvert B \rvert$, then $f$ is not one-to-one: two different elements
of $A$ have the same image. Here $\lvert A \rvert$ is the number of elements in $A$.

## Why It Is True

We prove the general version by **contradiction**: assume it is false and show that
this leads to something impossible.

Let $m = \lceil N/k \rceil$. Suppose every box held at most $m - 1$ objects. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{total objects} &\le k (m - 1) && \text{(}k \text{ boxes, at most } m - 1 \text{ each)} \\
&\lt k \cdot \frac{N}{k} && \text{(since } m - 1 \lt N/k \text{, by the definition of the ceiling)} \\
&= N
\end{aligned}
$$

So the boxes would hold fewer than $N$ objects. But they hold exactly $N$. This is
impossible, so some box holds at least $m$ objects. The basic version is the case
$N = n + 1$ and $k = n$, where $m = \lceil (n+1)/n \rceil = 2$.

Why is $m - 1 \lt N/k$? Because $m$ is the **smallest** whole number $\ge N/k$, the whole
number $m - 1$ must be below $N/k$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Hash Collisions

**Problem.** A hash table has 1,000 buckets. (a) If 1,001 keys are stored, must two keys
share a bucket? (b) If 10,000 keys are stored, what can you say about the fullest bucket?
(c) What is the probability of at least one collision in case (a)?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Keys are objects, buckets are boxes. $1001 \gt 1000$, so some bucket gets at
   least 2 keys, whatever the hash function.
2. (b) The generalized principle gives a bucket with at least
   $\lceil 10000 / 1000 \rceil = 10$ keys.
3. (c) A collision is certain, so its probability is exactly 1. No hash function, however
   clever, can avoid it.

**Answer.** (a) Yes. (b) Some bucket holds at least 10 keys. (c) $P(\text{collision}) = 1$.

**Check.** The principle gives only a **lower bound**. With a random hash, the fullest
bucket usually holds many more than 10 keys; the simulation below shows this.

### Example 2: No Compressor Shrinks Every File

**Problem.** A **lossless compressor** turns each file into a shorter or equal file, and
different files must give different outputs (otherwise you could not get the original
back). Show that no lossless compressor makes **every** file of $n$ bits strictly shorter.

**Solution.**

1. There are $2^n$ files of exactly $n$ bits (multiplication rule).
2. Strictly shorter outputs have length $0, 1, \dots, n-1$. Their number is

$$
2^0 + 2^1 + \dots + 2^{n-1} = 2^n - 1
$$

3. So $2^n$ files (objects) would have to go into $2^n - 1$ shorter outputs (boxes). By the
   pigeonhole principle, two files get the same output, and the compressor cannot
   tell them apart.

**Answer.** Impossible: every lossless compressor leaves some file the same length or
longer.

**Check.** Real compressors work because real files are not random: text and images
have patterns. A file of random bits almost never gets shorter.

### Example 3: Socks and Cards

**Problem.** (a) A drawer has 10 black, 10 white and 10 grey socks. How many socks must
you take, in the dark, to be sure of a matching pair? (b) How many cards must you draw
from a 52-card deck to be sure of 3 cards of the same suit?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The boxes are the 3 colors. With 3 socks you could have one of each color. With
   $3 + 1 = 4$ socks, some color has 2. Answer: 4.
2. (b) The boxes are the 4 suits. We need some box with at least 3, that is
   $\lceil N/4 \rceil \ge 3$, which first holds at $N = 9$, since $\lceil 9/4 \rceil = 3$.
   With 8 cards you could have exactly 2 of each suit, so 8 is not enough.

**Answer.** (a) 4 socks. (b) 9 cards.

**Check.** In each case we showed both sides: the number is enough (pigeonhole) and
one less is not enough (an example that fails).

## Simulate It

The code hashes keys into 1,000 buckets at random. With 1,001 keys a collision happens
in every trial. With 10,000 keys the fullest bucket always holds at least 10 keys,
and usually far more.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_buckets, n_trials = 1_000, 2_000

# Case (a): 1,001 keys. Is there always a bucket with 2 or more keys?
always = all(np.bincount(rng.integers(0, n_buckets, 1_001)).max() >= 2
             for _ in range(n_trials))
print(f"1,001 keys: collision in every trial? {always}")

# Case (b): 10,000 keys. Size of the fullest bucket in each trial.
fullest = np.array([np.bincount(rng.integers(0, n_buckets, 10_000)).max()
                    for _ in range(n_trials)])
print(f"10,000 keys: smallest fullest bucket seen = {fullest.min()} "
      f"(pigeonhole bound {int(np.ceil(10_000 / n_buckets))})")
print(f"             average fullest bucket      = {fullest.mean():.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
1,001 keys: collision in every trial? True
10,000 keys: smallest fullest bucket seen = 18 (pigeonhole bound 10)
             average fullest bucket      = 21.7
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking pigeonhole tells you which box.** It proves that a full box exists, not
  where it is. Finding it may still need a search.
- **Thinking fewer objects than boxes means no collision.** With 23 people and 365
  days a shared birthday is not certain, but it already has probability above 0.5.
  Pigeonhole gives certainty; the [birthday problem](2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md)
  gives the probability below the limit.
- **Forgetting to round up.** 10 objects in 3 boxes give a box with at least
  $\lceil 10/3 \rceil = 4$ objects, not 3.33, and not just 3.
- **Not checking that the bound is tight.** To answer "how many are needed", show that
  one fewer can fail, as in Example 3.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** hash collisions are unavoidable once keys outnumber buckets;
  every hash function from long inputs to short outputs has collisions, which is why
  cryptographic hashes aim only to make them **hard to find**.
- **Data compression:** no lossless method shrinks all inputs (Example 2).
- **AI and machine learning:** a model with fewer possible internal states than inputs
  must map different inputs to the same state, so it must lose some information.
- **Probability:** the "averaging" form says that some outcome is at least the average;
  for random variables this becomes "some value is at least the mean", see
  [6.1 Expected Value](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.1-Expected-Value.md).
- **Everyday life:** socks in the dark, and why a large school must have two
  students with the same birthday.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Show that in any group of 13 people, two were born in the same month.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

People are objects and the 12 months are boxes. $13 \gt 12$, so some month holds at least
2 people.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** 100 emails arrive at 7 servers. What is the smallest number of emails the
busiest server can receive?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\left\lceil \frac{100}{7} \right\rceil = \lceil 14.29 \rceil = 15$$

And 15 is possible: for example, two servers get 15 and five servers get 14
($30 + 70 = 100$).

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Show that if you choose any 51 numbers from $1, 2, \dots, 100$, two of them
are consecutive (differ by 1).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Make 50 boxes: $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace, \lbrace 3, 4 \rbrace, \dots, \lbrace 99, 100 \rbrace$. The 51 chosen
numbers go into 50 boxes, so some box holds two chosen numbers. The two numbers in a
box are consecutive. (With 50 numbers it can fail: take all the odd numbers.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Five points are placed anywhere inside a $2 \times 2$ square. Show that two of
them are at distance at most $\sqrt{2}$ from each other.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cut the square into four $1 \times 1$ squares. Five points in four small squares: some
small square holds two points (count a point on a shared edge in just one of the
squares). Two points in a $1 \times 1$ square are at most its diagonal apart:

$$\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** At a party with $n \ge 2$ people, some pairs shake hands (nobody shakes
their own hand, and each pair shakes at most once). Prove that two people shook the
same number of hands.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each person shook between $0$ and $n - 1$ hands: that is $n$ possible numbers for $n$
people, so pigeonhole does not apply yet. But $0$ and $n - 1$ cannot both happen: if
someone shook hands with all $n - 1$ others, then nobody shook 0 hands. So the $n$ people
use at most $n - 1$ different numbers (either $0, \dots, n-2$ or $1, \dots, n-1$). With $n$
people (objects) and $n - 1$ numbers (boxes), two people have the same number.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4: place 5 random points in a $2 \times 2$ square many
times, and confirm that the smallest distance between two points is never above
$\sqrt{2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
pts = rng.uniform(0, 2, size=(n_trials, 5, 2))        # 5 points per trial
diff = pts[:, :, None, :] - pts[:, None, :, :]
dist = np.sqrt((diff ** 2).sum(axis=3))
dist[:, np.arange(5), np.arange(5)] = np.inf           # ignore a point vs itself
closest = dist.min(axis=(1, 2))
print(f"Largest 'closest pair' distance seen: {closest.max():.4f}")
print(f"Pigeonhole bound sqrt(2):             {np.sqrt(2):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Largest 'closest pair' distance seen: 1.0924
Pigeonhole bound sqrt(2):             1.4142
```

</details>

## Summary

- More objects than boxes: some box has at least 2.
- $N$ objects in $k$ boxes: some box has at least $\lceil N/k \rceil$.
- The proof is by contradiction: if every box were below the bound, the total would be
  too small.
- Pigeonhole gives **certainty** (probability 1); counting and probability tell you how
  likely a collision is before that point.
- To find "how many are needed", show the bound works and that one fewer can fail.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{N \text{ objects in } k \text{ boxes} \implies \text{some box holds at least } \left\lceil \frac{N}{k} \right\rceil}
$$
