# 2.6 Multinomial Coefficients

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.6 Multinomial Coefficients

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [2.5 Stars and Bars](2.5-Stars-and-Bars.md)

> **Big idea:** The number of ways to split $n$ different objects into labeled groups of sizes $n_1, \dots, n_r$ is $\frac{n!}{n_1! \thinspace n_2! \cdots n_r!}$: arrange everything, then divide out the orders inside each group.

---

## Why It Matters

A binomial coefficient splits objects into **two** groups: chosen and not chosen.
Real problems often have more: tasks split among three servers, the letters of a word
with repeated letters, a die rolled 12 times showing each face twice. The
**multinomial coefficient** handles any number of groups. It is the counting part of
the multinomial distribution
([7.9 Multinomial](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.9-Multinomial.md)), which models word
counts, votes and class labels.

## Intuition

How many different words can you make from the letters of BANANA? If all 6 letters
were different, there would be $6! = 720$ orders. But the three A's look the same.
Take any word, say ANABAN. Swapping the three A's among their places gives the same
word, and there are $3! = 6$ ways to do that. Swapping the two N's also changes
nothing: $2! = 2$ ways. So each word appears $3! \cdot 2! = 12$ times among the 720
orders, and the number of different words is $\frac{720}{12} = 60$.

Another way to see it: a word is fixed by **where** each letter goes. Choose the 3
places for A: $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ ways. Choose 2 of the 3 remaining places for N:
$\binom{3}{2} = 3$ ways. B takes the last place: 1 way. Total $20 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 60$. Same
answer, two methods.

## Picture

```text
   n = 6 places:   [ 1 ]  [ 2 ]  [ 3 ]  [ 4 ]  [ 5 ]  [ 6 ]

   step 1: choose 3 places for A    C(6, 3) = 20
   step 2: choose 2 places for N    C(3, 2) =  3     (3 places left)
   step 3: choose 1 place  for B    C(1, 1) =  1     (1 place left)
                                    --------------
                                    20 x 3 x 1 = 60  =  6! / (3! 2! 1!)
```

Filling the groups one after another gives a product of binomial coefficients, and
the product always simplifies to one fraction of factorials.

## Definition

Let $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_r$ be whole numbers $\ge 0$ with $n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_r = n$. The
**multinomial coefficient** is

$$
\binom{n}{n_1, n_2, \dots, n_r} = \frac{n!}{n_1! \thinspace n_2! \cdots n_r!}
$$

It counts two things that turn out to be the same:

- the number of ways to split $n$ **different** objects into $r$ **labeled** groups,
  with $n_1$ objects in group 1, $n_2$ in group 2, and so on (order inside a group does
  not matter);
- the number of different rows made of $n_1$ copies of symbol 1, $n_2$ copies of
  symbol 2, ..., $n_r$ copies of symbol $r$.

With $r = 2$ it is the binomial coefficient: $\binom{n}{k, n-k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} = \binom{n}{k}$.

**In words:** arrange all $n$ objects in $n!$ ways, then divide by the orders inside
each group, because those orders do not create anything new.

## Why It Is True

**Proof 1: choose the groups one after another.** Choose group 1 from all $n$ objects,
then group 2 from the $n - n_1$ that are left, and so on:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ways} &= \binom{n}{n_1} \binom{n - n_1}{n_2} \cdots \binom{n_r}{n_r} && \text{(multiplication rule)} \\
&= \frac{n!}{n_1! \thinspace (n - n_1)!} \cdot \frac{(n - n_1)!}{n_2! \thinspace (n - n_1 - n_2)!} \cdots && \text{(write each binomial in factorials)} \\
&= \frac{n!}{n_1! \thinspace n_2! \cdots n_r!} && \text{(each new top cancels the last bottom)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last factor is $\binom{n_r}{n_r} = 1$, and the leftover $0! = 1$ at the end
disappears.

**Proof 2: divide out the repeats.** Put all $n$ objects in a row: $n!$ ways. Let the
first $n_1$ places be group 1, the next $n_2$ places be group 2, and so on. Every split
into groups appears many times, once for each way to reorder group 1 ($n_1!$ ways),
group 2 ($n_2!$ ways), and so on. So each split appears exactly
$n_1! \thinspace n_2! \cdots n_r!$ times, and dividing gives the formula.

**The multinomial theorem.** Expanding $(x_1 + \dots + x_r)^n$ means picking one $x_i$
from each of the $n$ brackets. The term $x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_r^{n_r}$ appears once for each
way to say which brackets gave $x_1$, which gave $x_2$, and so on. That is a split of the
$n$ brackets into labeled groups, so:

$$
(x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_r)^n = \sum_{n_1 + \dots + n_r = n} \frac{n!}{n_1! \cdots n_r!} \thinspace x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2} \cdots x_r^{n_r}
$$

**In words:** the coefficient of $x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_r^{n_r}$ is the multinomial coefficient.
The case $r = 2$ is the binomial theorem of [2.7](2.7-Binomial-Theorem-and-Pascals-Triangle.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Mississippi

**Problem.** How many different arrangements of the letters of MISSISSIPPI are there?
If the 11 letter tiles are shuffled at random, what is the probability that they spell
MISSISSIPPI?

**Solution.**

1. Count the letters: M once, I four times, S four times, P twice. Check:
   $1 + 4 + 4 + 2 = 11$.
2. Apply the formula:

$$
\frac{11!}{1! \thinspace 4! \thinspace 4! \thinspace 2!} = \frac{39916800}{1 \cdot 24 \cdot 24 \cdot 2} = \frac{39916800}{1152} = 34650
$$

3. A random shuffle of the 11 tiles makes all $11!$ orders of the **tiles** equally
   likely. Each word comes from exactly $1! \thinspace 4! \thinspace 4! \thinspace 2! = 1152$ tile orders, so
   all 34650 words are equally likely too.

**Answer.** 34650 words, and $P(\text{MISSISSIPPI}) = \frac{1}{34650} \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-5}$.

**Check.** Step by step: $\binom{11}{1} \binom{10}{4} \binom{6}{4} \binom{2}{2} = 11 \cdot 210 \cdot 15 \cdot 1 = 34650$.

### Example 2: Jobs on Three Servers

**Problem.** Ten different jobs are sent to three servers A, B and C. (a) How many
ways put exactly 5 jobs on A, 3 on B and 2 on C? (b) If each job picks a server at
random, all three equally likely and independently, what is the probability of that
5, 3, 2 split?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Split 10 different jobs into labeled groups of sizes 5, 3, 2:

$$
\frac{10!}{5! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 2!} = \frac{3628800}{120 \cdot 6 \cdot 2} = \frac{3628800}{1440} = 2520
$$

2. (b) Each job has 3 choices, so there are $3^{10} = 59049$ equally likely assignments
   (ordered sampling with replacement, [2.4](2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md)).

$$
P(5, 3, 2) = \frac{2520}{3^{10}} = \frac{2520}{59049} \approx 0.0427
$$

**Answer.** 2520 ways, and probability about $0.0427$.

**Check.** Adding the multinomial coefficients over **all** splits must give all
$3^{10}$ assignments; this is the multinomial theorem with $x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = 1$. The
probability in (b) is one value of the multinomial distribution.

### Example 3: Groups Without Labels

**Problem.** Six players are split into 3 pairs for the first round of a tennis
tournament. The pairs have no names or courts. How many ways are there?

**Solution.**

1. If the pairs were labeled (court 1, court 2, court 3), the count would be
   $\frac{6!}{2! \thinspace 2! \thinspace 2!} = \frac{720}{8} = 90$.
2. Without labels, the same three pairs on different courts are the same split.
   The 3 pairs can be given court labels in $3! = 6$ ways, so each unlabeled split is
   counted 6 times:

$$
\frac{90}{3!} = \frac{90}{6} = 15
$$

**Answer.** 15 ways.

**Check.** Direct count: player 1 has 5 possible partners. Of the 4 players left,
the lowest-numbered one has 3 possible partners. The last 2 players form the last
pair. Total $5 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 15$.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2 by sending 10 jobs to random servers many times, and checks
the BANANA count by random shuffles.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Example 2: each of 10 jobs picks server 0, 1 or 2 at random.
servers = rng.integers(0, 3, size=(n_trials, 10))
sizes = np.stack([(servers == s).sum(axis=1) for s in range(3)], axis=1)
hit = (sizes[:, 0] == 5) & (sizes[:, 1] == 3) & (sizes[:, 2] == 2)
print(f"P(5, 3, 2 split): simulation {hit.mean():.4f}, exact {2520 / 3**10:.4f}")

# BANANA: shuffle the 6 tiles; how often do we get the word BANANA back?
tiles = np.array(list("BANANA"))
shuffles = rng.permuted(np.tile(tiles, (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)
same = np.all(shuffles == tiles, axis=1)
print(f"P(spells BANANA): simulation {same.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 60:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(5, 3, 2 split): simulation 0.0430, exact 0.0427
P(spells BANANA): simulation 0.0165, exact 0.0167
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting a repeated letter.** In MISSISSIPPI both I and S appear 4 times.
  Write the letter counts first and check that they add to $n$.
- **Ignoring whether groups have labels.** Three named teams of 2 give 90 splits;
  three unnamed pairs give 15. Divide by the orderings of **equal-size** unlabeled
  groups.
- **Thinking all splits are equally likely.** When each job picks a server at random,
  the split 10, 0, 0 has 1 assignment but 4, 3, 3 has 4200. Count assignments, not
  splits.
- **Using the multinomial for identical objects.** If the 10 jobs were identical, a
  split would be just its sizes, counted by stars and bars
  ([2.5](2.5-Stars-and-Bars.md)), not by the multinomial.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the multinomial distribution of word counts (bag of
  words, Naive Bayes for text) uses the multinomial coefficient. See
  [21.1.1 Probabilistic Classifiers and Naive Bayes](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md).
- **Computer science:** load balancing (how many ways jobs can be spread over
  machines), and counting strings with given letter frequencies, which is the basis
  of entropy and compression bounds.
- **Biology:** counting DNA sequences with a given number of A, C, G and T.
- **Physics:** the number of microstates with given occupation numbers, the starting
  point of statistical mechanics.
- **Games and polls:** the probability of a given split of votes, dice faces or cards.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** How many different arrangements of the letters of LEVEL are there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

L twice, E twice, V once:

$$\frac{5!}{2! \thinspace 2! \thinspace 1!} = \frac{120}{4} = 30$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Nine students are split into three labeled teams (Red, Green, Blue) of
3 students each. How many ways are there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\frac{9!}{3! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 3!} = \frac{362880}{216} = 1680$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** What is the coefficient of $x^2 y^3 z$ in $(x + y + z)^6$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the multinomial theorem, with exponents 2, 3, 1 (which add to 6):

$$\frac{6!}{2! \thinspace 3! \thinspace 1!} = \frac{720}{12} = 60$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** The nine students of Problem 2 are split into three study groups of
3, with no names. How many ways are there now?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each unlabeled split can be labeled in $3! = 6$ ways, so:

$$\frac{1680}{3!} = \frac{1680}{6} = 280$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that the multinomial coefficients with $r$ groups add up to $r^n$:
the sum of $\frac{n!}{n_1! \cdots n_r!}$ over all $n_1 + \dots + n_r = n$ equals $r^n$. Give a
counting proof, not an algebra proof.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Count the ways to give each of $n$ different objects one of $r$ labels. Each object
has $r$ choices, so there are $r^n$ ways. Now count the same thing by group sizes:
first decide how many objects get each label, $(n_1, \dots, n_r)$, and then which
objects get which label, which can be done in $\frac{n!}{n_1! \cdots n_r!}$ ways. Every
labeling has exactly one list of group sizes, so adding over all lists counts every
labeling once. The two counts must be equal. (The algebra proof is the multinomial
theorem with all $x_i = 1$.)

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** A fair die is rolled 12 times. Find the probability that every face
appears exactly twice, and check it by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Favorable sequences: $\frac{12!}{(2!)^6} = 7484400$. All sequences: $6^{12}$.

$$P = \frac{12!}{2^6 \cdot 6^{12}} = \frac{7484400}{2176782336} \approx 0.00344$$

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 2_000_000
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(n_trials, 12))
counts = np.stack([(rolls == f).sum(axis=1) for f in range(1, 7)], axis=1)
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(np.all(counts == 2, axis=1)):.5f}")
print(f"Exact:      {7484400 / 6**12:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.00341
Exact:      0.00344
```

</details>

## Summary

- The multinomial coefficient splits $n$ different objects into labeled groups of
  given sizes.
- It also counts arrangements of a word with repeated letters.
- Two proofs: choose the groups one after another (a product of binomials), or
  arrange everything and divide out the orders inside each group.
- For groups with no labels, also divide by the orders of the equal-size groups.
- The multinomial theorem gives the coefficients of $(x_1 + \dots + x_r)^n$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\binom{n}{n_1, \dots, n_r} = \frac{n!}{n_1! \thinspace n_2! \cdots n_r!}}
$$
