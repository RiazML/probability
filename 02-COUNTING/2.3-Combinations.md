# 2.3 Combinations

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.3 Combinations

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [2.2 Permutations](2.2-Permutations.md)

> **Big idea:** The number of ways to choose $k$ of $n$ different things when order does not matter is $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$: count the ordered lists, then divide by the $k!$ orders of each group.

---

## Why It Matters

A lottery ticket, a poker hand, a committee and a random sample of users are all
**groups**: what matters is who is in, not in which order they were picked. The
count $\binom{n}{k}$ is the most used number in all of discrete probability. It is
the heart of the binomial distribution
([7.2 Binomial](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md)), the hypergeometric
distribution ([7.6 Hypergeometric](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.6-Hypergeometric.md))
and the binomial theorem ([2.7](2.7-Binomial-Theorem-and-Pascals-Triangle.md)).

## Intuition

A pizza shop has 4 toppings: A (anchovy), B (beef), C (corn) and D (dates). You may
pick 2. How many different pizzas?

If order mattered, the multiplication rule would give $4 \cdot 3 = 12$ ordered pairs:
AB, AC, AD, BA, BC, BD, CA, CB, CD, DA, DB, DC. But a pizza with A then B is the same
pizza as B then A. Every pizza appears exactly **twice** in this list, once for each
order of its 2 toppings. So there are $12 / 2 = 6$ pizzas: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD.

With 3 toppings out of 10, the ordered count is $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$. Each group of 3
appears once for each of its $3! = 6$ orders. So there are $720 / 6 = 120$ different
pizzas. That is the whole idea: **count with order, then divide by the number of
orders of each group.**

## Picture

```text
   Ordered pairs from {A, B, C, D}          Unordered groups

      AB   BA   ------------------------->   {A, B}
      AC   CA   ------------------------->   {A, C}
      AD   DA   ------------------------->   {A, D}
      BC   CB   ------------------------->   {B, C}
      BD   DB   ------------------------->   {B, D}
      CD   DC   ------------------------->   {C, D}

   12 ordered pairs  =  6 groups  x  2! orders per group
```

Each unordered group collects exactly $k! = 2$ ordered lists, so dividing the ordered
count by $k!$ gives the number of groups.

## Definition

A **combination** of $k$ objects from $n$ different objects is a subset of size $k$:
a group where order does not matter and nothing is repeated. The number of them is
the **binomial coefficient**, written $\binom{n}{k}$ and read "n choose k":

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k! \thinspace (n-k)!} = \frac{n (n-1) \cdots (n-k+1)}{k!}
$$

where $n$ is the number of objects to choose from and $k$ is the group size, with
$0 \le k \le n$. If $k \gt n$ or $k \lt 0$ there is no such group, and we set
$\binom{n}{k} = 0$.

**In words:** n choose k equals the number of ordered lists of $k$ things, divided by
the $k!$ ways to order each list.

Two facts follow at once:

$$
\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1, \qquad \binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}
$$

**In words:** there is one way to choose nothing and one way to choose everything,
and choosing the $k$ who are **in** is the same as choosing the $n - k$ who are **out**.

## Why It Is True

Count the ordered lists of $k$ different objects from $n$ in two ways.

1. **Directly** ([2.2 Permutations](2.2-Permutations.md)): there are
   $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ ordered lists.
2. **In two steps:** first choose the group (there are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways, the number
   we want), and then put the group in order ($k!$ ways). Every ordered list comes
   from exactly one group and one order.

Both methods count the same lists, so the answers are equal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\binom{n}{k} \cdot k! &= \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} && \text{(two ways to count ordered lists)} \\
\binom{n}{k} &= \frac{n!}{k! \thinspace (n-k)!} && \text{(divide both sides by } k! \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The symmetry $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$ follows by swapping $k$ and $n - k$ in the
formula: the bottom $k!(n-k)!$ does not change. This method, counting one set in two
ways, is the main tool of [2.10 Story Proofs](2.10-Story-Proofs.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Lottery

**Problem.** A lottery draws 6 numbers from 1 to 49. Your ticket has 6 numbers. What
is the probability that you match all 6? What is the probability that you match
exactly 3?

**Solution.**

1. The draw is a group of 6 from 49, and every group is equally likely:

$$
\binom{49}{6} = \frac{49 \cdot 48 \cdot 47 \cdot 46 \cdot 45 \cdot 44}{6!} = \frac{10068347520}{720} = 13983816
$$

2. Only one group matches your ticket, so $P(\text{all 6}) = \frac{1}{13983816} \approx 7.2 \times 10^{-8}$.
3. For exactly 3 matches, build the draw in two steps. Choose 3 of **your** 6 numbers:
   $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ ways. Then choose the other 3 drawn numbers from the
   $49 - 6 = 43$ numbers **not** on your ticket: $\binom{43}{3} = 12341$ ways.
4. By the multiplication rule, $20 \cdot 12341 = 246820$ draws give exactly 3 matches:

$$
P(\text{exactly 3}) = \frac{\binom{6}{3} \binom{43}{3}}{\binom{49}{6}} = \frac{246820}{13983816} \approx 0.0177
$$

**Answer.** About 1 in 14 million for the jackpot, and about $0.0177$ (1 in 57) for
exactly 3 matches.

**Check.** The two steps choose from separate pools (your numbers and the others), so
nothing is counted twice. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: A Random Committee

**Problem.** A class has 7 women and 5 men. A committee of 4 is chosen at random.
What is the probability that it has exactly 2 women?

**Solution.**

1. All committees: $\binom{12}{4} = \frac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9}{24} = \frac{11880}{24} = 495$.
2. Choose 2 of the 7 women: $\binom{7}{2} = \frac{7 \cdot 6}{2} = 21$. Choose 2 of the 5
   men: $\binom{5}{2} = \frac{5 \cdot 4}{2} = 10$. Multiply: $21 \cdot 10 = 210$.

$$
P(\text{2 women}) = \frac{210}{495} = \frac{14}{33} \approx 0.424
$$

**Answer.** $\frac{14}{33} \approx 0.424$.

**Check.** Sampling a group from two kinds of objects like this is the
**hypergeometric** setting; see
[7.6 Hypergeometric](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.6-Hypergeometric.md).

### Example 3: Bit Strings With Three Ones

**Problem.** A random 10-bit string is chosen (all $2^{10} = 1024$ strings equally
likely). What is the probability that it contains exactly three 1s?

**Solution.**

1. A string with exactly three 1s is fixed by **which positions** hold the 1s. That is
   a group of 3 positions out of 10, and order does not matter:

$$
\binom{10}{3} = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = \frac{720}{6} = 120
$$

2. Divide by all strings:

$$
P(\text{exactly three 1s}) = \frac{120}{1024} \approx 0.117
$$

**Answer.** About $0.117$.

**Check.** By symmetry, exactly seven 1s has the same probability, because
$\binom{10}{7} = \binom{10}{3}$: choosing where the three 1s go is the same as choosing where the
seven 0s go.

## Simulate It

The code runs many lottery draws to check Example 1, and many random committees to
check Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Example 1: your ticket is 1..6. Draw 6 of 49 without replacement.
draws = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(1, 50), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)[:, :6]
matches = np.sum(draws <= 6, axis=1)
exact3 = comb(6, 3) * comb(43, 3) / comb(49, 6)
print(f"P(exactly 3 match): simulation {np.mean(matches == 3):.4f}, exact {exact3:.4f}")

# Example 2: people 0-6 are women, 7-11 are men. Pick 4 at random.
committee = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(12), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)[:, :4]
women = np.sum(committee < 7, axis=1)
print(f"P(2 women):         simulation {np.mean(women == 2):.4f}, exact {14 / 33:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(exactly 3 match): simulation 0.0176, exact 0.0177
P(2 women):         simulation 0.4236, exact 0.4242
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using combinations when order matters.** President, vice president and secretary
  are three different jobs, so use $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8$, not $\binom{10}{3}$. Ask: "if I
  swap two chosen items, do I get a different outcome?" If yes, order matters.
- **Counting "at least one" by picking one first.** For a committee of 4 from 7 women
  and 5 men with at least one woman, "pick 1 woman, then any 3 others" gives
  $7 \cdot \binom{11}{3} = 1155$, which is more than all 495 committees! Each committee
  with several women is counted several times. Use the complement:
  $\binom{12}{4} - \binom{5}{4} = 495 - 5 = 490$.
- **Adding instead of multiplying across groups.** "2 women and 2 men" is
  $\binom{7}{2} \cdot \binom{5}{2}$, because you choose the women **and then** the men.
- **Mixing counting methods in one fraction.** If the top counts unordered groups,
  the bottom must count unordered groups too. Ordered over ordered also works; mixed
  does not.

## Where It Is Used

- **Probability:** $\binom{n}{k}$ counts the ways to get $k$ successes in $n$ trials, which
  gives the binomial distribution in [Module 07](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/README.md).
- **AI and machine learning:** choosing $k$ of $n$ features gives $\binom{n}{k}$ models to
  compare, which is why trying every feature subset is impossible for large $n$.
  Random mini-batches and random train/test splits are random combinations.
- **Computer science:** the number of $n$-bit strings with exactly $k$ ones, the number
  of edges in a complete graph ($\binom{n}{2}$), and the number of pairs to compare
  when looking for duplicates.
- **Medicine and science:** choosing which patients get a treatment in a random
  trial; counting possible test panels.
- **Games:** lotteries, card hands and team selection.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A pizza shop has 10 toppings. How many pizzas with exactly 3 different
toppings can you order?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\binom{10}{3} = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{3!} = \frac{720}{6} = 120$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** At a meeting, each of 20 people shakes hands once with every other
person. How many handshakes are there?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A handshake is an unordered pair of people:

$$\binom{20}{2} = \frac{20 \cdot 19}{2} = 190$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A committee of 5 is chosen from 8 women and 6 men. How many
committees contain at least one man?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the complement. All committees: $\binom{14}{5} = 2002$. Committees with no man (all
women): $\binom{8}{5} = 56$. So:

$$\binom{14}{5} - \binom{8}{5} = 2002 - 56 = 1946$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** You are dealt 5 cards from a shuffled 52-card deck. What is the
probability that you get exactly 2 aces?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Choose 2 of the 4 aces and 3 of the 48 other cards:

$$P(\text{exactly 2 aces}) = \frac{\binom{4}{2} \binom{48}{3}}{\binom{52}{5}} = \frac{6 \cdot 17296}{2598960} = \frac{103776}{2598960} \approx 0.0399$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** On a grid of streets, you walk from the corner $(0, 0)$ to the corner
$(4, 3)$, taking only steps Right (R) or Up (U). How many shortest paths are there?
Explain why the answer is a binomial coefficient.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every shortest path uses exactly 4 R steps and 3 U steps, so it is a word of length 7
with 4 R and 3 U, such as RRURUUR. The word is fixed by choosing **which 3 of the 7
positions** are U. Different choices give different paths, and every path gives one
choice:

$$\binom{7}{3} = \frac{7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5}{6} = 35$$

In general, there are $\binom{m+n}{n}$ shortest paths from $(0, 0)$ to $(m, n)$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 by dealing many random 5-card hands.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 500_000
# Cards 0-3 are the aces. Deal 5 of 52 without replacement.
hands = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(52), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)[:, :5]
aces = np.sum(hands < 4, axis=1)
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(aces == 2):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {103776 / 2598960:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.0401
Exact:      0.0399
```

</details>

## Summary

- A combination is a group where order does not matter and nothing repeats.
- $\binom{n}{k}$ = (ordered lists of $k$) divided by $k!$.
- Symmetry: choosing who is in is the same as choosing who is out, so
  $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$.
- For groups from several pools (women and men, aces and non-aces), choose from each
  pool and multiply.
- For "at least one", use the complement instead of "pick one first".

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k! \thinspace (n-k)!}}
$$
