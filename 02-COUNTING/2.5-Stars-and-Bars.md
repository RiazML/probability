# 2.5 Stars and Bars

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.5 Stars and Bars

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [2.4 Sampling With Replacement](2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md)

> **Big idea:** Putting $n$ identical items into $r$ labeled boxes is the same as arranging $n$ stars and $r - 1$ bars in a row, so there are $\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}$ ways.

---

## Why It Matters

Many counting questions ask how to **split a total**: 10 identical cookies among 3
children, 100 votes among 4 candidates, a sum of 10 from three dice, a degree-3
polynomial in 10 variables. All of them become the same question: how many whole
number solutions does $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_r = n$ have? **Stars and bars** answers it
with one picture. It also gives a second proof of the multiset formula from
[2.4 Sampling With Replacement](2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md).

## Intuition

You have 6 identical sweets for 3 children: Ali, Bina and Chen. One way is Ali 2,
Bina 1, Chen 3. Write the sweets as stars and put a bar between children:

```text
   * * | * | * * *
```

Read it from left to right: 2 stars before the first bar (Ali), 1 star between the
bars (Bina), 3 stars after the second bar (Chen). Every way to share the sweets gives
a row of 6 stars and 2 bars, and every such row gives a way to share. For example,
`| * * * * * * |` means Ali 0, Bina 6, Chen 0.

So we only need to count rows. A row has $6 + 2 = 8$ places, and we choose which 2
of them hold the bars: $\binom{8}{2} = 28$ ways. That is the whole trick.

## Picture

```text
   n = 6 stars (items), r = 3 boxes, r - 1 = 2 bars (walls)

   position:   1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8
   symbol:     *   *   |   *   |   *   *   *
              \_____/     \_/     \_________/
              box 1 = 2   box 2   box 3 = 3
                          = 1

   choose the 2 bar positions out of 8:  C(8, 2) = 28 ways
```

A distribution of $n$ identical items into $r$ boxes is the same thing as a choice of
$r - 1$ bar positions among $n + r - 1$ places.

## Definition

The **stars and bars** theorem counts the ways to write $n$ as an ordered sum of $r$
whole numbers.

**Non-negative version** (boxes may be empty): the number of solutions of
$x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_r = n$ with every $x_i \ge 0$ is

$$
\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}
$$

**Positive version** (every box gets at least one): the number of solutions with
every $x_i \ge 1$ is

$$
\binom{n-1}{r-1}
$$

where $n$ is the total (the number of identical items) and $r$ is the number of
labeled boxes (the number of variables). The boxes are **labeled**: Ali 2, Bina 1 is
different from Ali 1, Bina 2. The items are **identical**: only how many go in each
box matters.

**In words:** to split $n$ identical items among $r$ boxes, choose where the $r - 1$
walls go in a row of $n + r - 1$ places. If no box may be empty, choose $r - 1$ of the
$n - 1$ gaps between the items.

## Why It Is True

**Non-negative version.** Match each solution $(x_1, \dots, x_r)$ with the row
$x_1$ stars, a bar, $x_2$ stars, a bar, ..., a bar, $x_r$ stars.

1. The row has $n$ stars (because $x_1 + \dots + x_r = n$) and $r - 1$ bars, so
   $n + r - 1$ symbols.
2. Different solutions give different rows, and every row of $n$ stars and $r - 1$ bars
   comes from exactly one solution (count the stars between the bars). So this is a
   one-to-one matching (a **bijection**).
3. A row is fixed by choosing which $r - 1$ of the $n + r - 1$ places hold bars:
   $\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}$ rows.

**Positive version.** Line up the $n$ stars. There are $n - 1$ gaps between
neighboring stars. If every box gets at least one star, no two bars can be in the
same gap and no bar can be at either end. So a solution is a choice of $r - 1$
different gaps out of $n - 1$:

$$
\binom{n-1}{r-1}
$$

A second proof uses a **substitution**: put $y_i = x_i - 1 \ge 0$. Then
$y_1 + \dots + y_r = n - r$, and the non-negative version gives
$\binom{(n-r)+r-1}{r-1} = \binom{n-1}{r-1}$. Same answer.

**Link to multisets.** A multiset of size $k$ from $n$ kinds is fixed by how many of
each kind it holds: $x_1 + \dots + x_n = k$ with $x_i \ge 0$. Stars and bars gives
$\binom{k+n-1}{n-1} = \binom{n+k-1}{k}$, the formula of
[2.4](2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Sharing Cookies

**Problem.** In how many ways can 10 identical cookies be shared among 3 children?
In how many ways if every child must get at least one?

**Solution.**

1. Non-negative solutions of $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 10$, with $n = 10$ and $r = 3$:

$$
\binom{10+3-1}{3-1} = \binom{12}{2} = \frac{12 \cdot 11}{2} = 66
$$

2. Positive solutions (every child at least one):

$$
\binom{10-1}{3-1} = \binom{9}{2} = \frac{9 \cdot 8}{2} = 36
$$

**Answer.** 66 ways, or 36 ways if nobody is left out.

**Check.** Give each child 1 cookie first. Then 7 cookies are left to share freely:
$\binom{7+2}{2} = \binom{9}{2} = 36$. Same answer.

### Example 2: Three Dice Adding to 10

**Problem.** You roll three fair dice. What is the probability that the sum is 10?

**Solution.**

1. The equally likely outcomes are the $6^3 = 216$ ordered triples $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ with
   each $x_i$ from 1 to 6. We need the number with $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 10$. Each such
   triple is one solution, so stars and bars counts exactly the outcomes we need.
2. Remove the lower bound with $y_i = x_i - 1$. Then $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 = 7$ with
   $0 \le y_i \le 5$.
3. Without the upper bound: $\binom{7+2}{2} = \binom{9}{2} = 36$ solutions.
4. Remove the bad ones, where some $y_i \ge 6$. Two variables cannot both be $\ge 6$,
   because $6 + 6 = 12 \gt 7$. So the bad cases for $y_1$, $y_2$ and $y_3$ do not overlap,
   and we can add them. If $y_1 \ge 6$, put $z = y_1 - 6 \ge 0$: then $z + y_2 + y_3 = 1$,
   which has $\binom{1+2}{2} = 3$ solutions. The same holds for $y_2$ and $y_3$:
   $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ bad solutions.
5. Good solutions: $36 - 9 = 27$.

$$
P(\text{sum} = 10) = \frac{27}{216} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{sum} = 10) = 0.125$.

**Check.** The simulation below lists all 216 outcomes and finds 27. When the bad
cases **can** overlap, you need [2.8 Inclusion-Exclusion](2.8-Inclusion-Exclusion.md).

### Example 3: Counting Polynomial Features

**Problem.** A machine learning model uses **polynomial features**: every product of
the input variables up to degree 3, such as $u_1^2 u_4$ or $u_2 u_5 u_9$, plus the
constant 1. With 10 input variables, how many features are there?

**Solution.**

1. A feature (a **monomial**) is fixed by its exponents $e_1, \dots, e_{10} \ge 0$ with
   $e_1 + \dots + e_{10} \le 3$.
2. Turn "at most 3" into "exactly 3" with a **slack** variable $e_{11} = 3 - (e_1 + \dots + e_{10}) \ge 0$:

$$
e_1 + e_2 + \dots + e_{10} + e_{11} = 3, \qquad e_i \ge 0
$$

3. Stars and bars with $n = 3$ and $r = 11$:

$$
\binom{3+11-1}{11-1} = \binom{13}{10} = \binom{13}{3} = \frac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11}{6} = 286
$$

**Answer.** 286 features.

**Check.** By degree: 1 constant, 10 linear terms, $\binom{11}{2} = 55$ of degree 2 and
$\binom{12}{3} = 220$ of degree 3. Total $1 + 10 + 55 + 220 = 286$.

## Simulate It

The code lists all three-dice outcomes and all solutions of the cookie problem, and
also estimates $P(\text{sum} = 10)$ by rolling.

```python
import numpy as np
from itertools import product
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Example 2: count ordered triples with sum 10, then simulate.
count = sum(1 for t in product(range(1, 7), repeat=3) if sum(t) == 10)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(1_000_000, 3))
print(f"Triples with sum 10: {count} (formula {comb(9, 2) - 3 * comb(3, 2)})")
print(f"P(sum = 10): simulation {np.mean(rolls.sum(axis=1) == 10):.4f}, "
      f"exact {27 / 216:.4f}")

# Example 1: solutions of x1 + x2 + x3 = 10.
sols = [t for t in product(range(11), repeat=3) if sum(t) == 10]
positive = [t for t in sols if min(t) >= 1]
print(f"Non-negative: {len(sols)} (formula {comb(12, 2)})")
print(f"Positive:     {len(positive)} (formula {comb(9, 2)})")
```

Output:

```text
Triples with sum 10: 27 (formula 27)
P(sum = 10): simulation 0.1250, exact 0.1250
Non-negative: 66 (formula 66)
Positive:     36 (formula 36)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using stars and bars for different items.** If the cookies are all different
  (distinguishable), each cookie picks a child: $3^{10}$ ways, not 66. Stars and bars is
  for **identical** items in **labeled** boxes.
- **Treating the solutions as equally likely by default.** If 10 people each pick one
  of 3 rooms at random, the split (10, 0, 0) is far less likely than (4, 3, 3). Stars
  and bars counts splits; it does not say they are equally likely. (In Example 2 it
  works, because each solution is exactly one equally likely ordered roll.)
- **Mixing up the two versions.** Boxes may be empty: $\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}$. No box empty:
  $\binom{n-1}{r-1}$. Check with a tiny case, such as $n = 2$ and $r = 2$: (0,2), (1,1),
  (2,0) gives 3, and $\binom{3}{1} = 3$.
- **Using $r$ bars instead of $r - 1$.** Three boxes need only two walls between them.
- **Ignoring upper limits.** A die face is at most 6. Subtract the cases that break the
  limit, as in Example 2.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** counting polynomial features and the number of
  possible count vectors (word counts in a bag-of-words model with a fixed length).
- **Probability:** the possible outcomes of a multinomial experiment (how many of each
  category) are the stars-and-bars solutions; see
  [7.9 Multinomial](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.9-Multinomial.md).
- **Computer science:** the number of ways to split $n$ identical jobs or units of
  memory among $r$ machines, and the number of multisets in a data structure.
- **Physics:** Bose-Einstein statistics count identical particles in energy levels
  this way.
- **Everyday life:** sharing identical items, and the number of ways dice can add to
  a given total.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In how many ways can 8 identical balls be put into 4 labeled boxes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\binom{8+4-1}{4-1} = \binom{11}{3} = \frac{11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9}{6} = 165$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** How many solutions does $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 8$ have in **positive**
integers?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\binom{8-1}{4-1} = \binom{7}{3} = 35$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** How many solutions does $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 12$ have with
$x_1 \ge 2$, $x_2 \ge 3$ and $x_3 \ge 0$ (all whole numbers)?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Give $x_1$ its 2 and $x_2$ its 3 first: put $y_1 = x_1 - 2$ and $y_2 = x_2 - 3$. Then
$y_1 + y_2 + x_3 = 7$ with all three $\ge 0$:

$$\binom{7+2}{2} = \binom{9}{2} = 36$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Four fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum is 10?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Put $y_i = x_i - 1$, so $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 + y_4 = 6$ with $0 \le y_i \le 5$. Without the upper
bound: $\binom{9}{3} = 84$. A bad solution has some $y_i \ge 6$, which forces that
$y_i = 6$ and the others 0; there are 4 of these, and no two overlap. So
$84 - 4 = 80$ good outcomes out of $6^4 = 1296$:

$$P(\text{sum} = 10) = \frac{80}{1296} = \frac{5}{81} \approx 0.0617$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that the number of monomials of degree **at most** $d$ in $r$
variables is $\binom{d+r}{r}$. Check it for $r = 2$ and $d = 2$ by listing.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A monomial of degree at most $d$ is a list of exponents $e_1, \dots, e_r \ge 0$ with sum
at most $d$. Add a slack variable $e_{r+1} = d - (e_1 + \dots + e_r) \ge 0$. This is a
bijection with the non-negative solutions of $e_1 + \dots + e_{r+1} = d$, which number

$$\binom{d+(r+1)-1}{(r+1)-1} = \binom{d+r}{r}$$

For $r = 2$, $d = 2$: the monomials are $1, u, v, u^2, uv, v^2$, which is 6, and
$\binom{4}{2} = 6$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 by rolling four dice many times.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(1_000_000, 4))
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(rolls.sum(axis=1) == 10):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {80 / 1296:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.0618
Exact:      0.0617
```

</details>

## Summary

- Splitting $n$ identical items among $r$ labeled boxes is a row of $n$ stars and
  $r - 1$ bars.
- Boxes may be empty: $\binom{n+r-1}{r-1}$. Every box non-empty: $\binom{n-1}{r-1}$.
- Lower bounds: give each box its minimum first, then share the rest.
- Upper bounds: subtract the solutions that break a bound (use inclusion-exclusion
  if the bad cases overlap).
- Stars and bars counts splits; check separately whether the splits are equally
  likely before dividing.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{solutions of } x_1 + \dots + x_r = n \text{ with all } x_i \ge 0 = \binom{n+r-1}{r-1}}
$$
