# 2.1 Multiplication and Addition Rules

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](README.md) / 2.1 Multiplication and Addition Rules

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 01: Sets, Sample Spaces and Events](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/README.md)

> **Big idea:** To count choices made one after another, multiply; to count choices that are one OR the other (with no overlap), add.

---

## Why It Matters

When all outcomes are equally likely, a probability is a count divided by a count
(see [1.1 What is Probability](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.1-What-is-Probability.md)).
Real sample spaces are far too big to list: there are hundreds of trillions of
8-character passwords. The multiplication rule and the addition rule let you count
them without listing. Every other counting tool in this module (permutations,
combinations, the binomial theorem) is built from these two rules.

## Intuition

A small cafe sells 3 starters, 4 main dishes and 2 desserts. A full meal is one
starter, then one main, then one dessert. For each of the 3 starters you can pick
any of the 4 mains, so there are 3 groups of 4, that is 12 starter-main pairs. For
each of those 12 pairs you can pick any of the 2 desserts, so there are
12 groups of 2, that is 24 meals. Choices made **one after another** multiply.

Now you only want a drink. The cafe has 5 kinds of tea and 3 kinds of coffee, and no
drink is both. You pick a tea **or** a coffee, so there are 5 + 3 = 8 drinks.
Choices from **separate groups, one or the other**, add.

The two phrases to listen for are "and then" (multiply) and "or" (add, but only when
the groups do not overlap).

## Picture

```text
   Shirt          Trousers          Outfit
                 +-- black  -->  (white, black)
   white  -------+-- blue   -->  (white, blue)
                 +-- grey   -->  (white, grey)

                 +-- black  -->  (red, black)
   red    -------+-- blue   -->  (red, blue)
                 +-- grey   -->  (red, grey)

   2 shirts  x  3 trousers  =  6 outfits   (count the leaves of the tree)
```

A **tree diagram** shows every sequence of choices. Each leaf is one outcome, and each
shirt has the same number of branches (3), so there are 2 x 3 leaves.

## Definition

**Multiplication rule.** Suppose a process happens in $k$ steps. Step 1 can be done
in $n_1$ ways. Then, **no matter what happened in step 1**, step 2 can be done in
$n_2$ ways, and so on up to step $k$. Then the whole process can be done in

$$
n_1 \cdot n_2 \cdots n_k
$$

ways. In set language: if $A$ and $B$ are finite sets, the **Cartesian product**
$A \times B$ is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$ and $b \in B$, and

$$
\lvert A \times B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert \cdot \lvert B \rvert
$$

where $\lvert A \rvert$ means the **size** of $A$: the number of elements in it.

**In words:** the number of ways to do one thing and then another is the product of
the numbers of ways to do each.

**Addition rule.** If $A$ and $B$ are finite sets with no common element
($A \cap B = \varnothing$, called **disjoint**), then

$$
\lvert A \cup B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert
$$

More generally, if $A_1, \dots, A_k$ are **pairwise disjoint** (no two of them share an
element), the size of their union is $\lvert A_1 \rvert + \dots + \lvert A_k \rvert$.

**In words:** if every outcome is in exactly one group, count each group and add.

> **Note:** The multiplication rule needs the **number** of choices at each step to
> stay the same, not the choices themselves. For a 3-letter code with no repeated
> letter, the letters left for step 2 depend on step 1, but there are always 25 of
> them. So the count is $26 \cdot 25 \cdot 24 = 15600$.

## Why It Is True

**Multiplication rule, two steps.** Write the outcomes in a table. Each row is one
choice for step 1, so there are $n_1$ rows. Each row holds the $n_2$ ways to finish
with step 2. Different rows hold different outcomes, because they start
differently. So the table holds

$$
\underbrace{n_2 + n_2 + \dots + n_2}_{n_1 \text{ rows}} = n_1 \cdot n_2
$$

outcomes. (This uses the addition rule: the rows are disjoint groups.)

**Many steps.** Treat steps 1 and 2 together as one big step with $n_1 n_2$ ways,
then add step 3, and keep going:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ways for steps 1 to 3} &= (n_1 n_2) \cdot n_3 && \text{(two-step rule: "steps 1-2", then step 3)} \\
\text{ways for steps 1 to } k &= (n_1 n_2 \cdots n_{k-1}) \cdot n_k && \text{(repeat the same idea)} \\
&= n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k
\end{aligned}
$$

**Addition rule.** When we count $A$ and then count $B$, an element of $A \cup B$ is
counted once if it is in exactly one of them, and twice if it is in both. Disjoint
means no element is in both, so every element is counted exactly once. When the
sets **do** overlap, the sum counts the overlap twice; the fix is
[2.8 Inclusion-Exclusion](2.8-Inclusion-Exclusion.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Dice Showing Different Numbers

**Problem.** You roll a red die and a blue die. What is the probability that they show
different numbers? What is the probability that at least one die shows a 6?

**Solution.**

1. An outcome is a pair (red, blue). Red has 6 values and then blue has 6 values,
   so $\lvert \Omega \rvert = 6 \cdot 6 = 36$ equally likely pairs.
2. "Different numbers": red has 6 choices, and then blue must avoid the red value,
   so it has 5 choices. That gives $6 \cdot 5 = 30$ pairs.

$$
P(\text{different}) = \frac{30}{36} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833
$$

3. "At least one 6" is easier through its **complement**, "no 6 at all". With no 6,
   red has 5 choices and blue has 5 choices: $5 \cdot 5 = 25$ pairs. Every pair is
   either "no 6" or "at least one 6", never both, so by the addition rule
   $36 = 25 + (\text{pairs with at least one 6})$. That leaves $36 - 25 = 11$ pairs.

$$
P(\text{at least one 6}) = \frac{11}{36} \approx 0.306
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{different}) = \frac{5}{6}$ and $P(\text{at least one 6}) = \frac{11}{36}$.

**Check.** Count "at least one 6" directly: red is 6 (6 pairs) or blue is 6
(6 pairs), but $(6, 6)$ is in both groups, so $6 + 6 - 1 = 11$. Same answer.

### Example 2: How Long to Crack a Password

**Problem.** A password has exactly 8 characters. Each character is a lowercase
letter, an uppercase letter or a digit. An attacker can test 10 billion
($10^{10}$) passwords per second. How long does it take to try them all? What is
the probability that one random guess is right?

**Solution.**

1. Characters per position, by the addition rule: $26 + 26 + 10 = 62$
   (the three groups do not overlap).
2. Eight positions, filled one after another, by the multiplication rule:

$$
62^8 = 218{,}340{,}105{,}584{,}896 \approx 2.18 \times 10^{14}
$$

3. Time to try them all: $2.18 \times 10^{14} / 10^{10} \approx 21834$ seconds.
   Divide by 3600 seconds per hour: about $6.07$ hours.
4. A random guess is one of $62^8$ equally likely passwords:

$$
P(\text{correct}) = \frac{1}{62^8} \approx 4.6 \times 10^{-15}
$$

**Answer.** About 6 hours to try every password; a single guess almost never works.

**Check.** If the password may have 6, 7 **or** 8 characters, the addition rule gives
$62^6 + 62^7 + 62^8$. The 8-character term is 62 times bigger than the 7-character
term, so it dominates. Each extra character multiplies the work by 62, which is why
length matters so much for password strength.

### Example 3: Choosing the Order of Steps

**Problem.** How many 3-digit numbers (100 to 999) are odd and have three different
digits?

**Solution.**

1. Pick the **last** digit first, because it has the strongest rule: it must be odd,
   so it is one of $1, 3, 5, 7, 9$. That is 5 choices.
2. Then the first digit: it cannot be 0 and cannot equal the last digit. The last
   digit is never 0, so this removes 2 of the 10 digits: 8 choices.
3. Then the middle digit: it must differ from the two digits already used:
   $10 - 2 = 8$ choices.
4. Multiply:

$$
5 \cdot 8 \cdot 8 = 320
$$

**Answer.** 320 numbers.

**Check.** If you start with the first digit (9 choices), the number of choices for
the last digit depends on whether the first digit was odd or even. The rule then
fails, unless you split into two cases and add. Start with the most restricted
step. The code below confirms 320 by brute force.

## Simulate It

The code rolls two dice many times to check Example 1, and tests all 900 three-digit
numbers to check Example 3.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Example 1: two fair dice.
red = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
blue = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
print(f"P(different):      simulation {np.mean(red != blue):.4f}, exact {30 / 36:.4f}")
print(f"P(at least one 6): simulation {np.mean((red == 6) | (blue == 6)):.4f}, "
      f"exact {11 / 36:.4f}")

# Example 3: count odd 3-digit numbers with three different digits.
count = sum(1 for x in range(100, 1000)
            if x % 2 == 1 and len(set(str(x))) == 3)
print(f"Odd 3-digit numbers with distinct digits: {count} (rule gives {5 * 8 * 8})")
```

Output:

```text
P(different):      simulation 0.8331, exact 0.8333
P(at least one 6): simulation 0.3050, exact 0.3056
Odd 3-digit numbers with distinct digits: 320 (rule gives 320)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Multiplying when the number of choices changes.** In Example 3, starting with the
  first digit makes the count for the last digit depend on earlier choices. Reorder
  the steps, or split into cases and add.
- **Adding groups that overlap.** "Red die is 6 or blue die is 6" is not 6 + 6 = 12,
  because $(6, 6)$ is counted twice. Check that the groups are disjoint, or use
  [2.8 Inclusion-Exclusion](2.8-Inclusion-Exclusion.md).
- **Multiplying when order does not matter.** Picking 2 people from 10 gives
  $10 \cdot 9 = 90$ ordered pairs, but only 45 unordered pairs, because (Ana, Ben) and
  (Ben, Ana) are the same pair. See [2.3 Combinations](2.3-Combinations.md).
- **Mixing up "and then" with "or".** A meal is a starter **and then** a main
  (multiply). A drink is a tea **or** a coffee (add). Say the sentence out loud.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** an $n$-bit string has $2^n$ values, so an IPv4 address has
  $2^{32} \approx 4.3$ billion values. The size of a search space tells you whether
  brute force is possible.
- **Security:** password and PIN strength is the size of the space, computed with the
  multiplication rule. See [21.4 Cryptography and Security](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md).
- **AI and machine learning:** a grid search over 5 learning rates, 4 batch sizes and
  3 model sizes runs $5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 60$ experiments. A vocabulary of 50,000 tokens
  gives $50000^{10}$ possible 10-token texts.
- **Probability:** the sample space of repeated experiments (dice, coins, cards,
  network packets) is a Cartesian product, so its size is a product.
- **Everyday life:** outfits, menus, travel routes and phone numbers.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You own 4 shirts, 3 pairs of trousers and 2 pairs of shoes. How many
outfits (one of each) can you make?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Shirt, then trousers, then shoes:

$$4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 24$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A 4-digit PIN (0000 to 9999) is chosen at random. What is the
probability that its 4 digits are all different?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are $10^4 = 10000$ PINs. With all digits different there are 10, then 9, then
8, then 7 choices:

$$P(\text{all different}) = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{10^4} = \frac{5040}{10000} = 0.504$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Car plates have either 2 letters followed by 4 digits, or 3 letters
followed by 3 digits. How many plates are possible?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The two formats never produce the same plate (one has 2 letters, the other 3), so
add the two counts:

$$26^2 \cdot 10^4 + 26^3 \cdot 10^3 = 6760000 + 17576000 = 24336000$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A menu lets you pick one of 5 pizzas **or** one of 4 burgers, and
then one of 3 drinks. How many orders are possible? Name each rule you use.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Addition rule for the food (pizzas and burgers are separate groups): $5 + 4 = 9$
choices. Multiplication rule for food and then drink:

$$(5 + 4) \cdot 3 = 27$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that a set with $n$ elements has exactly $2^n$ subsets.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write the elements as $x_1, \dots, x_n$. Build a subset in $n$ steps: in step $i$,
decide whether $x_i$ is in or out. Each step has 2 choices, whatever happened
before. Different sequences of decisions give different subsets, and every subset
comes from exactly one sequence. So by the multiplication rule the number of
subsets is

$$\underbrace{2 \cdot 2 \cdots 2}_{n \text{ times}} = 2^n$$

For example, $\lbrace a, b \rbrace$ has the $2^2 = 4$ subsets $\varnothing$, $\lbrace a \rbrace$,
$\lbrace b \rbrace$ and $\lbrace a, b \rbrace$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 2 by simulation: draw many random PINs and estimate
the probability that all four digits are different.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000
pins = rng.integers(0, 10, size=(n_trials, 4))   # 4 random digits per row
# After sorting a row, its digits are all different when no neighbors are equal.
s = np.sort(pins, axis=1)
all_diff = np.all(s[:, 1:] != s[:, :-1], axis=1)
print(f"Simulation: {all_diff.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {5040 / 10000:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.5036
Exact:      0.5040
```

</details>

## Summary

- **Multiplication rule:** steps done one after another multiply, as long as each step
  has the same number of choices whatever came before.
- **Addition rule:** disjoint groups ("one or the other, never both") add.
- Start with the most restricted step, and use the complement when "at least one" is
  hard to count directly.
- With equally likely outcomes, these counts give probabilities:
  favorable count divided by total count.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\lvert A \times B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert \cdot \lvert B \rvert \qquad \lvert A \cup B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert \ \text{ if } A \cap B = \varnothing}
$$
