# 2.11.3 Poker Hands

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](../README.md) / [2.11 Classic Counting Problems](README.md) / 2.11.3 Poker Hands

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [2.11.2 Derangements and Matching](2.11.2-Derangements-and-Matching.md)

> **Big idea:** Every poker probability is (number of hands of that type) divided by $\binom{52}{5} = 2598960$, and each count comes from choosing the ranks first and then the suits, step by step.

---

## Why It Matters

Poker hands are the classic training ground for counting. Each hand type needs a
different mix of [combinations](../2.3-Combinations.md), the
[multiplication rule](../2.1-Multiplication-and-Addition-Rules.md) and careful
subtraction of overlaps, and the mistakes people make here (counting order when it does
not matter, double counting pairs) are the same mistakes they make everywhere. The
numbers also explain the rules of the game: rarer hands rank higher. The method
carries over to any "draw a group and classify it" problem: quality checks, genetics,
or a random sample of users.

## Intuition

A standard deck has 52 cards: 13 **ranks** (2, 3, ..., 10, J, Q, K, A) in each of 4
**suits** (spades, hearts, diamonds, clubs). A poker hand is 5 cards, and order does not
matter. So there are $\binom{52}{5} = 2598960$ possible hands, all equally likely after a
fair shuffle.

To count a hand type, describe it as a sequence of choices. A **full house** is three
cards of one rank and two of another, like K K K 7 7. To build one:

1. choose the rank for the three (K): 13 ways;
2. choose which 3 of the 4 kings: $\binom{4}{3} = 4$ ways;
3. choose a different rank for the pair (7): 12 ways;
4. choose which 2 of the 4 sevens: $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ ways.

Multiply: $13 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 = 3744$ full houses. Divide by 2598960, and a full house
comes about once in 694 hands. Every hand type is counted the same way: ranks first,
then suits.

The ten hand types split all $\binom{52}{5}$ hands into disjoint groups; the rarer the
hand, the higher it ranks ((s), (h), (d) mean all five cards are spades, hearts or
diamonds).

## Definition

A **poker hand** is a set of 5 cards from a 52-card deck, so the sample space has

$$
\lvert \Omega \rvert = \binom{52}{5} = \frac{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49 \cdot 48}{5!} = 2598960
$$

equally likely hands (after a fair shuffle). For any hand type $H$,

$$
P(H) = \frac{\text{number of hands of type } H}{\binom{52}{5}}
$$

The hand types, from best to worst, are:

- **Straight flush:** five ranks in a row, all one suit (the best one, A K Q J 10, is a
  **royal flush**). The ace can be high (10 J Q K A) or low (A 2 3 4 5), so there are 10
  possible runs of ranks.
- **Four of a kind:** four cards of one rank.
- **Full house:** three of one rank and two of another.
- **Flush:** all one suit, but not a straight flush.
- **Straight:** five ranks in a row, but not all one suit.
- **Three of a kind:** three of one rank, and two other cards of two different ranks.
- **Two pair:** two of one rank, two of another, and one card of a third rank.
- **One pair:** two of one rank and three other cards of three different ranks.
- **High card:** none of the above.

**In words:** the probability of a hand type is how many 5-card sets have that shape,
divided by all 5-card sets. Each set is counted once, so every count must ignore order.

## Why It Is True

The recipe for each count: first choose the **ranks** (and which rank plays which role),
then choose the **suits** for each rank. Use $\binom{13}{k}$ when $k$ ranks play the **same**
role (order does not matter), and multiply separate choices when ranks play
**different** roles. Here are the counts that are not in the Intuition.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Four of a kind} &= 13 \cdot \binom{4}{4} \cdot 12 \cdot 4 = 624 && \text{(rank of the four, then any 1 of the other 48 cards)} \\
\text{Three of a kind} &= 13 \binom{4}{3} \cdot \binom{12}{2} \cdot 4^2 = 13 \cdot 4 \cdot 66 \cdot 16 = 54912 && \text{(the two odd cards play the same role)} \\
\text{Two pair} &= \binom{13}{2} \binom{4}{2}^2 \cdot 11 \cdot 4 = 78 \cdot 36 \cdot 44 = 123552 && \text{(the two pairs play the same role)} \\
\text{One pair} &= 13 \binom{4}{2} \cdot \binom{12}{3} \cdot 4^3 = 13 \cdot 6 \cdot 220 \cdot 64 = 1098240 && \text{(three odd cards, same role)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For hands with five **different** ranks, choose the 5 ranks ($\binom{13}{5} = 1287$ ways) and a
suit for each card ($4^5 = 1024$ ways), then sort by "in a row?" and "one suit?":

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Straight flush (incl. royal)} &= 10 \cdot 4 = 40 && \text{(10 runs, 4 suits)} \\
\text{Flush} &= \binom{13}{5} \cdot 4 - 40 = 5148 - 40 = 5108 && \text{(one suit, remove straight flushes)} \\
\text{Straight} &= 10 \cdot 4^5 - 40 = 10240 - 40 = 10200 && \text{(a run, remove straight flushes)} \\
\text{High card} &= (1287 - 10)(1024 - 4) = 1277 \cdot 1020 = 1302540 && \text{(not a run, and not one suit)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every hand has exactly one type, so the ten counts must add to $\binom{52}{5}$. They do:
$4 + 36 + 624 + 3744 + 5108 + 10200 + 54912 + 123552 + 1098240 + 1302540 = 2598960$.
This total is a strong check that no hand was missed or counted twice.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Full House

**Problem.** Find the probability of being dealt a full house.

**Solution.**

1. Rank of the three of a kind: 13 choices. Which 3 suits: $\binom{4}{3} = 4$.
2. Rank of the pair: 12 choices (it must differ from the first rank). Which 2 suits:
   $\binom{4}{2} = 6$.
3. Multiply: $13 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 = 3744$.

$$
P(\text{full house}) = \frac{3744}{2598960} \approx 0.00144
$$

**Answer.** About $0.00144$, roughly 1 hand in 694.

**Check.** Why $13 \cdot 12$ and not $\binom{13}{2}$ for the ranks? Because the two ranks play
**different** roles: K K K 7 7 and 7 7 7 K K are different hands. Order of roles
matters, so we use an ordered count.

### Example 2: Two Pair, and a Classic Overcount

**Problem.** Count the two-pair hands. A friend counts "13 ranks for the first pair, 12
for the second pair" and gets twice the right answer. Explain why.

**Solution.**

1. The two pairs play the **same** role: J J 3 3 A is the same hand whether we call the
   jacks "first" or the threes "first". So choose the two pair ranks as a group:
   $\binom{13}{2} = 78$ ways.
2. Suits for each pair: $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ each, so $6^2 = 36$.
3. The fifth card: a rank different from both pairs (11 choices), and any suit (4 choices):
   $11 \cdot 4 = 44$.
4. Multiply: $78 \cdot 36 \cdot 44 = 123552$.

$$
P(\text{two pair}) = \frac{123552}{2598960} \approx 0.0475
$$

5. The friend's count $13 \cdot 12 \cdot 36 \cdot 44 = 247104$ counts every hand twice, once
   for each order of the two pairs.

**Answer.** 123552 hands, probability about $0.0475$.

**Check.** The simulation below deals a million hands and finds about $0.047$, not $0.095$.

### Example 3: Flush, Without the Straight Flushes

**Problem.** Count the flushes that are not straight flushes.

**Solution.**

1. All five cards of one suit: choose the suit (4 ways), then 5 of its 13 ranks
   ($\binom{13}{5} = 1287$): $4 \cdot 1287 = 5148$ hands.
2. Some of these are straight flushes, which rank higher. A straight flush is a run
   (10 choices, from A-2-3-4-5 to 10-J-Q-K-A) in one suit (4 choices): 40 hands.
3. Remove them: $5148 - 40 = 5108$.

$$
P(\text{flush}) = \frac{5108}{2598960} \approx 0.00197
$$

**Answer.** 5108 flushes, probability about $0.00197$.

**Check.** A flush (1 in 509) is rarer than a straight (1 in 255), which is why a flush
beats a straight.

## Simulate It

The code deals a million random hands, sorts them by the pattern of repeated ranks, and
compares with the exact counts.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Deal 5 of 52 cards. Card c has rank c % 13 (0 = two, ..., 12 = ace), suit c // 13.
hands = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(52), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)[:, :5]
ranks, suits = hands % 13, hands // 13

# How many cards of each rank? Sort so the biggest count comes first.
counts = np.stack([(ranks == r).sum(axis=1) for r in range(13)], axis=1)
top = -np.sort(-counts, axis=1)[:, :2]                 # e.g. (3, 2) = full house
flush = np.all(suits == suits[:, :1], axis=1)

total = comb(52, 5)
checks = {
    "Four of a kind": ((top[:, 0] == 4), 624),
    "Full house": ((top[:, 0] == 3) & (top[:, 1] == 2), 3744),
    "Three of a kind": ((top[:, 0] == 3) & (top[:, 1] == 1), 54912),
    "Two pair": ((top[:, 0] == 2) & (top[:, 1] == 2), 123552),
    "One pair": ((top[:, 0] == 2) & (top[:, 1] == 1), 1098240),
    "Any flush": (flush, 5148),                          # includes straight flushes
}
for name, (hit, count) in checks.items():
    print(f"{name:<16} simulation {hit.mean():.5f}, exact {count / total:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Four of a kind   simulation 0.00023, exact 0.00024
Full house       simulation 0.00143, exact 0.00144
Three of a kind  simulation 0.02124, exact 0.02113
Two pair         simulation 0.04741, exact 0.04754
One pair         simulation 0.42267, exact 0.42257
Any flush        simulation 0.00197, exact 0.00198
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Counting ordered hands.** $52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49 \cdot 48$ counts ordered deals. If you
  use it for the total, you must count the favorable hands in order too. Mixing ordered
  and unordered counts gives answers off by a factor of $5! = 120$.
- **Using $13 \cdot 12$ for ranks that play the same role.** Two pair needs $\binom{13}{2}$; a
  full house needs $13 \cdot 12$. Ask: "if I swap the two ranks, do I get a different
  hand?"
- **Forgetting to exclude better hands.** "Three of a kind" must not include full
  houses, and "flush" must not include straight flushes. The extra cards must avoid
  making a better hand.
- **Forgetting the low ace.** A-2-3-4-5 is a straight, so there are 10 runs, not 9.
- **Not checking the total.** Add all categories; if they do not give 2598960,
  something is wrong.

## Where It Is Used

- **Games and gambling:** hand rankings, odds of improving a hand, and the value of a
  bet all start from these counts. See
  [21.10 Games, Gambling and Sports](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md).
- **AI:** poker-playing programs use exact counts and simulation to estimate the
  chance of winning from a given hand, a key step in game-playing AI.
- **Computer science:** testing a card-dealing or shuffling program: the observed
  frequencies of hand types must match the table, or the shuffle is biased.
- **Quality control and biology:** the same "choose groups, then classify" counting
  gives the chance of finding a given pattern of defects in a sample, or of genes in
  offspring.
- **Probability:** many hand probabilities are hypergeometric
  ([7.6 Hypergeometric](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.6-Hypergeometric.md)), for example
  the number of hearts in a hand.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** What is the probability of four of a kind?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Choose the rank (13 ways); all 4 cards of that rank are in; the fifth card is any of the
other 48:

$$P(\text{four of a kind}) = \frac{13 \cdot 48}{2598960} = \frac{624}{2598960} \approx 0.00024$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** How many 5-card hands contain only hearts? What is the probability?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\binom{13}{5} = 1287, \qquad P = \frac{1287}{2598960} \approx 0.000495$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Show that there are 54912 three-of-a-kind hands. Why is it
$\binom{12}{2}$ for the other two ranks and not $12 \cdot 11$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rank of the three: 13. Suits for it: $\binom{4}{3} = 4$. The two other cards must have two
**different** ranks, also different from the first (otherwise we get a full house or four
of a kind): $\binom{12}{2} = 66$ ways. Each of these cards has any of 4 suits: $4^2 = 16$.

$$13 \cdot 4 \cdot 66 \cdot 16 = 54912$$

The two extra cards play the same role ("odd cards"), so swapping their ranks gives the
same hand. Using $12 \cdot 11$ would count each hand twice.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** What is the probability that a hand contains **at least one pair**, that
is, at least two cards of the same rank?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the complement: all five ranks different. Choose 5 ranks and a suit for each card:
$\binom{13}{5} \cdot 4^5 = 1287 \cdot 1024 = 1317888$ hands.

$$P(\text{at least one pair}) = 1 - \frac{1317888}{2598960} \approx 1 - 0.5071 = 0.4929$$

This includes one pair, two pair, three and four of a kind, and full houses.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** In the game Texas hold'em, you get 2 cards. What is the probability that they
form a pair (two cards of the same rank)? Solve it in two ways: with combinations, and
by thinking of the second card.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Combinations:** there are $\binom{52}{2} = 1326$ two-card hands. A pair is a rank (13
ways) and 2 of its 4 suits ($\binom{4}{2} = 6$): 78 hands.

$$P(\text{pair}) = \frac{78}{1326} = \frac{1}{17} \approx 0.0588$$

**Second card:** whatever the first card is, 3 of the remaining 51 cards match its rank:

$$P(\text{pair}) = \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{17}$$

Both methods agree. The second method is a preview of conditional probability
([4.1 Conditional Probability](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.1-Conditional-Probability.md)).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 by dealing many hands.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 500_000
hands = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(52), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)[:, :5]
ranks = np.sort(hands % 13, axis=1)
has_pair = np.any(ranks[:, 1:] == ranks[:, :-1], axis=1)
print(f"Simulation: {has_pair.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {1 - 1317888 / 2598960:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.4924
Exact:      0.4929
```

</details>

## Summary

- There are $\binom{52}{5} = 2598960$ equally likely poker hands.
- Count a hand type by choosing ranks first, then suits, and multiply.
- Ranks with the same role are chosen with $\binom{13}{k}$; ranks with different roles are
  chosen in order ($13 \cdot 12$).
- Subtract hands that belong to a better category (straight flushes from flushes).
- The ten categories add up to the total, which is the best check of your work.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(\text{full house}) = \frac{13 \binom{4}{3} \cdot 12 \binom{4}{2}}{\binom{52}{5}} = \frac{3744}{2598960} \approx 0.00144}
$$
