# 2.11.1 Birthday Problem

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 02: Counting](../README.md) / [2.11 Classic Counting Problems](README.md) / 2.11.1 Birthday Problem

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [2.10 Story Proofs](../2.10-Story-Proofs.md)

> **Big idea:** In a group of just 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday is more than one half, because the number of pairs grows like $n^2$.

---

## Why It Matters

Most people guess that you need about 183 people (half of 365) for a 50% chance of a
shared birthday. The true answer is 23. This surprise is not a party trick: the same
counting tells you how soon random IDs, hash values and cryptographic fingerprints
**collide**. It explains why a 32-bit hash already collides after about 77,000 random
keys, and why secure hashes need 256 bits. The tools are ones you know:
[2.2 Permutations](../2.2-Permutations.md) and the complement.

## Intuition

Think about pairs, not people. With 23 people there are $\binom{23}{2} = 253$ pairs, and
each pair has a $\frac{1}{365}$ chance of sharing a birthday. With 253 chances of size
$\frac{1}{365}$ each, a match somewhere is no longer surprising.

Now compare with the question "does anyone share **my** birthday?". That involves only
22 pairs (me with each other person), so it is much less likely: about $0.06$. The
famous surprise comes from mixing up these two questions.

The easiest way to compute the answer is through the complement. Line the people up.
Person 1 can have any birthday. Person 2 must avoid 1 day, so has 364 good days of
365. Person 3 must avoid 2 days: 363 of 365. Each new person has to avoid more days, and
the chance that everyone avoids everyone shrinks fast.

## Picture

```text
   n    P(at least two share a birthday)        (50 marks = probability 1)
   5    0.027  #
  10    0.117  ######
  15    0.253  #############
  20    0.411  #####################
  23    0.507  #########################   <- first n above 0.5
  30    0.706  ###################################
  40    0.891  #############################################
  50    0.970  #################################################
  60    0.994  ##################################################
  70    0.999  ##################################################
```

The probability of a shared birthday rises quickly: past one half at 23 people and
above 0.99 by 60 people, far below the 366 people that would make it certain.

## Definition

Assume each person's birthday is one of $N = 365$ days, all days equally likely, and
different people's birthdays are unrelated (no twins, and ignore February 29). Then for
a group of $n$ people, with $n \le N$:

$$
P(\text{no shared birthday}) = \frac{N (N-1) (N-2) \cdots (N-n+1)}{N^n} = \frac{N!}{(N-n)! \thinspace N^n}
$$

$$
P(\text{at least one shared birthday}) = 1 - \frac{N (N-1) \cdots (N-n+1)}{N^n}
$$

where $n$ is the group size and $N$ is the number of equally likely days (or hash
values, or IDs). For $n \gt N$ the probability of a shared birthday is 1, by the
[pigeonhole principle](../2.9-Pigeonhole-Principle.md).

**In words:** the chance of no match is the number of birthday lists with all days
different, divided by the number of all birthday lists.

A very useful **approximation** is

$$
P(\text{no shared birthday}) \approx \exp\left( -\frac{n(n-1)}{2N} \right)
$$

**In words:** the chance of no collision falls like $e$ to the minus (number of pairs
divided by $N$). Setting it equal to $\frac{1}{2}$ gives the 50% point
$n \approx \sqrt{2 N \ln 2} \approx 1.18 \sqrt{N}$.

## Why It Is True

**The exact formula.** List the birthdays in order: person 1, person 2, ..., person $n$.

1. Each person has $N$ possible days, so there are $N^n$ lists
   ([2.4 Sampling With Replacement](../2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md)). Under our
   assumptions all lists are equally likely.
2. Lists with all days different: person 1 has $N$ choices, person 2 has $N - 1$, ...,
   person $n$ has $N - n + 1$. This is a permutation count,
   $N(N-1) \cdots (N-n+1)$.
3. Divide, then use the complement: "at least one shared" is everything except
   "no shared".

**The approximation.** Write the exact answer as a product of $n$ factors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{no shared}) &= \frac{N}{N} \cdot \frac{N-1}{N} \cdots \frac{N-n+1}{N} = \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} \left( 1 - \frac{i}{N} \right) && \text{(one factor per person)} \\
&\approx \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} e^{-i/N} && \text{(since } 1 - x \approx e^{-x} \text{ for small } x \text{)} \\
&= \exp\left( -\frac{0 + 1 + \dots + (n-1)}{N} \right) = \exp\left( -\frac{n(n-1)}{2N} \right) && \text{(add the exponents)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The fact $1 - x \approx e^{-x}$ for small $x$ is easy to check on a calculator:
$e^{-0.01} \approx 0.99005$, very close to $0.99$. The sum $0 + 1 + \dots + (n-1)$ equals
$\binom{n}{2}$, the number of pairs, which is where "think about pairs" comes from.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Twenty-Three People

**Problem.** Find the probability that at least two of 23 people share a birthday.
Compare the exact answer with the approximation.

**Solution.**

1. Exact: multiply the 23 factors. The last one is for person 23, who must avoid 22
   days, so it is $\frac{365 - 22}{365} = \frac{343}{365}$:

$$
P(\text{no shared}) = \frac{365}{365} \cdot \frac{364}{365} \cdot \frac{363}{365} \cdots \frac{343}{365} \approx 0.4927
$$

2. Complement: $P(\text{shared}) = 1 - 0.4927 = 0.5073$.
3. Approximation: $\frac{n(n-1)}{2N} = \frac{23 \cdot 22}{730} = \frac{506}{730} \approx 0.6932$, and
   $e^{-0.6932} \approx 0.5000$. So $P(\text{shared}) \approx 0.5000$.

**Answer.** $P(\text{shared birthday}) \approx 0.507$. The approximation gives $0.500$.

**Check.** With 22 people the exact answer is $0.476$, below one half, so 23 really is
the smallest group that passes 50%. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Hash Collisions

**Problem.** A program gives each record a random 32-bit ID, so there are
$N = 2^{32} \approx 4.29 \times 10^9$ equally likely IDs. (a) About how many records give a
50% chance that two IDs collide? (b) What is the chance of a collision with 10,000
records?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The 50% point is $n \approx \sqrt{2 N \ln 2}$:

$$
n \approx \sqrt{2 \ln 2} \cdot \sqrt{2^{32}} \approx 1.1774 \cdot 65536 \approx 77163
$$

2. (b) Use the approximation with $n = 10000$:

$$
P(\text{collision}) \approx 1 - \exp\left( -\frac{10000 \cdot 9999}{2 \cdot 2^{32}} \right) = 1 - e^{-0.01164} \approx 0.0116
$$

**Answer.** (a) About 77,000 records. (b) About $0.0116$, a bit more than 1 in 100.

**Check.** The 50% point grows like $\sqrt{N}$, not like $N$. With a $b$-bit hash,
collisions appear after about $2^{b/2}$ items. This is the **birthday attack**: to make
collisions too hard to find, cryptographic hashes such as SHA-256 use 256 bits, so that
$2^{128}$ tries would be needed.

### Example 3: Sharing My Birthday

**Problem.** You are in a room with 22 other people. What is the probability that
someone shares **your** birthday? How many other people are needed for this to pass
one half?

**Solution.**

1. Each other person misses your birthday on 364 of 365 days. The lists of their
   birthdays that all miss yours number $364^{22}$, out of $365^{22}$:

$$
P(\text{someone shares mine}) = 1 - \left( \frac{364}{365} \right)^{22} \approx 1 - 0.9414 = 0.0586
$$

2. For one half we need $\left( \frac{364}{365} \right)^m \le \frac{1}{2}$. Trying values: $m = 252$
   gives $0.4991$ for the probability of a match, and $m = 253$ gives $0.5005$.

**Answer.** About $0.059$ with 22 others; you need 253 other people to pass one half.

**Check.** The answer for "any pair" with 23 people ($0.507$) is almost 9 times bigger
than for "a pair including me" ($0.059$), because there are 253 pairs but only 22 of them
include you. Note that 253 shows up in both questions!

## Simulate It

The code makes many random groups, checks whether any birthday repeats, and compares
with the exact formula and the approximation.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000
N = 365

for n in [10, 23, 50]:
    days = np.sort(rng.integers(0, N, size=(n_trials, n)), axis=1)
    shared = np.any(days[:, 1:] == days[:, :-1], axis=1)   # equal neighbors
    exact = 1 - np.prod((N - np.arange(n)) / N)
    approx = 1 - np.exp(-n * (n - 1) / (2 * N))
    print(f"n = {n:>2}: simulation {shared.mean():.4f}, "
          f"exact {exact:.4f}, approximation {approx:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n = 10: simulation 0.1174, exact 0.1169, approximation 0.1160
n = 23: simulation 0.5092, exact 0.5073, approximation 0.5000
n = 50: simulation 0.9707, exact 0.9704, approximation 0.9651
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Answering the "my birthday" question.** "Two people share a birthday" and "someone
  shares my birthday" are different events. The first needs 23 people for one half; the
  second needs 253 others.
- **Thinking half of 365 people are needed.** The number of pairs, $\binom{n}{2}$, grows
  much faster than $n$. That is why the answer is near $\sqrt{365}$, not $365/2$.
- **Counting without order.** The equally likely outcomes are **ordered** lists of
  birthdays. Counting sets of birthdays gives outcomes that are not equally likely, as
  in [2.4](../2.4-Sampling-With-Replacement.md).
- **Worrying that real birthdays are uneven.** Real birthdays are not perfectly
  uniform, but unevenness only makes a match **more** likely. So 23 people give at least
  about one half in real life too.
- **Forgetting the assumptions.** The formula assumes unrelated people. A room of
  twins or a group chosen by birth month breaks it.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** hash table collisions, random ID generation (UUIDs use 122
  random bits so collisions stay negligible), and checksums. See
  [21.3.2 Hashing and Bloom Filters](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.2-Hashing-and-Bloom-Filters.md).
- **Security:** the birthday attack on hash functions and digital signatures; see
  [21.4 Cryptography and Security](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md).
- **AI and machine learning:** duplicate detection in large data sets, and why
  randomly sampled mini-batches with replacement quickly repeat examples.
- **Biology and forensics:** the chance that two people in a database share a DNA
  profile by coincidence grows with the square of the database size.
- **Everyday life:** coincidences ("we have the same birthday!") are far more common
  than they feel, because there are so many pairs; see
  [22.7 Law of Small Numbers](../../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** What is the probability that at least two of 10 people share a birthday?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$1 - \frac{365 \cdot 364 \cdots 356}{365^{10}} \approx 1 - 0.8831 = 0.1169$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** In a class of 31 students, what is the probability that at least one of the
other 30 students shares **your** birthday?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$1 - \left( \frac{364}{365} \right)^{30} \approx 1 - 0.9210 = 0.0790$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A website gives each user a random 6-digit code (000000 to 999999, all
equally likely). About how many users give a 50% chance that two of them get the same
code?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $N = 10^6$, so

$$n \approx \sqrt{2 N \ln 2} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 0.6931} \approx 1177.4$$

About 1,178 users. The exact formula agrees: $n = 1178$ is the first group size with a
collision probability above one half.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that adding one person to a group of $n$ multiplies
$P(\text{no shared birthday})$ by $\frac{365 - n}{365}$. Use this to explain why the
probability of no match falls faster and faster.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The exact formula for $n + 1$ people has one more factor than the formula for $n$ people:

$$\frac{365 \cdots (365 - n)}{365^{n+1}} = \frac{365 \cdots (365 - n + 1)}{365^n} \cdot \frac{365 - n}{365}$$

The new person must avoid the $n$ birthdays already taken. As $n$ grows, the factor
$\frac{365 - n}{365}$ gets smaller, so each new person cuts the "no match" probability by a
bigger fraction than the person before.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $P(\text{no shared birthday}) \le \exp\left( -\frac{n(n-1)}{2N} \right)$
for every $n$, using the fact that $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ for all real $x$. What does this say
about the approximation?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each factor satisfies $1 - \frac{i}{N} \le e^{-i/N}$, and all factors are $\ge 0$ for $n \le N$, so
the product of the left sides is at most the product of the right sides:

$$\prod_{i=0}^{n-1} \left( 1 - \frac{i}{N} \right) \le \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} e^{-i/N} = \exp\left( -\frac{n(n-1)}{2N} \right)$$

So the approximation always **overestimates** the chance of no match, and therefore
underestimates the chance of a collision. For $n = 23$: exact $0.5073$, approximation
$0.5000$. A collision is at least as likely as the approximation says, which makes it a
safe estimate when designing hash sizes.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Use simulation to find the smallest group size $n$ for which the
probability of a shared birthday is above one half. Compare with the exact formula.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

for n in range(20, 26):
    days = np.sort(rng.integers(0, 365, size=(n_trials, n)), axis=1)
    sim = np.mean(np.any(days[:, 1:] == days[:, :-1], axis=1))
    exact = 1 - np.prod((365 - np.arange(n)) / 365)

```

n = 21: simulation 0.4451, exact 0.4437
n = 22: simulation 0.4763, exact 0.4757

Both columns cross one half between $n = 22$ and $n = 23$.

</details>

## Summary

- Use the complement: count the birthday lists with no repeat, $N(N-1) \cdots (N-n+1)$,
  out of $N^n$.
- With 365 days, 23 people give a probability of about $0.507$ of a shared birthday.
- What matters is the number of pairs, $\binom{n}{2}$, so collisions appear near
  $n \approx 1.18\sqrt{N}$.
- The approximation $\exp\left( -\frac{n(n-1)}{2N} \right)$ works for any large $N$: dates,
  hashes, IDs.
- "Any two match" and "someone matches me" are very different questions.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(\text{shared birthday}) = 1 - \frac{N (N-1) \cdots (N-n+1)}{N^n} \approx 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/(2N)}}
$$
