# 5.7 Distribution vs Random Variable

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 05: Discrete Random Variables](README.md) / 5.7 Distribution vs Random Variable

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [5.6 Functions of a Random Variable](5.6-Functions-of-a-Random-Variable.md)

> **Big idea:** Two random variables can have exactly the same distribution and still be different functions, even never equal, so "same distribution" is much weaker than "same random variable".

---

## Why It Matters

When we say that $X$ is binomial, or that the data is normal, we describe a
**distribution**, not a particular random variable. Mixing up the two causes real
errors: treating two dice as one die, or thinking that $X + X$ is the same as the
sum of two independent copies of $X$. This topic separates the two ideas. It
prepares you for "independent and identically distributed" in
[10.6 IID](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.6-IID.md), the basic assumption of most of
statistics and machine learning.

## Intuition

Flip a fair coin once. Let $X = 1$ if it shows heads and $X = 0$ otherwise. Let
$Y = 1$ if it shows tails and $Y = 0$ otherwise. Each one is 0 or 1 with chance
$\frac{1}{2}$, so their PMFs are identical. If someone showed you only a long list
of values of $X$, or only a list of values of $Y$, you could not tell which was
which.

But $X$ and $Y$ are **never** equal. When the coin shows heads, $X = 1$ and $Y = 0$.
When it shows tails, $X = 0$ and $Y = 1$. In fact $X + Y = 1$ on every flip.

So a PMF tells you how **one** variable behaves on its own. It says nothing about
which outcome gives which value, or how two variables move together. Think of two
students who each score A, B or C with the same chances. They have the same
distribution of grades, but they are different people, and on a given day their
grades can differ.

## Picture

```text
        Ω          X = 1 if heads          Ω          Y = 1 if tails
     +-----+                            +-----+
     |  H  | ----------> 1              |  H  | ----------> 0
     |  T  | ----------> 0              |  T  | ----------> 1
     +-----+                            +-----+

     PMF of X:  p(0) = 1/2, p(1) = 1/2   PMF of Y:  p(0) = 1/2, p(1) = 1/2

          same distribution, different functions: X and Y never agree
```

$X$ and $Y$ have the same PMF, yet on every outcome they give different values.

## Definition

The **distribution** of a random variable $X$ (also called its **law**) is the rule
that gives $P(X \in B)$ for every set of numbers $B$. For a discrete variable the
distribution is completely described by its PMF $p_X$, or equally by its CDF $F_X$.

Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are **identically distributed**, or **equal in
distribution**, if they have the same distribution:

$$
X \overset{d}{=} Y \quad \iff \quad p_X(x) = p_Y(x) \ \text{ for every } x
$$

where the $d$ over the equals sign stands for "distribution".

**In words:** $X$ and $Y$ are equal in distribution when each value has the same
probability under both, even if they come from different outcomes.

Two random variables on the same $\Omega$ are **equal**, $X = Y$, if they agree
on every outcome:

$$
X = Y \quad \iff \quad X(\omega) = Y(\omega) \ \text{ for every } \omega \in \Omega
$$

**In words:** equal random variables give the same number on every single outcome.

The connection between the two ideas is one-way:

- If $X = Y$, then $X \overset{d}{=} Y$.
- If $X \overset{d}{=} Y$, then $X$ and $Y$ may still be different, and may even
  never be equal.

## Why It Is True

**Equal implies equal in distribution.** If $X(\omega) = Y(\omega)$ for every
$\omega$, then for every set $B$ the events $\lbrace X \in B \rbrace$ and
$\lbrace Y \in B \rbrace$ contain the same outcomes. Equal events have equal
probabilities, so $P(X \in B) = P(Y \in B)$.

**The converse fails.** The coin example is a counterexample: $X \overset{d}{=} Y$,
but $P(X = Y) = 0$.

**Functions keep equality in distribution.** If $X \overset{d}{=} Y$ and $g$ is any
function, then $g(X) \overset{d}{=} g(Y)$. Use the rule from
[5.6 Functions of a Random Variable](5.6-Functions-of-a-Random-Variable.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{g(X)}(z) &= \sum_{x \thinspace : \thinspace g(x) = z} p_X(x) && \text{(PMF of a function)} \\
&= \sum_{x \thinspace : \thinspace g(x) = z} p_Y(x) && \text{(} p_X = p_Y \text{)} \\
&= p_{g(Y)}(z) && \text{(PMF of a function, read backwards)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So **any** question about one variable alone has the same answer for $X$ and $Y$.
But a question that involves **both**, such as $P(X = Y)$, $P(X \lt Y)$ or the
PMF of $X + Y$, is not decided by the two PMFs. For that you need the **joint
distribution** of [Module 10](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/README.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Top and Bottom of a Die

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. Let $X$ be the number on top and $Y$ the number on
the bottom. On a standard die opposite faces add to 7, so $Y = 7 - X$. Are $X$ and
$Y$ equal in distribution? Find $P(X = Y)$ and the PMF of $X + Y$.

**Solution.**

1. $X$ is uniform on $\lbrace 1, \dots, 6 \rbrace$, each value with probability $\frac{1}{6}$.
2. $g(x) = 7 - x$ is one-to-one and sends $\lbrace 1, \dots, 6 \rbrace$ to
   $\lbrace 6, \dots, 1 \rbrace$, the same set. So each value of $Y$ also has
   probability $\frac{1}{6}$, and $X \overset{d}{=} Y$.
3. $X = Y$ would need $X = 7 - X$, that is $X = 3.5$, which is impossible. So
   $P(X = Y) = 0$.
4. $X + Y = X + (7 - X) = 7$ on every outcome, so $P(X + Y = 7) = 1$.

**Answer.** Yes, $X \overset{d}{=} Y$, but $P(X = Y) = 0$ and $X + Y = 7$ always.

**Check.** Compare with two **separate** dice in Example 2, whose sum can be
anything from 2 to 12. Same individual distributions, completely different sums.

### Example 2: Two Dice vs One Die Doubled

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice independently and call the results $X_1$ and
$X_2$. Both are uniform on $\lbrace 1, \dots, 6 \rbrace$, so $X_1 \overset{d}{=} X_2$.
Compare the PMFs of $X_1 + X_2$ and $2 X_1$ at the values 7 and 8.

**Solution.**

1. $X_1 + X_2$ is the sum of two dice. From [5.3](5.3-Probability-Mass-Function.md),
   $P(X_1 + X_2 = 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$ and
   $P(X_1 + X_2 = 8) = \frac{5}{36}$.
2. $2 X_1$ takes only even values $2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12$, each with probability
   $\frac{1}{6}$.
3. So $P(2 X_1 = 7) = 0$ and $P(2 X_1 = 8) = P(X_1 = 4) = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Answer.** $P(X_1 + X_2 = 7) = \frac{1}{6}$ but $P(2X_1 = 7) = 0$;
$P(X_1 + X_2 = 8) = \frac{5}{36} \approx 0.139$ but $P(2X_1 = 8) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$.
The two sums have different distributions.

**Check.** $X_1 + X_2$ adds two **different** random variables that happen to share
a distribution. $2X_1 = X_1 + X_1$ adds one random variable to itself. Replacing
$X_2$ by $X_1$ because "they have the same distribution" is wrong.

### Example 3: A One-Time Pad Hides the Message

**Problem.** A secret bit $m$ (0 or 1) is sent as the XOR of $m$ and a key bit $K$,
that is, $C = (m + K) \operatorname{mod} 2$. The key is a fair random bit,
$P(K = 1) = \frac{1}{2}$. Find the PMF of $C$ when $m = 0$ and when $m = 1$.

**Solution.**

1. If $m = 0$: $C = (0 + K) \operatorname{mod} 2 = K$, so $P(C = 0) = P(C = 1) = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. If $m = 1$: $C = (1 + K) \operatorname{mod} 2 = 1 - K$, so $C = 1$ when $K = 0$ and $C = 0$ when
   $K = 1$. Again $P(C = 0) = P(C = 1) = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Answer.** In both cases $C$ is a fair random bit. The two ciphertexts are equal
in distribution, although as random variables they are different ($K$ and $1 - K$).

**Check.** An attacker who sees only $C$ sees the same distribution whatever the
message is, so $C$ gives no information about $m$. This is why the one-time pad is
perfectly secret ([21.4 Cryptography and Security](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md)).

## Simulate It

We check Examples 1 and 2: the top and bottom faces have the same PMF but never
agree, and $X_1 + X_2$ has a different PMF from $2X_1$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

X = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)    # top face
Y = 7 - X                                # bottom face: same distribution as X
print("k   P(X = k)  P(Y = k)")
for k in range(1, 7):
    print(f"{k}   {np.mean(X == k):.4f}    {np.mean(Y == k):.4f}")
print(f"P(X = Y) = {np.mean(X == Y):.4f}  (exact 0)")

# Two different dice vs one die doubled.
X1, X2 = X, rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
for s, sum_exact, double_exact in [(7, 6 / 36, 0.0), (8, 5 / 36, 1 / 6)]:
    print(f"s = {s}: P(X1+X2 = s) {np.mean(X1 + X2 == s):.4f} (exact {sum_exact:.4f}), "
          f"P(2X1 = s) {np.mean(2 * X1 == s):.4f} (exact {double_exact:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
k   P(X = k)  P(Y = k)
1   0.1669    0.1662
2   0.1668    0.1664
3   0.1665    0.1672
4   0.1672    0.1665
5   0.1664    0.1668
6   0.1662    0.1669
P(X = Y) = 0.0000  (exact 0)
s = 7: P(X1+X2 = s) 0.1668 (exact 0.1667), P(2X1 = s) 0.0000 (exact 0.0000)
s = 8: P(X1+X2 = s) 0.1391 (exact 0.1389), P(2X1 = s) 0.1672 (exact 0.1667)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **"Same distribution, so same variable."** Top and bottom faces of a die have
  the same distribution but are never equal. Equality in distribution says nothing
  about individual outcomes.
- **Writing $2X$ when you mean $X_1 + X_2$.** The sum of two independent copies is
  not the same as one copy doubled (Example 2). Give each copy its own name.
- **Computing $P(X \lt Y)$ or the PMF of $X + Y$ from the two PMFs alone.** These
  depend on how $X$ and $Y$ are linked. You need their joint distribution
  ([10.1 Joint PMF and PDF](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md)).
- **Confusing "identically distributed" with "independent".** Top and bottom faces
  are identically distributed but completely dependent. Two unrelated variables
  can be independent with different distributions. "iid" asks for **both**.
- **Thinking a distribution belongs to one experiment.** A fair coin flip and
  "the die shows an even number" give the same $\text{Bern}(\frac{1}{2})$
  distribution, from very different sample spaces.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** models assume training and test data are
  identically distributed. When the test data's distribution changes
  (**distribution shift**), accuracy drops.
- **Generative models:** a generative model tries to produce new samples that are
  equal in distribution to the data, not copies of the data
  ([21.1.4 Generative Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
- **Simulation:** we may replace a hard experiment by any easy one with the same
  distribution. A computer never flips a real coin; it produces bits that are
  equal in distribution to coin flips.
- **Cryptography:** a cipher is secure when ciphertexts for different messages are
  (close to) equal in distribution, as in Example 3.
- **Everyday life:** two players with the same chances of winning are not the same
  player; on a given day one can win while the other loses.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $Y$ be the indicator that a fair die shows an odd number, and $Z$
the indicator that a fair coin shows heads. Are $Y$ and $Z$ equal in distribution?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Yes. Both are 1 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ and 0 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$,
so both are $\text{Bern}(\frac{1}{2})$:

$$P(Y = 1) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} = P(Z = 1)$$

They even live on different sample spaces, so the statement $Y = Z$ does not make sense, but
the statement $Y \overset{d}{=} Z$ does.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ is uniform on $\lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$ and $Y = 4 - X$. Show that
$X \overset{d}{=} Y$ and find $P(X = Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Y$ takes the values $3, 2, 1$ when $X$ takes $1, 2, 3$, each with probability
$\frac{1}{3}$, so $Y$ is also uniform on $\lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$. $X = Y$ means
$X = 4 - X$, so $X = 2$:

$$P(X = Y) = P(X = 2) = \frac{1}{3}$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Flip two fair coins independently, with $X_1, X_2 \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$
(1 for heads). Find the PMF of $X_1 + X_2$ and of $2X_1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $X_1 + X_2$, the outcomes 00, 01, 10, 11 are equally likely:

$$P(X_1 + X_2 = 0) = \tfrac{1}{4}, \quad P(X_1 + X_2 = 1) = \tfrac{2}{4}, \quad P(X_1 + X_2 = 2) = \tfrac{1}{4}$$

For $2X_1$, only one coin matters:

$$P(2X_1 = 0) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \quad P(2X_1 = 2) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

The distributions differ: $2X_1$ can never be 1.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X$ is uniform on $\lbrace -1, 0, 1 \rbrace$. Show that
$X \overset{d}{=} -X$. Are $X$ and $X^2$ equal in distribution? Find $P(X = -X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$-X$ takes $1, 0, -1$ when $X$ takes $-1, 0, 1$, each with probability
$\frac{1}{3}$, so $-X$ is also uniform on $\lbrace -1, 0, 1 \rbrace$.

$X^2$ takes the value 1 with probability $\frac{2}{3}$ and 0 with probability
$\frac{1}{3}$, and never $-1$. So $X$ and $X^2$ are **not** equal in distribution.

$X = -X$ means $2X = 0$, so $X = 0$:

$$P(X = -X) = P(X = 0) = \frac{1}{3}$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $X_1 \overset{d}{=} X_2$ and $Y_1 \overset{d}{=} Y_2$. Does it
follow that $X_1 + Y_1 \overset{d}{=} X_2 + Y_2$? Prove it or give a
counterexample.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No. Flip one fair coin and let $X$ be the indicator of heads. Take
$X_1 = X_2 = X$, $Y_1 = X$ and $Y_2 = 1 - X$. All four are $\text{Bern}(\frac{1}{2})$,
so $X_1 \overset{d}{=} X_2$ and $Y_1 \overset{d}{=} Y_2$. But

$$X_1 + Y_1 = 2X \in \lbrace 0, 2 \rbrace, \qquad X_2 + Y_2 = X + (1 - X) = 1$$

The first is 0 or 2, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$; the second is always 1.
The distribution of a sum depends on how the two variables are linked, not only on
their separate distributions. (If the pairs are independent, the answer becomes
yes; see [Module 10](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/README.md).)

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the one-time pad of Example 3 with one million random
keys. Print the fraction of $C = 1$ for $m = 0$ and for $m = 1$, and the fraction of
keys where the two ciphertexts are equal.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
K = rng.integers(0, 2, size=1_000_000)   # fair random key bits
C0 = 0 ^ K                               # ciphertext when m = 0
C1 = 1 ^ K                               # ciphertext when m = 1
print(f"m = 0: P(C = 1) = {C0.mean():.4f}  (exact 0.5)")
print(f"m = 1: P(C = 1) = {C1.mean():.4f}  (exact 0.5)")
print(f"Fraction of keys with C0 = C1: {np.mean(C0 == C1):.4f}  (exact 0)")
```

Output:

```text
m = 0: P(C = 1) = 0.4998  (exact 0.5)
m = 1: P(C = 1) = 0.5002  (exact 0.5)
Fraction of keys with C0 = C1: 0.0000  (exact 0)
```

Same distribution, yet the two ciphertexts never agree for the same key.

</details>

## Summary

- The **distribution** of $X$ is the rule $B \mapsto P(X \in B)$; for a discrete
  variable it is the PMF (or the CDF).
- $X \overset{d}{=} Y$ means equal PMFs; $X = Y$ means equal values on every
  outcome. The second implies the first, not the other way round.
- Identically distributed variables answer every question about **one** variable
  the same way, including questions about $g(X)$ and $g(Y)$.
- Questions about **two** variables together, like $X + Y$ or $P(X = Y)$, need the
  joint distribution.
- $X_1 + X_2$ (two copies) and $2X_1$ (one copy doubled) are different.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{X \overset{d}{=} Y \iff p_X(x) = p_Y(x) \text{ for all } x, \quad \text{which does not imply } X = Y}
$$
