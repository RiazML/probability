# 1.1 What is Probability

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 01: Sets, Sample Spaces and Events](README.md) / 1.1 What is Probability

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 30 minutes · **Before this:** nothing. This is the very first topic.

> **Big idea:** Probability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely something is to happen.

---

## Why It Matters

Almost nothing in life is certain. Will it rain tomorrow? Is this email spam? Will
the server crash tonight? **Probability** gives us a language and a set of rules to
answer questions like these with numbers instead of guesses. Every other file in
this repo, and a large part of AI and machine learning, is built on this one idea.

## Intuition

Think about a weather app that says "70% chance of rain". It does not promise rain.
It says: on days like today, it rained on about 7 out of 10 of them. The number
tells you how strongly to expect rain, so you can decide whether to carry an
umbrella.

Now think about a fair die. It has 6 faces, and no face is special. If you roll it,
each face has the same chance. So the chance of a 6 is 1 out of 6, which is about
0.167.

Notice two things that are always true:

- A chance can never be less than "never" (0) or more than "always" (1).
- The more ways something can happen, the bigger its chance. Rolling an even number
  (2, 4 or 6) is three times as likely as rolling a 6.

Where do the numbers come from? There are three common ways to get them:

| Way | Idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Counting** | All outcomes are equally likely, so count them | A fair die: 1 out of 6 |
| **Frequency** | Repeat the experiment many times and see how often it happens | A factory finds 3 broken phones in every 1,000 |
| **Belief** | A careful, honest degree of confidence, updated with evidence | A doctor says "I am 90% sure it is the flu" |

All three follow the **same rules**, which you will learn in
[Module 03](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/README.md). Topic
[3.1 Interpretations of Probability](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.1-Interpretations-of-Probability.md)
compares them in detail.

## Picture

```text
    0.0              0.25             0.5              0.75             1.0

 one die         from a deck     shows heads      heart            from 1 to 6
```

Every probability sits somewhere on this line, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

## Definition

The **probability** of an event $A$, written $P(A)$, is a number that measures how
likely $A$ is to happen. An **event** is simply something that can happen or not
happen, such as "the die shows 6". It is always true that:

$$
0 \le P(A) \le 1
$$

- $P(A) = 0$ means $A$ is impossible.
- $P(A) = 1$ means $A$ is certain.
- Numbers in between mean "possible, but not certain". Bigger means more likely.

**In words:** a probability is never negative and never more than 1.

When every outcome of an experiment is **equally likely**, there is a simple way to
compute it. We call this the **counting rule** (also called the classical or naive
definition):

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of outcomes}}
$$

**In words:** count the ways your event can happen, and divide by the number of
ways anything at all can happen.

> **Warning:** The counting rule works **only** when all outcomes are equally
> likely. A coin that lands heads 60% of the time, or the sum of two dice, needs more
> care. See Example 3 below and [3.7 Equally Likely Outcomes](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.7-Equally-Likely-Outcomes.md).

## Why It Is True

Why must the counting rule give a number between 0 and 1? Say the experiment has
$n$ equally likely outcomes, and the event $A$ contains $k$ of them. The event cannot
contain fewer than 0 outcomes or more than all $n$ of them, so $0 \le k \le n$.
Divide every part by $n$, which is positive:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 \le k \le n &\implies \frac{0}{n} \le \frac{k}{n} \le \frac{n}{n} && \text{(divide by } n \gt 0 \text{)} \\
&\implies 0 \le P(A) \le 1 && \text{(since } P(A) = k/n \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Also, if $k = 0$ the event is impossible and $P(A) = 0$. If $k = n$ every outcome is
in the event, so it is certain and $P(A) = 1$. The rule agrees with common sense.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Rolling an Even Number

**Problem.** You roll a fair six-sided die. What is the probability of an even number?

**Solution.**

1. The die is fair, so the 6 outcomes $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ are equally likely.
2. The even outcomes are $2, 4, 6$. That is 3 outcomes.
3. Use the counting rule:

$$
P(\text{even}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{even}) = 0.5$. Half of all rolls are even, in the long run.

**Check.** There are as many even faces (3) as odd faces (3), so the answer should be
exactly one half. It is.

### Example 2: Guessing a PIN

**Problem.** A phone uses a 4-digit PIN, from 0000 to 9999. A thief guesses one PIN at
random. What is the probability the guess is right? What if the thief gets 3
different guesses?

**Solution.**

1. Each digit has 10 choices, so there are $10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10{,}000$
   possible PINs. A random guess makes them all equally likely.
2. Exactly 1 PIN is correct:

$$
P(\text{correct guess}) = \frac{1}{10000} = 0.0001
$$

3. With 3 **different** guesses, the thief covers 3 of the 10,000 PINs:

$$
P(\text{correct within 3 guesses}) = \frac{3}{10000} = 0.0003
$$

**Answer.** About 1 in 10,000 for one guess, and 3 in 10,000 for three guesses.

**Check.** This is why phones lock after a few wrong tries. The chance stays tiny.
(Counting PINs like this is the topic of [Module 02](../02-COUNTING/README.md).)

### Example 3: The Sum of Two Dice

**Problem.** You roll two fair dice and add them. A friend says: "The sum can be any of
the 11 numbers from 2 to 12, so each sum has probability $\frac{1}{11}$." Is that right?

**Solution.**

1. The 11 sums are **not** equally likely, so we cannot count them directly.
2. Instead, list the pairs (first die, second die). Each die has 6 faces, so there
   are $6 \times 6 = 36$ pairs, and these **are** equally likely.
3. A sum of 2 needs $(1, 1)$: only 1 pair. A sum of 7 can come from
   $(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)$: 6 pairs.

$$
P(\text{sum} = 2) = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.028, \qquad P(\text{sum} = 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167
$$

**Answer.** The friend is wrong. A sum of 7 is six times as likely as a sum of 2.

**Check.** Board game players know that 7 comes up far more often than 2 or 12. The
simulation below agrees.

## Simulate It

The "frequency" view says: repeat an experiment many times, and the fraction of times
an event happens gets close to its probability. Let us test it with a die.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Roll a fair die many times and watch the fraction of sixes settle down.
for n_rolls in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_rolls)   # numbers 1 to 6
    fraction_six = np.mean(rolls == 6)
    print(f"{n_rolls:>9,} rolls: fraction of sixes = {fraction_six:.4f}")

print(f"Exact probability:             {1 / 6:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
       10 rolls: fraction of sixes = 0.1000
      100 rolls: fraction of sixes = 0.1300
    1,000 rolls: fraction of sixes = 0.1670
   10,000 rolls: fraction of sixes = 0.1622
  100,000 rolls: fraction of sixes = 0.1664
1,000,000 rolls: fraction of sixes = 0.1664
Exact probability:             0.1667
```

With 10 rolls the fraction is far from $\frac{1}{6}$. With a million rolls it is very
close. This pattern, called the **Law of Large Numbers**, is proved in
[Module 17](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/README.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Getting a probability above 1 or below 0.** If your answer is 1.3 or -0.2, there is
  a mistake in the calculation. Go back and check it.
- **"It either happens or it doesn't, so it is 50%."** Two possible results do not
  mean two equally likely results. Winning the lottery is possible, but its
  probability is far below 0.5.
- **Using the counting rule when outcomes are not equally likely.** The sums of two
  dice (Example 3) are the classic trap. Count equally likely outcomes, such as pairs.
- **Mixing up percent and decimal.** 25% is 0.25, not 25. Inside formulas, always use
  the decimal or the fraction.
- **Expecting short runs to match the probability.** Ten rolls with no 6 is not
  strange. The fraction settles down only after many repeats.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a spam filter does not just say "spam". It outputs a
  probability, such as $0.97$, and you choose a cut-off.
- **Computer science:** randomized algorithms such as quicksort with a random pivot
  are fast with high probability. Hash tables are analyzed with probability.
- **Medicine:** tests and treatments are judged by the probability that they work and
  the probability of side effects.
- **Insurance and finance:** prices are set from the probability of accidents,
  illness or losses.
- **Everyday life:** weather forecasts, sports predictions, and deciding whether a
  risk is worth taking.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You draw one card from a standard deck of 52 cards. What is the
probability that it is an ace?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are 4 aces among 52 equally likely cards:

$$P(\text{ace}) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13} \approx 0.077$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Which of these numbers can be probabilities? $0.3$, $-0.2$, $1.5$, $0$,
$1$, $\frac{7}{8}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A probability must be between 0 and 1, including 0 and 1. So $0.3$, $0$, $1$ and
$\frac{7}{8}$ can be probabilities. The numbers $-0.2$ (negative) and $1.5$ (bigger
than 1) cannot.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You flip two fair coins. What is the probability of getting exactly
one head? A friend says it is $\frac{1}{3}$, because the result is "0, 1 or 2 heads".
Who is right?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The equally likely outcomes are the 4 ordered pairs HH, HT, TH, TT. Exactly one head
happens in HT and TH:

$$P(\text{exactly one head}) = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$

The friend is wrong, because "0, 1 or 2 heads" are not equally likely. "1 head" can
happen in two ways.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A password is made of 3 lowercase letters (a to z), chosen at random.
What is the probability that a single random guess is correct?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each letter has 26 choices, so there are $26 \times 26 \times 26 = 17576$ equally likely
passwords, and 1 of them is right:

$$P(\text{correct}) = \frac{1}{17576} \approx 0.000057$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** You roll two fair dice. Which sum is the most likely, and what is its
probability? Which sums are the least likely?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Count the pairs for each sum out of 36. The sum 7 has the most pairs (6 of them):

$$P(\text{sum} = 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$$

The sums 2 and 12 are the least likely. Each needs one exact pair, $(1, 1)$ or
$(6, 6)$, so each has probability $\frac{1}{36}$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Write code that rolls two dice 100,000 times and estimates
$P(\text{sum} = 7)$ and $P(\text{sum} = 2)$. Compare with the exact answers.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
total = die1 + die2
print(f"P(sum = 7): simulation {np.mean(total == 7):.4f}, exact {6 / 36:.4f}")
print(f"P(sum = 2): simulation {np.mean(total == 2):.4f}, exact {1 / 36:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(sum = 7): simulation 0.1688, exact 0.1667
P(sum = 2): simulation 0.0270, exact 0.0278
```

</details>

## Summary

- Probability measures how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1
  (certain).
- The numbers can come from counting, from long-run frequency, or from careful
  belief. All three follow the same rules.
- When all outcomes are equally likely, count: favorable outcomes divided by total
  outcomes.
- Always check that the outcomes you count really are equally likely.
- Over many repeats, the fraction of times an event happens gets close to its
  probability.

The one formula to remember (for equally likely outcomes):

$$
\boxed{P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of outcomes}}}
$$
