## Warm-Up

**1. (Easy · 1.2 Sample Spaces)** You roll two fair dice, a red one and a blue one. How many
outcomes does the sample space have? In how many of them does the red die show a larger
number than the blue die? What is the probability of that?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\Omega = \lbrace (i, j) : i, j \in \lbrace 1, \dots, 6 \rbrace \rbrace$ has $6 \times 6 = 36$
equally likely outcomes. Of these, 6 are doubles $(i, i)$. The other 30 split evenly into
"red larger" and "blue larger", because swapping the two numbers turns one kind into the
other. So 15 outcomes have red larger:

$$P(\text{red larger}) = \frac{15}{36} = \frac{5}{12} \approx 0.417$$

Check by listing red values: red 2 beats 1 blue value, red 3 beats 2, and so on:
$0 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15$.

</details>

**2. (Easy · 1.3 Events as Subsets)** For two dice, write the events "the sum is 7" and
"the sum is at least 11" as sets, and find their probabilities.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"Sum is 7" is $\lbrace (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) \rbrace$, with probability
$\frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.

"Sum at least 11" is $\lbrace (5,6), (6,5), (6,6) \rbrace$:

$$P(\text{sum} \ge 11) = \frac{3}{36} = \frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083$$

</details>

**3. (Easy · 1.4 Set Operations)** A number is picked at random from
$\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, \dots, 10 \rbrace$. Let $A$ = "multiple of 2" and $B$ = "multiple of
3". Find $A \cup B$, $A \cap B$, $A^c$ and $B \setminus A$, and the probabilities of the
first two.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$A = \lbrace 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace 3, 6, 9 \rbrace$. Then
$A \cup B = \lbrace 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10 \rbrace$, $A \cap B = \lbrace 6 \rbrace$,
$A^c = \lbrace 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 \rbrace$ and $B \setminus A = \lbrace 3, 9 \rbrace$.

$$P(A \cup B) = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7, \qquad P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1$$

Check: $\lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert - \lvert A \cap B \rvert = 5 + 3 - 1 = 7$.

</details>

**4. (Easy · 1.6 Mutually Exclusive Events)** You draw one card from a deck of 52. Which
pairs of events are mutually exclusive? (a) heart and spade; (b) king and face card;
(c) red card and club; (d) ace and spade.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Yes: one card has one suit. (b) No: every king is a face card. (c) Yes: clubs are black.
(d) No: the ace of spades is both.

</details>

**5. (Easy · 1.9 Sizes of Sample Spaces)** Classify each sample space as finite, countably
infinite or uncountable: (a) the number of heads in 10 coin flips; (b) the number of flips
until you first see two heads in a row; (c) the angle at which a spinning wheel stops; (d)
the day of the week a random person was born.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Finite: $\lbrace 0, 1, \dots, 10 \rbrace$, 11 outcomes. (b) Countably infinite:
$\lbrace 2, 3, 4, \dots \rbrace$, with no largest value. (c) Uncountable: any angle in
$[0, 360)$ degrees. (d) Finite: 7 outcomes.

</details>

## Core

**6. (Medium · 1.5 Venn Diagrams)** 120 students choose electives. 50 take Machine Learning
(M), 45 take Databases (D) and 40 take Networks (N). 15 take M and D, 12 take M and N, 10
take D and N, and 5 take all three. How many take none of the three? Exactly one? What is
the probability that a random student takes at least two?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fill the regions from the center outward. All three: 5. M and D only: $15 - 5 = 10$. M and N
only: $12 - 5 = 7$. D and N only: $10 - 5 = 5$. M only: $50 - 10 - 7 - 5 = 28$. D only:
$45 - 10 - 5 - 5 = 25$. N only: $40 - 7 - 5 - 5 = 23$.

Inside the shapes: $5 + 10 + 7 + 5 + 28 + 25 + 23 = 103$, so $120 - 103 = 17$ students take
none. Exactly one: $28 + 25 + 23 = 76$. At least two: $10 + 7 + 5 + 5 = 27$:

$$P(\text{at least two}) = \frac{27}{120} = 0.225$$

Check: $76 + 27 + 17 = 120$.

</details>

**7. (Medium · 1.8 De Morgan's Laws)** A 4-digit PIN (0000 to 9999) is chosen at random.
What is the probability that it contains at least one 0?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $Z_i$ = "digit $i$ is 0". We want $Z_1 \cup Z_2 \cup Z_3 \cup Z_4$. By De Morgan, its
complement is $Z_1^c \cap Z_2^c \cap Z_3^c \cap Z_4^c$, "no digit is 0". Then each digit has 9
choices, so that event has $9^4 = 6561$ of the $10^4 = 10000$ PINs. The event we want has
$10000 - 6561 = 3439$ PINs:

$$P(\text{at least one 0}) = \frac{3439}{10000} = 0.3439$$

</details>

**8. (Medium · 1.7 Partitions)** Roll two fair dice. Use the partition by the value of the
first die to find $P(\text{sum is } 9)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Inside the piece where the first die shows $i$, the second die must be $9 - i$, which must be between 1
and 6. So $i = 1$ and $i = 2$ give nothing (they need 8 and 7), and $i = 3, 4, 5, 6$ give
$(3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3)$:

$$P(\text{sum is } 9) = \frac{0 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1}{36} = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$$

</details>

**9. (Medium · 1.4 Set Operations)** A service runs on three servers. Let $D_i$ = "server $i$
is down", for $i = 1, 2, 3$. Write in set notation: (a) all servers are down; (b) at least
one server is down; (c) at least two servers are down; (d) at most one server is down.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $D_1 \cap D_2 \cap D_3$. (b) $D_1 \cup D_2 \cup D_3$.

(c) Some pair is down together:

$$(D_1 \cap D_2) \cup (D_1 \cap D_3) \cup (D_2 \cap D_3)$$

(d) "At most one down" is the complement of (c). Using De Morgan twice (the complement of a
union is the intersection of complements, and each $(D_i \cap D_j)^c$ is $D_i^c \cup D_j^c$):

$$(D_1^c \cup D_2^c) \cap (D_1^c \cup D_3^c) \cap (D_2^c \cup D_3^c)$$

In words: in every pair of servers, at least one is up.

</details>

**10. (Medium · 1.6 and 1.8)** $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive. Must $A^c$ and $B^c$ also be
mutually exclusive? If not, give a counterexample and say exactly when they are.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Not in general. By De Morgan, $A^c \cap B^c = (A \cup B)^c$. This is empty only when
$A \cup B = \Omega$. Counterexample with one die: $A = \lbrace 1 \rbrace$, $B = \lbrace 2 \rbrace$
are disjoint, but $A^c \cap B^c = \lbrace 3, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$ is not empty.

So $A^c$ and $B^c$ are mutually exclusive exactly when $A \cup B = \Omega$. Together with
$A \cap B = \varnothing$, that means $A$ and $B$ form a partition, so $B = A^c$.

</details>

**11. (Medium · 1.9 Countable Spaces)** Flip a fair coin until the first head. Find the
probability that the number of flips is odd, and the probability that you need at least 4
flips.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From [1.9](1.9-Finite-Countable-and-Uncountable-Spaces.md), $P(\lbrace k \rbrace) = \frac{1}{2^k}$
and $P(\text{even}) = \frac{1}{3}$. The odd and even numbers partition $\Omega$, so:

$$P(\text{odd}) = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$$

At least 4 flips means the first 3 flips are tails, which is 1 of 8 equally likely strings:

$$P(\text{at least 4 flips}) = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125$$

</details>

## Challenge

**12. (Hard · 1.4 and 1.8, proof)** Prove that for any events $A$, $B$, $C$:
$A \setminus (B \cup C) = (A \setminus B) \cap (A \setminus C)$ and
$A \setminus (B \cap C) = (A \setminus B) \cup (A \setminus C)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use $A \setminus X = A \cap X^c$ ([1.4](1.4-Set-Operations-on-Events.md)). For the first law,
use De Morgan, then $A = A \cap A$ and the commutative and associative laws to share the $A$:

$$A \setminus (B \cup C) = A \cap (B \cup C)^c = A \cap (B^c \cap C^c) = (A \cap B^c) \cap (A \cap C^c) = (A \setminus B) \cap (A \setminus C)$$

For the second law, use De Morgan, then the distributive law:

$$A \setminus (B \cap C) = A \cap (B \cap C)^c = A \cap (B^c \cup C^c) = (A \cap B^c) \cup (A \cap C^c) = (A \setminus B) \cup (A \setminus C)$$

In words: "A but not (B or C)" is "A but not B, and A but not C"; "A but not (B and C)" is
"A but not B, or A but not C". These are De Morgan's laws living inside $A$.

</details>

**13. (Hard · 1.3 and 1.6, counting)** A sample space has $n$ outcomes. (a) How many events
contain one given outcome $\omega$? (b) How many ordered pairs $(A, B)$ of events are
mutually exclusive? Check (b) for $n = 1$ by listing.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $\omega$ must be "in". Each of the other $n - 1$ outcomes is free to be in or out, so
there are $2^{n-1}$ such events. That is exactly half of all $2^n$ events.

(b) For a disjoint pair, each outcome has exactly 3 choices: in $A$ only, in $B$ only, or in
neither ("in both" is not allowed). The choices for different outcomes are free, so there
are $3 \times 3 \times \dots \times 3 = 3^n$ pairs.

Check for $n = 1$, $\Omega = \lbrace \omega \rbrace$: the pairs are
$(\varnothing, \varnothing)$, $(\lbrace \omega \rbrace, \varnothing)$ and
$(\varnothing, \lbrace \omega \rbrace)$. That is $3 = 3^1$. The pair
$(\lbrace \omega \rbrace, \lbrace \omega \rbrace)$ is not disjoint.

</details>

**14. (Hard · 1.7 Partitions, proof)** Let $B_1, \dots, B_n$ and $C_1, \dots, C_m$ be two
partitions of $\Omega$. Prove that the non-empty sets among $B_i \cap C_j$ (for all $i$ and
$j$) also form a partition of $\Omega$. What do you get for two dice, when $B_i$ is the event that
the first die shows $i$ and $C_j$ is the event that the second die shows $j$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**They cover $\Omega$.** Take any outcome $\omega$. The first partition puts it in exactly
one $B_i$, and the second in exactly one $C_j$. So $\omega \in B_i \cap C_j$, which is then
non-empty and on our list.

**They do not overlap.** Take two different pairs $(i, j) \ne (k, l)$. Then $i \ne k$ or
$j \ne l$. If $i \ne k$:

$$(B_i \cap C_j) \cap (B_k \cap C_l) \subseteq B_i \cap B_k = \varnothing$$

and the case $j \ne l$ is the same with $C_j \cap C_l = \varnothing$. So the sets are
pairwise disjoint, and they form a partition. It is called the **common refinement** of the
two partitions.

For two dice, $B_i \cap C_j = \lbrace (i, j) \rbrace$: the common refinement is the 36 single
outcomes, the finest partition of all.

</details>

**15. (Hard · 1.9 Countable and Uncountable, proof)** Prove that the set of all **finite**
strings of 0s and 1s is countable. Then use the diagonal argument of 1.9 to explain why the
set of all **subsets** of $\lbrace 1, 2, 3, \dots \rbrace$ is uncountable. What does this
say about computer programs?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Finite strings are countable.** List them by length, and inside one length in
dictionary order: the empty string, 0, 1, 00, 01, 10, 11, 000, ... There are $2^L$ strings
of length $L$, a finite number, so every string of length $L$ appears after at most
$1 + 2 + 4 + \dots + 2^L$ places. Every finite string has a finite place in the list.

**Subsets of the positive integers are uncountable.** Match a subset $S$ with the infinite
0/1 sequence whose digit $k$ is 1 if $k \in S$ and 0 if not. Different subsets give different
sequences, and every sequence comes from a subset. So listing all subsets would list all
infinite 0/1 sequences, which Cantor's diagonal argument
([1.9](1.9-Finite-Countable-and-Uncountable-Spaces.md)) shows is impossible.

**Programs.** Every program is a finite string of bits, so there are only countably many
programs. But there are uncountably many subsets of $\lbrace 1, 2, 3, \dots \rbrace$. So most
subsets have **no** program that decides whether a given number belongs to them. Some
problems cannot be solved by any computer.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**16. (Simulate · 1.4, 1.5 and 1.8)** Roll two dice. Let $A$ = "the sum is even" and
$B$ = "at least one die shows 6". (a) Find $P(A \cup B)$, $P(A \cap B)$ and
$P(\text{neither})$ exactly, by listing the 36 outcomes in code. (b) Estimate the same three
numbers from 1,000,000 simulated rolls. (c) Check De Morgan's first law on every simulated
roll.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By hand: $A$ has 18 outcomes and $B$ has 11. The overlap $A \cap B$ is
$\lbrace (2,6), (4,6), (6,6), (6,2), (6,4) \rbrace$, 5 outcomes. So
$\lvert A \cup B \rvert = 18 + 11 - 5 = 24$ and "neither" has $36 - 24 = 12$:

$$P(A \cup B) = \frac{24}{36} = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad P(A \cap B) = \frac{5}{36} \approx 0.139, \qquad P(\text{neither}) = \frac{12}{36} = \frac{1}{3}$$

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

# Exact answers: list the 36 equally likely outcomes and count.
omega = list(itertools.product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
in_A = lambda w: (w[0] + w[1]) % 2 == 0       # sum is even
in_B = lambda w: 6 in w                       # at least one six
exact = {
    "A or B": sum(in_A(w) or in_B(w) for w in omega) / 36,
    "A and B": sum(in_A(w) and in_B(w) for w in omega) / 36,
    "neither": sum(not in_A(w) and not in_B(w) for w in omega) / 36,
}

# Simulation: the same events as True/False arrays.
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(1_000_000, 2))
A = rolls.sum(axis=1) % 2 == 0
B = (rolls == 6).any(axis=1)
simulated = {"A or B": A | B, "A and B": A & B, "neither": ~A & ~B}

for name in exact:
    print(f"P({name}): simulation {simulated[name].mean():.4f}, exact {exact[name]:.4f}")
print("De Morgan on every trial:", np.array_equal(~(A | B), ~A & ~B))
```

Output:

```text
P(A or B): simulation 0.6669, exact 0.6667
P(A and B): simulation 0.1386, exact 0.1389
P(neither): simulation 0.3331, exact 0.3333
De Morgan on every trial: True
```

</details>

**17. (Simulate · 1.2 and 1.7)** You put 3 keys into a hash table with 5 buckets; each key
goes to a random bucket, and no key affects another. (a) List the sample space in code and
partition it by the number of different buckets used (1, 2 or 3). How big is each piece?
(b) Simulate 1,000,000 insertions and estimate the probability of each piece.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By hand: $\lvert \Omega \rvert = 5^3 = 125$. All keys in one bucket: 5 outcomes. All in
different buckets: $5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$. The rest, exactly two buckets used, is
$125 - 5 - 60 = 60$. So the three probabilities are $\frac{5}{125} = 0.04$,
$\frac{60}{125} = 0.48$ and $\frac{60}{125} = 0.48$.

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

# Exact: list all 5^3 = 125 ways to put 3 keys into 5 buckets.
omega = list(itertools.product(range(5), repeat=3))
used = [len(set(w)) for w in omega]               # buckets used: 1, 2 or 3
print("Size of sample space:", len(omega))
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    print(f"  B_{k} (exactly {k} bucket(s) used): {used.count(k)} outcomes")

# Simulation: pick each key's bucket at random.
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
keys = rng.integers(0, 5, size=(1_000_000, 3))
sorted_keys = np.sort(keys, axis=1)
n_used = 1 + (np.diff(sorted_keys, axis=1) != 0).sum(axis=1)
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    print(f"P(B_{k}): simulation {np.mean(n_used == k):.4f}, "
          f"exact {used.count(k) / 125:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Size of sample space: 125
  B_1 (exactly 1 bucket(s) used): 5 outcomes
  B_2 (exactly 2 bucket(s) used): 60 outcomes
  B_3 (exactly 3 bucket(s) used): 60 outcomes
P(B_1): simulation 0.0400, exact 0.0400
P(B_2): simulation 0.4808, exact 0.4800
P(B_3): simulation 0.4792, exact 0.4800
```

The three pieces add up to $5 + 60 + 60 = 125$, so they really are a partition. A
collision (pieces $B_1$ and $B_2$) happens more than half the time, with only 3 keys.

</details>
