# 1.4 Set Operations on Events

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 01: Sets, Sample Spaces and Events](README.md) / 1.4 Set Operations on Events

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [1.3 Events as Subsets](1.3-Events-as-Subsets.md)

> **Big idea:** OR, AND and NOT of events are union, intersection and complement of sets, so you can build any complicated event from simple ones.

---

## Why It Matters

Real questions combine simple ones: "the email has a link **and** comes from an unknown
sender", "the server is slow **or** returns an error", "the patient does **not** have the
disease". Because events are sets ([1.3](1.3-Events-as-Subsets.md)), these words become set
operations with exact rules. Every probability rule in
[Module 03](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/README.md), such as the addition rule, is written
with them.

## Intuition

Draw one card from a standard deck of 52. Let "heart" be one event (13 cards) and "face
card" be another (jack, queen or king: 12 cards).

- "Heart **or** face card" means at least one of the two is true. Count the 13 hearts, then
  add the face cards that are not hearts: 9 more. That gives 22 cards. The jack, queen and
  king of hearts are counted once, not twice.
- "Heart **and** face card" means both are true: only the jack, queen and king of hearts.
  That is 3 cards.
- "**Not** a heart" means every card except the hearts: $52 - 13 = 39$ cards.

Each new event is again a set of cards. So you can combine it again, as many times as you
like. Three operations are enough to build any event you can describe in words.

## Picture

```text
   outcome ω:            1     2     3     4     5     6
   -------------------------------------------------------
   A = "even"                  x           x           x
   B = "more than 3"                       x     x     x
   -------------------------------------------------------
   A ∪ B   (A or B)            x           x     x     x
   A ∩ B   (A and B)                       x           x
   A^c     (not A)       x           x           x
   A \ B   (A, not B)          x
   B \ A   (B, not A)                            x
```

Each row marks the outcomes of one event; the new rows are built column by column from the
rows for A and B.

## Definition

Let $A$ and $B$ be events in the sample space $\Omega$.

The **union** $A \cup B$ ("A or B") holds the outcomes that are in $A$, in $B$, or in both:

$$
A \cup B = \lbrace \omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ or } \omega \in B \rbrace
$$

The **intersection** $A \cap B$ ("A and B") holds the outcomes that are in both:

$$
A \cap B = \lbrace \omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \in B \rbrace
$$

The **complement** $A^c$ ("not A") holds the outcomes of $\Omega$ that are not in $A$:

$$
A^c = \lbrace \omega \in \Omega : \omega \notin A \rbrace
$$

The **difference** $A \setminus B$ ("A but not B") holds the outcomes in $A$ that are not in
$B$:

$$
A \setminus B = \lbrace \omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \notin B \rbrace = A \cap B^c
$$

**In words:** $A \cup B$ happens when at least one of $A$, $B$ happens. $A \cap B$ happens
when both happen. $A^c$ happens when $A$ does not. $A \setminus B$ happens when $A$ happens
and $B$ does not.

In probability, "or" always means **inclusive or**: "A or B" is true when both are true.
For many events $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ we use the big symbols:

$$
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i = A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \dots \cup A_n, \qquad \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i = A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n
$$

**In words:** the big union happens when **at least one** $A_i$ happens. The big
intersection happens when **all** of them happen.

This table turns English into sets:

| In words | In sets |
|---|---|
| A or B (or both) | $A \cup B$ |
| A and B | $A \cap B$ |
| not A | $A^c$ |
| A but not B | $A \setminus B = A \cap B^c$ |
| at least one of them | $A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n$ |
| all of them | $A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n$ |
| neither A nor B | $A^c \cap B^c$ (see [1.8](1.8-De-Morgans-Laws.md)) |
| exactly one of A, B | $(A \setminus B) \cup (B \setminus A)$ |

The operations follow a few laws. You will use them all the time:

| Law | For union | For intersection |
|---|---|---|
| Commutative | $A \cup B = B \cup A$ | $A \cap B = B \cap A$ |
| Associative | $(A \cup B) \cup C = A \cup (B \cup C)$ | $(A \cap B) \cap C = A \cap (B \cap C)$ |
| Distributive | $A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C)$ | $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$ |
| Identity | $A \cup \varnothing = A$ | $A \cap \Omega = A$ |
| Complement | $A \cup A^c = \Omega$ | $A \cap A^c = \varnothing$ |
| Double complement | $(A^c)^c = A$ | $(A^c)^c = A$ |

## Why It Is True

To prove that two sets are equal, show that an outcome is in the first set **if and only
if** it is in the second ([1.3](1.3-Events-as-Subsets.md), Practice 5). This method is
called **element chasing**. Here it is for the distributive law
$A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega \in A \cap (B \cup C) &\iff \omega \in A \text{ and } (\omega \in B \text{ or } \omega \in C) && \text{(definitions of } \cap \text{ and } \cup \text{)} \\
&\iff (\omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \in B) \text{ or } (\omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \in C) && \text{(logic, checked below)} \\
&\iff \omega \in A \cap B \text{ or } \omega \in A \cap C && \text{(definition of } \cap \text{)} \\
&\iff \omega \in (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C) && \text{(definition of } \cup \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second step is a fact of logic: "and" spreads over "or". Check it by trying every case
for where $\omega$ can be:

```text
  in A  in B  in C | B or C | A and (B or C) | (A and B) or (A and C)
  -----------------+--------+----------------+------------------------
  yes   yes   yes  |  yes   |      yes       |          yes
  yes   yes   no   |  yes   |      yes       |          yes
  yes   no    yes  |  yes   |      yes       |          yes
  yes   no    no   |  no    |      no        |          no
  no    (any) (any)|  ...   |      no        |          no
```

The last two columns agree in every case, so the step is valid. The table above is called a
**truth table**. The other laws are proved the same way. For example, $A \setminus B = A \cap B^c$
holds because the statement ($\omega \in A$ and $\omega \notin B$) is the same as the
statement ($\omega \in A$ and $\omega \in B^c$).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: One Die

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. Let $A = \lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$ (even) and
$B = \lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$ (more than 3). Find $A \cup B$, $A \cap B$, $A^c$,
$A \setminus B$ and $B \setminus A$, and the probability of each.

**Solution.**

1. Union: put together every outcome of $A$ and of $B$, each once:
   $A \cup B = \lbrace 2, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$. So $P(A \cup B) = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$.
2. Intersection: keep the outcomes in both: $A \cap B = \lbrace 4, 6 \rbrace$. So
   $P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$.
3. Complement: the outcomes of $\Omega$ not in $A$: $A^c = \lbrace 1, 3, 5 \rbrace$. So
   $P(A^c) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$.
4. Differences: $A \setminus B = \lbrace 2 \rbrace$ and $B \setminus A = \lbrace 5 \rbrace$.
   Each has probability $\frac{1}{6}$.

**Answer.** $P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{3}$, $P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{3}$, $P(A^c) = \frac{1}{2}$,
$P(A \setminus B) = P(B \setminus A) = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Check.** $P(A) + P(B) = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = 1$, which is bigger than
$P(A \cup B) = \frac{4}{6}$. The difference, $\frac{2}{6}$, is exactly $P(A \cap B)$: the
outcomes 4 and 6 were counted twice. [3.4 Addition Rule](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.4-Addition-Rule.md)
turns this into a general rule.

### Example 2: Hearts and Face Cards

**Problem.** Draw one card from a well-shuffled deck of 52. Let $H$ = "heart" and
$F$ = "face card" (jack, queen, king). Find $P(H \cup F)$, $P(H \cap F)$, $P(H^c)$ and
$P(F \setminus H)$.

**Solution.**

1. $\lvert H \rvert = 13$. There are 3 face cards in each of 4 suits, so
   $\lvert F \rvert = 3 \times 4 = 12$.
2. $H \cap F$ is the jack, queen and king of hearts: $\lvert H \cap F \rvert = 3$.
3. $F \setminus H$ is the face cards of the other three suits:
   $\lvert F \setminus H \rvert = 12 - 3 = 9$.
4. $H \cup F$ is all hearts plus the face cards that are not hearts:
   $\lvert H \cup F \rvert = 13 + 9 = 22$.
5. $\lvert H^c \rvert = 52 - 13 = 39$.
6. Divide each count by 52:

$$
P(H \cup F) = \frac{22}{52} \approx 0.423, \quad P(H \cap F) = \frac{3}{52} \approx 0.058, \quad P(H^c) = \frac{39}{52} = 0.75, \quad P(F \setminus H) = \frac{9}{52} \approx 0.173
$$

**Answer.** About 42% for "heart or face", about 6% for "heart and face", 75% for "not a
heart", and about 17% for "face but not heart".

**Check.** $\lvert H \cup F \rvert = \lvert H \rvert + \lvert F \rvert - \lvert H \cap F \rvert = 13 + 12 - 3 = 22$.
Same answer, counted another way.

### Example 3: Slow or Broken Requests

**Problem.** A web service logged 1,000 requests. Let $S$ = "the request was slow" and
$E$ = "the request returned an error". The log shows 50 slow requests, 30 errors, and 10
requests that were both slow and errors. Write each event in set notation and count it:
"slow but no error", "error but not slow", "something went wrong", "a perfect request".

**Solution.**

1. Slow but no error is $S \setminus E$. Remove the 10 that were also errors: $50 - 10 = 40$.
2. Error but not slow is $E \setminus S$: $30 - 10 = 20$.
3. Something went wrong means slow **or** error: $S \cup E$. Its outcomes split into
   slow only, error only, and both: $40 + 20 + 10 = 70$.
4. A perfect request is the complement $(S \cup E)^c$: $1000 - 70 = 930$.

**Answer.** $\lvert S \setminus E \rvert = 40$, $\lvert E \setminus S \rvert = 20$,
$\lvert S \cup E \rvert = 70$ and $\lvert (S \cup E)^c \rvert = 930$. So about 7% of
requests had a problem and 93% were perfect.

**Check.** The four groups "slow only", "error only", "both" and "perfect" have
$40 + 20 + 10 + 930 = 1000$ requests in total. Every request is in exactly one group.

## Simulate It

In numpy, an event is a True/False array with one entry per run. Then `|` is union, `&` is
intersection and `~` is complement. The code checks Example 1 and also tests the
distributive law on every run.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
roll = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)

# Each event is a True/False array: True on the runs where it happened.
A = roll % 2 == 0          # even: {2, 4, 6}
B = roll > 3               # greater than 3: {4, 5, 6}
C = roll == 1              # {1}

for name, event, exact in [("A or B", A | B, 4 / 6),
                           ("A and B", A & B, 2 / 6),
                           ("not A", ~A, 3 / 6),
                           ("A but not B", A & ~B, 1 / 6)]:
    print(f"P({name}): simulation {event.mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

# The distributive law holds on every single run, not just on average.
left = A & (B | C)
right = (A & B) | (A & C)
print("Distributive law true on every run:", np.array_equal(left, right))
```

Output:

```text
P(A or B): simulation 0.6677, exact 0.6667
P(A and B): simulation 0.3328, exact 0.3333
P(not A): simulation 0.5004, exact 0.5000
P(A but not B): simulation 0.1669, exact 0.1667
Distributive law true on every run: True
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading "or" as "exactly one".** In probability, $A \cup B$ includes the outcomes in
  both. "Exactly one" is $(A \setminus B) \cup (B \setminus A)$.
- **Adding counts to get a union.** $\lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert$ counts the overlap
  $A \cap B$ twice. Count the overlap once, as in Example 2.
- **Mixing up the symbols.** $\cup$ looks like a cup that collects everything: the union is
  the bigger set. $\cap$ is the smaller set, the part the two events share.
- **Taking the complement outside $\Omega$.** For a die, "not even" is $\lbrace 1, 3, 5 \rbrace$,
  not "every odd number". The complement only contains outcomes of $\Omega$.
- **Thinking difference is symmetric.** $A \setminus B$ and $B \setminus A$ are different
  sets. In Example 1 they are $\lbrace 2 \rbrace$ and $\lbrace 5 \rbrace$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a **true positive** is the event "the model says positive
  **and** the case is really positive", an intersection. Precision and recall are built
  from such intersections.
- **Computer science:** Boolean logic and bit masks: OR, AND and NOT on bits are union,
  intersection and complement on the set of positions that hold a 1.
- **Databases and search:** SQL has `UNION`, `INTERSECT` and `EXCEPT`, and a search engine
  combines word lists with AND, OR and NOT.
- **Reliability:** a chain of two parts fails if part 1 fails **or** part 2 fails, a union
  of failure events.
- **Medicine:** "has the symptom and tests positive" is an intersection; doctors reason with
  such combined events every day.

**1. (Easy)** For one die roll, let $A = \lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$ and
$B = \lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$. Find $A \cup B$, $A \cap B$, $A^c$ and $B \setminus A$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$A \cup B = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 \rbrace$, $A \cap B = \lbrace 2 \rbrace$,
$A^c = \lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$ and $B \setminus A = \lbrace 4, 6 \rbrace$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Write in set notation, using events $A$, $B$, $C$: (a) $A$ happens but $B$
does not; (b) neither $A$ nor $B$ happens; (c) at least one of $A$, $B$, $C$ happens;
(d) all three happen.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $A \cap B^c$, which is $A \setminus B$. (b) $A^c \cap B^c$. (c) $A \cup B \cup C$.
(d) $A \cap B \cap C$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Draw one card from a deck of 52. Let $R$ = "red card" and $K$ = "ace".
Find $P(R \cup K)$, $P(R \cap K)$ and $P(R^c \cap K)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are 26 red cards and 4 aces, 2 of them red. So $R \cap K$ has 2 cards, and
$R^c \cap K$ (the black aces) has 2 cards. The union is the 26 red cards plus the 2 black
aces: 28 cards.

$$P(R \cup K) = \frac{28}{52} = \frac{7}{13} \approx 0.538, \qquad P(R \cap K) = P(R^c \cap K) = \frac{2}{52} = \frac{1}{26} \approx 0.038$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Prove the other distributive law:
$A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Chase an outcome $\omega$ through both sides. By the definitions:

$$\omega \in A \cup (B \cap C) \iff \omega \in A \text{ or } (\omega \in B \text{ and } \omega \in C)$$

$$\omega \in (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C) \iff (\omega \in A \text{ or } \omega \in B) \text{ and } (\omega \in A \text{ or } \omega \in C)$$

Now check the two cases. If $\omega \in A$, both statements are true. If $\omega \notin A$,
both statements reduce to the same thing: $\omega \in B$ and $\omega \in C$. So the two statements are always both true or both
false, and the sets are equal.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Write the event "exactly one of $A$, $B$, $C$ happens" in set notation. Then
check it for one die roll with $A = \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$, $B = \lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace$ and
$C = \lbrace 3, 4, 5 \rbrace$, and find its probability.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Exactly one happens when $A$ happens alone, or $B$ alone, or $C$ alone:

$$(A \cap B^c \cap C^c) \cup (A^c \cap B \cap C^c) \cup (A^c \cap B^c \cap C)$$

Check each outcome. 1 is only in $A$. 2 is in $A$ and $B$. 3 is in $B$ and $C$. 4 and 5 are
only in $C$. 6 is in none. So the event is $\lbrace 1, 4, 5 \rbrace$:

$$P(\text{exactly one}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the answer to Problem 5 with 100,000 die rolls, using `&`, `|` and
`~`.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000
roll = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
A = np.isin(roll, [1, 2])
B = np.isin(roll, [2, 3])
C = np.isin(roll, [3, 4, 5])
exactly_one = (A & ~B & ~C) | (~A & B & ~C) | (~A & ~B & C)
print(f"Simulation: {exactly_one.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {3 / 6:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.5021
Exact:      0.5000
```

</details>

## Summary

- Union $A \cup B$ is "A or B" (inclusive), intersection $A \cap B$ is "A and B", and the
  complement $A^c$ is "not A".
- The difference $A \setminus B = A \cap B^c$ is "A but not B".
- Combined events are again events, so the operations can be repeated to describe anything.
- Prove set laws by element chasing: an outcome is in the left side if and only if it is
  in the right side.
- In code, events are Boolean arrays and the operations are `|`, `&` and `~`.

The one formula to remember (the distributive law, used again in [1.7 Partitions](1.7-Partitions.md)):

$$
\boxed{A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)}
$$
