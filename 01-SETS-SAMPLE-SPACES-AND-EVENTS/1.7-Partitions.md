# 1.7 Partitions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 01: Sets, Sample Spaces and Events](README.md) / 1.7 Partitions

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [1.6 Mutually Exclusive Events](1.6-Mutually-Exclusive-Events.md)

> **Big idea:** A partition cuts the sample space into pieces that do not overlap and together cover everything, so any event can be counted piece by piece.

---

## Why It Matters

Hard events often become easy when you split them into cases: "if the first die is 1 ...,
if it is 2 ...". A **partition** is the exact rule for splitting into cases without missing
anything and without counting anything twice. It is the idea behind the law of total
probability ([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md))
and Bayes' theorem ([4.4](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)),
two of the most used tools in this book.

## Intuition

A university has 1,200 students, and every student is in exactly one year: first, second,
third or fourth. The four year groups do not overlap, and together they are the whole
university. That is a partition.

Now you want to know how many students failed the probability exam. You do not need one big
list. Ask each year group: 40 failed in first year, 25 in second, 10 in third and 5 in
fourth. The answer is $40 + 25 + 10 + 5 = 80$. Adding is safe, because no student is in two
groups, and complete, because no student is outside every group.

The same works with outcomes. Cut $\Omega$ into pieces, look at your event inside each piece,
and add the pieces. Choose the cut so that each piece is easy.

## Picture

```text
   Ω
  +---------------+---------------+---------------+---------------+
  |      B1       |      B2       |      B3       |      B4       |
  |               |               |               |               |
  |   +-----------+---------------+---------------+-----------+   |
  |   |  A ∩ B1   |    A ∩ B2     |    A ∩ B3     |  A ∩ B4   |   |
  |   +-----------+---------------+---------------+-----------+   |
  |               |               |               |               |
  +---------------+---------------+---------------+---------------+
      <------------------------- event A --------------------->
```

The pieces B1 to B4 cut Ω into strips; they also cut the event A into the pieces A ∩ B1 to
A ∩ B4.

## Definition

Events $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_n$ form a **partition** of $\Omega$ when both of these hold:

$$
B_i \cap B_j = \varnothing \ \text{ for all } i \ne j \qquad \text{and} \qquad B_1 \cup B_2 \cup \dots \cup B_n = \Omega
$$

**In words:** the pieces are pairwise disjoint (no outcome is in two pieces), and together
they cover the sample space (no outcome is left out). So every outcome is in **exactly one**
piece.

We usually also ask that each piece is non-empty, since an empty piece adds nothing. A
partition may also have infinitely many pieces $B_1, B_2, B_3, \dots$, as long as they can
be listed one after another (see [1.9](1.9-Finite-Countable-and-Uncountable-Spaces.md)).

The main use of a partition is to cut **another** event $A$ into pieces:

$$
A = (A \cap B_1) \cup (A \cap B_2) \cup \dots \cup (A \cap B_n)
$$

and these pieces are pairwise disjoint, so their probabilities add:

$$
P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \cap B_i)
$$

**In words:** to find the probability of $A$, find the probability that $A$ happens inside piece $i$,
for every piece, and add them up.

A good way to find a partition: ask a question about the outcome that has exactly one answer,
such as "what does the first die show?" or "how many heads?". The outcomes with the same
answer form one piece.

## Why It Is True

**The pieces of $A$ make up $A$.** Start from the distributive law of
[1.4](1.4-Set-Operations-on-Events.md), which works for any number of sets in the same way:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= A \cap \Omega && \text{(}A \text{ is inside } \Omega \text{)} \\
&= A \cap (B_1 \cup B_2 \cup \dots \cup B_n) && \text{(the pieces cover } \Omega \text{)} \\
&= (A \cap B_1) \cup (A \cap B_2) \cup \dots \cup (A \cap B_n) && \text{(distributive law)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The pieces of $A$ do not overlap.** Take $i \ne j$. Any outcome in both $A \cap B_i$ and
$A \cap B_j$ would be in $B_i \cap B_j$, which is empty. So

$$
(A \cap B_i) \cap (A \cap B_j) \subseteq B_i \cap B_j = \varnothing
$$

**So the counts add.** The pieces of $A$ are pairwise disjoint, and
[1.6](1.6-Mutually-Exclusive-Events.md) showed that sizes (and, with equally likely
outcomes, probabilities) of pairwise disjoint events add up. This gives
$P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \cap B_i)$. From [Module 03](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/README.md) on,
this holds for every probability, because adding over disjoint events is an axiom.

**Why a question gives a partition.** If each outcome gives exactly one answer, then no
outcome is in two pieces (it would need two answers), and no outcome is outside all pieces
(it would need no answer).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Sum of Two Dice by Cases

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice. Find $P(\text{sum is } 8)$ by splitting on the first die.

**Solution.**

1. Let $B_i$ be the event that the first die shows $i$, for $i = 1, \dots, 6$. Every pair has exactly one
   first value, so $B_1, \dots, B_6$ is a partition of the 36 outcomes.
2. Let $A$ = "the sum is 8". Inside $B_i$, the second die must show $8 - i$, and that must
   be a number from 1 to 6.
3. Go through the pieces:
   $B_1$: needs 7, impossible, so $A \cap B_1 = \varnothing$.
   $B_2$: $(2,6)$. $B_3$: $(3,5)$. $B_4$: $(4,4)$. $B_5$: $(5,3)$. $B_6$: $(6,2)$.
4. Add the sizes of the pieces and divide by 36:

$$
P(A) = \frac{0 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1}{36} = \frac{5}{36} \approx 0.139
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{sum is } 8) = \frac{5}{36}$.

**Check.** The same method for a sum of 7 gives one pair in **every** piece, so
$\frac{6}{36}$, which matches [1.1](1.1-What-is-Probability.md). A sum of 8 loses one case,
because the first die cannot be 1.

### Example 2: Slow Requests by Region

**Problem.** A service handled 10,000 requests from three regions. Asia sent 5,000 requests,
and 400 of them were slow. Europe sent 3,000, and 150 were slow. America sent 2,000, and 50
were slow. What fraction of all requests were slow?

**Solution.**

1. Every request comes from exactly one region, so "Asia", "Europe", "America" is a
   partition of the 10,000 requests. Check: $5000 + 3000 + 2000 = 10000$.
2. Let $A$ = "the request was slow". Its pieces have sizes 400, 150 and 50.
3. Add the pieces: $\lvert A \rvert = 400 + 150 + 50 = 600$.
4. Divide by the total:

$$
P(\text{slow}) = \frac{600}{10000} = 0.06
$$

**Answer.** 6% of the requests were slow.

**Check.** Inside each region the slow fraction is $\frac{400}{5000} = 0.08$,
$\frac{150}{3000} = 0.05$ and $\frac{50}{2000} = 0.025$. The overall $0.06$ lies between the
smallest and the largest, as an average of the regions should. Writing $0.06$ as a weighted
average of these three fractions is exactly the law of total probability of
[4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md).

### Example 3: Is It a Partition

**Problem.** For one die roll, which of these lists are partitions of
$\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$?

- (a) $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 3, 4 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 5, 6 \rbrace$
- (b) $\lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 3, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$
- (c) $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$
- (d) $\lbrace 1, 4 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 2, 5 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 3, 6 \rbrace$

**Solution.**

1. (a): no overlaps, and every face appears. **Partition.**
2. (b): the face 3 is in both pieces. Not disjoint. **Not a partition.**
3. (c): the face 3 is in no piece. Does not cover $\Omega$. **Not a partition.**
4. (d): no overlaps, every face appears. **Partition.** It comes from the question "what is
   the remainder when the roll is divided by 3?": remainder 1 gives $\lbrace 1, 4 \rbrace$,
   remainder 2 gives $\lbrace 2, 5 \rbrace$, remainder 0 gives $\lbrace 3, 6 \rbrace$.

**Answer.** (a) and (d) are partitions; (b) overlaps and (c) has a gap.

**Check.** For a partition the piece sizes must add up to $\lvert \Omega \rvert = 6$. For
(a) and (d): $2 + 2 + 2 = 6$. For (b): $3 + 4 = 7$, too many. For (c): $2 + 3 = 5$, too few.

## Simulate It

The code rolls two dice 360,000 times, cuts the event "sum is 8" into the six pieces of
Example 1, and checks that the pieces add up to the whole event.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 360_000
die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
A = die1 + die2 == 8                      # the event "sum is 8"

# Cut A into the pieces "A and B_i", where B_i = "first die shows i".
total = 0
for i in range(1, 7):
    piece = A & (die1 == i)
    exact = (0 if i == 1 else 1) / 36     # only (i, 8 - i), and 8 - 1 = 7 is impossible
    print(f"P(A and B_{i}): simulation {piece.mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
    total += piece.sum()

print(f"The pieces happened {total} times in all; A happened {A.sum()} times.")
print(f"P(A): simulation {A.mean():.4f}, exact {5 / 36:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A and B_1): simulation 0.0000, exact 0.0000
P(A and B_2): simulation 0.0277, exact 0.0278
P(A and B_3): simulation 0.0277, exact 0.0278
P(A and B_4): simulation 0.0278, exact 0.0278
P(A and B_5): simulation 0.0281, exact 0.0278
P(A and B_6): simulation 0.0275, exact 0.0278
The pieces happened 49980 times in all; A happened 49980 times.
P(A): simulation 0.1388, exact 0.1389
```

The pieces add up to the event **exactly** (49,980 both times), because every run with a sum
of 8 has exactly one value of the first die.

## Common Mistakes

- **Overlapping pieces.** Splitting students into "takes math" and "takes physics" is not a
  partition if some take both. Those students are counted twice.
- **Pieces with a gap.** Splitting by "under 18" and "over 18" forgets the people who are
  exactly 18. Check that every outcome has a piece.
- **Thinking pieces must be the same size or equally likely.** Asia, Europe and America in
  Example 2 have very different sizes. A partition only needs "no overlap, no gap".
- **Adding the pieces $B_i$ instead of the pieces $A \cap B_i$.** The sizes of the $B_i$
  always add up to $\lvert \Omega \rvert$. To get $A$, add only the parts of $A$ inside them.
- **Picking an unhelpful cut.** Any partition gives the right answer, but a good one makes
  every piece easy. Split on the thing that, once known, makes the problem simple.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a train / validation / test split, and the $k$ folds of
  cross-validation, are partitions of a data set. A decision tree partitions the input space
  into leaves.
- **Computer science:** hash buckets partition the keys; database sharding partitions the
  rows; divide-and-conquer algorithms partition the input.
- **Probability itself:** the law of total probability
  ([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)) and
  Bayes' theorem ([4.4](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)) both
  start from a partition into causes or cases.
- **Statistics and surveys:** stratified sampling splits a population into groups (by
  region, age, ...) and samples each group.
- **Everyday life:** a budget splits your money into rent, food, travel and savings, with
  every taka in exactly one category.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Is $\lbrace 1, 3, 5 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 2, 4 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 6 \rbrace$ a
partition of the sample space of one die roll?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Yes. No face is in two pieces, and every face 1 to 6 is in some piece. The sizes add up:
$3 + 2 + 1 = 6$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You flip a coin 3 times. Write the partition of $\Omega$ given by the question
"how many heads?". How many outcomes are in each piece?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$B_0 = \lbrace TTT \rbrace$, $B_1 = \lbrace HTT, THT, TTH \rbrace$,
$B_2 = \lbrace HHT, HTH, THH \rbrace$, $B_3 = \lbrace HHH \rbrace$. The sizes are 1, 3, 3, 1,
and they add up to $1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 = \lvert \Omega \rvert$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Roll two fair dice. Use the partition by the first die to find
$P(\text{sum is } 6)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Inside $B_i$ the second die must show $6 - i$, which must be at least 1. So $B_1$ to $B_5$
each contain one pair, $(1,5), (2,4), (3,3), (4,2), (5,1)$, and $B_6$ contains none:

$$P(\text{sum is } 6) = \frac{1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0}{36} = \frac{5}{36} \approx 0.139$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $A$ and $B$ be any two events. Explain why $A$, $A^c$ is a partition of
$\Omega$ (when $A$ is neither $\varnothing$ nor $\Omega$). Then explain why the four sets
$A \cap B$, $A \cap B^c$, $A^c \cap B$, $A^c \cap B^c$ also cover $\Omega$ without overlap.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every outcome is either in $A$ or not in $A$, and never both. So $A \cap A^c = \varnothing$
and $A \cup A^c = \Omega$. For the four sets: each outcome gives one answer to "in $A$?" and
one answer to "in $B$?", so it lands in exactly one of the four sets. These are the four
regions of a two-event Venn diagram ([1.5](1.5-Venn-Diagrams.md)). If one of them is empty,
drop it, and the rest is a partition.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** List all partitions of $\Omega = \lbrace a, b, c \rbrace$. Then count the
partitions of $\lbrace a, b, c, d \rbrace$ by grouping them by their piece sizes.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $\lbrace a, b, c \rbrace$ there are 5:
$\lbrace abc \rbrace$; $\lbrace a \rbrace \lbrace bc \rbrace$; $\lbrace b \rbrace \lbrace ac \rbrace$;
$\lbrace c \rbrace \lbrace ab \rbrace$; $\lbrace a \rbrace \lbrace b \rbrace \lbrace c \rbrace$.

For four elements, group by piece sizes:

- one piece of 4: 1 partition;
- sizes 3 + 1: choose the single element, 4 ways;
- sizes 2 + 2: the piece with $a$ needs one partner from $b, c, d$, 3 ways;
- sizes 2 + 1 + 1: choose the pair, 6 ways ($ab, ac, ad, bc, bd, cd$);
- sizes 1 + 1 + 1 + 1: 1 partition.

The total is $1 + 4 + 3 + 6 + 1 = 15$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate 1,000,000 rolls of two dice. Estimate each piece
$P(A \cap B_i)$ of Problem 3, and check that they add up to $P(A)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
A = die1 + die2 == 6
for i in range(1, 7):
    print(f"P(A and B_{i}) = {np.mean(A & (die1 == i)):.4f}")
print(f"P(A): simulation {A.mean():.4f}, exact {5 / 36:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A and B_1) = 0.0279
P(A and B_2) = 0.0278
P(A and B_3) = 0.0277
P(A and B_4) = 0.0279
P(A and B_5) = 0.0277
P(A and B_6) = 0.0000
P(A): simulation 0.1391, exact 0.1389
```

Each of the first five pieces is close to $\frac{1}{36} \approx 0.0278$, and the last is 0.

</details>

## Summary

- A partition is a list of events that are pairwise disjoint and together cover $\Omega$.
  Every outcome is in exactly one piece.
- Any question with exactly one answer per outcome gives a partition.
- A partition cuts any event $A$ into disjoint pieces $A \cap B_i$, which add up to $A$.
- Choose the partition that makes each piece easy to count.
- This "split into cases and add" idea becomes the law of total probability in Module 04.

The one formula to remember (for a partition $B_1, \dots, B_n$):

$$
\boxed{P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \cap B_i)}
$$
