# 10.2 Joint CDF

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 10: Joint Distributions](README.md) / 10.2 Joint CDF

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [10.1 Joint PMF and PDF](10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md)

> **Big idea:** The joint CDF $F(x, y)$ is the probability that the random point $(X, Y)$ lands below and to the left of $(x, y)$, and it works the same way for discrete and continuous variables.

---

## Why It Matters

For one variable, the CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ was the one tool that worked for every
kind of random variable ([5.4 Cumulative Distribution Function](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.4-Cumulative-Distribution-Function.md)).
The **joint CDF** does the same job for two variables. It answers questions like
"are both jobs finished by time $t$?" directly. It also gives the probability of
any rectangle, and it hands you the joint density by differentiation.

## Intuition

A computer runs two jobs in parallel. Job A finishes at a random time $X$ and job B
at a random time $Y$ (in seconds). The result is ready only when **both** are done.
So the question "is the result ready by 1 second?" is the question
"is $X \le 1$ **and** $Y \le 1$?".

Picture the pair $(X, Y)$ as a point on a map. The event $\lbrace X \le 1, Y \le 1 \rbrace$
is the whole area to the **south-west** of the point $(1, 1)$: everything at or
to the left of $x = 1$ and at or below $y = 1$. The joint CDF measures exactly
this kind of south-west region, for every corner $(x, y)$.

If you know the probability of every south-west region, you can cut out any
rectangle by adding and subtracting four of them. That is why the joint CDF holds
all the information about $(X, Y)$.

## Picture

```text
   y                                     y
   ^                                     ^
   |###########|                       d |....+-----------+
   |###########|                         |    |  target   |
   |###########|                         |    | rectangle |
   |###########|                       c |....+-----------+
   |#####F(x,y)|                         |    :           :
   +-----------o (x, y)                  |    :           :
   |                                     +----+-----------+-----> x
   |                                          a           b
   +---------------------> x
                                    target = F(b,d) - F(a,d)
   F(x, y) = P(X <= x, Y <= y)                   - F(b,c) + F(a,c)
   = probability of the shaded
     south-west region
```

Left: the joint CDF is the probability of a south-west region. Right: any rectangle is built from four such regions.

## Definition

The **joint cumulative distribution function** (joint CDF) of $X$ and $Y$ is:

$$
F_{X,Y}(x, y) = P(X \le x, Y \le y)
$$

for every pair of real numbers $x$ and $y$.

**In words:** $F(x, y)$ is the probability that $X$ is at most $x$ **and** $Y$ is at
most $y$ at the same time.

How to compute it from the joint PMF or the joint PDF of
[10.1 Joint PMF and PDF](10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md):

$$
F(x, y) = \sum_{s \le x} \sum_{t \le y} p(s, t) \qquad \text{or} \qquad F(x, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} \int_{-\infty}^{y} f(s, t) \thinspace dt \thinspace ds
$$

Every joint CDF has these properties:

1. **Limits.** $F(x, y) \to 0$ when $x \to -\infty$ or $y \to -\infty$, and
   $F(x, y) \to 1$ when both $x \to \infty$ and $y \to \infty$.
2. **Never decreasing.** $F$ never goes down when $x$ or $y$ grows.
3. **Marginal CDFs.** Let one variable go to infinity: $F_X(x) = \lim_{y \to \infty} F(x, y)$
   and $F_Y(y) = \lim_{x \to \infty} F(x, y)$.
4. **Rectangle rule.** For $a \lt b$ and $c \lt d$:

$$
P(a \lt X \le b, \ c \lt Y \le d) = F(b, d) - F(a, d) - F(b, c) + F(a, c)
$$

**In words:** the big south-west region, minus the two strips you do not want, plus
the corner you subtracted twice.

5. **Density from the CDF.** If $X$ and $Y$ are jointly continuous, the joint PDF is
   the mixed second derivative:

$$
f(x, y) = \frac{\partial^2 F(x, y)}{\partial x \thinspace \partial y}
$$

Here $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ is a **partial derivative**: differentiate with
respect to $x$ while treating $y$ as a constant. Then do the same with respect to $y$.

## Why It Is True

**The rectangle rule.** Let $R = \lbrace a \lt X \le b, \ c \lt Y \le d \rbrace$ be the
rectangle. Look at the big region $\lbrace X \le b, Y \le d \rbrace$. A point in it is
either in $R$, or in the left strip $L = \lbrace X \le a, Y \le d \rbrace$, or in the
bottom strip $D = \lbrace X \le b, Y \le c \rbrace$. The two strips overlap in the
corner $L \cap D = \lbrace X \le a, Y \le c \rbrace$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(R) &= P(X \le b, Y \le d) - P(L \cup D) && \text{(} R \text{ and } L \cup D \text{ are disjoint, and fill the big region)} \\
&= F(b, d) - \big[ P(L) + P(D) - P(L \cap D) \big] && \text{(inclusion-exclusion)} \\
&= F(b, d) - F(a, d) - F(b, c) + F(a, c) && \text{(each piece is a CDF value)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $P(R) \ge 0$, every joint CDF must give a non-negative answer here. This is a
real extra condition: Practice 5 shows a function that has properties 1 to 3 but
fails the rectangle rule.

**The density.** Write $F$ as a double integral and differentiate twice. The
**fundamental theorem of calculus** says that the derivative of
$\int_{-\infty}^{x} g(s) \thinspace ds$ with respect to $x$ is $g(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \int_{-\infty}^{x} \left( \int_{-\infty}^{y} f(s, t) \thinspace dt \right) ds = \int_{-\infty}^{y} f(x, t) \thinspace dt && \text{(FTC in } x \text{)} \\
\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial y \thinspace \partial x} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \int_{-\infty}^{y} f(x, t) \thinspace dt = f(x, y) && \text{(FTC in } y \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For the nice densities in this book, the order of the two derivatives does not matter.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The CDF of a Table

**Problem.** Use the shop table ($X$ = items in the cart, $Y = 1$ if the customer
pays):

| | $Y = 0$ | $Y = 1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $X = 1$ | 0.20 | 0.10 |
| $X = 2$ | 0.15 | 0.20 |
| $X = 3$ | 0.05 | 0.30 |

Find $F(2, 0)$, $F(2, 1)$ and $F(2.5, 0.7)$.

**Solution.**

1. $F(2, 0) = P(X \le 2, Y \le 0)$. Add the cells with $x \le 2$ and $y = 0$:

$$
F(2, 0) = p(1, 0) + p(2, 0) = 0.20 + 0.15 = 0.35
$$

2. $F(2, 1)$ adds every cell with $x \le 2$ and $y \le 1$, so both columns:

$$
F(2, 1) = 0.20 + 0.10 + 0.15 + 0.20 = 0.65
$$

3. For $F(2.5, 0.7)$: the values of $X$ that are at most 2.5 are 1 and 2. The only
   value of $Y$ that is at most 0.7 is 0. So it is the same set of cells as in step 1:

$$
F(2.5, 0.7) = F(2, 0) = 0.35
$$

**Answer.** $F(2, 0) = 0.35$, $F(2, 1) = 0.65$, $F(2.5, 0.7) = 0.35$.

**Check.** For discrete variables the joint CDF is a "staircase": it is flat between
the possible values and jumps only at them. Step 3 shows this.

### Example 2: The CDF of a Density

**Problem.** Let $f(x, y) = x + y$ on the unit square (from
[10.1](10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md)). Find $F(x, y)$ for $0 \le x, y \le 1$. Use it to
find $P(X \gt \frac{1}{2}, Y \gt \frac{1}{2})$.

**Solution.**

1. Integrate the density over the rectangle from $(0, 0)$ to $(x, y)$. Inner integral
   over $t$:

$$
\int_0^y (s + t) \thinspace dt = sy + \frac{y^2}{2}
$$

2. Outer integral over $s$:

$$
F(x, y) = \int_0^x \left( sy + \frac{y^2}{2} \right) ds = \frac{x^2 y}{2} + \frac{x y^2}{2} = \frac{xy(x + y)}{2}
$$

   Check: $F(1, 1) = \frac{1 \cdot 1 \cdot 2}{2} = 1$. Good.
3. The region $X \gt \frac{1}{2}, Y \gt \frac{1}{2}$ is the rectangle with $a = c = \frac{1}{2}$
   and $b = d = 1$. Compute the four CDF values:

$$
F(1, 1) = 1, \quad F\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1\right) = \frac{\frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{3}{2}}{2} = 0.375, \quad F\left(1, \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 0.375, \quad F\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\frac{1}{4} \cdot 1}{2} = 0.125
$$

4. Use the rectangle rule:

$$
P\left(X \gt \tfrac{1}{2}, Y \gt \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 1 - 0.375 - 0.375 + 0.125 = 0.375
$$

**Answer.** $F(x, y) = \frac{xy(x + y)}{2}$ on the square, and the top-right quarter
has probability $0.375$.

**Check.** Differentiate back: $\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = \frac{2xy + y^2}{2}$,
then $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ of that is $\frac{2x + 2y}{2} = x + y$. We get $f$
again. Also, $0.375 \gt 0.25$ makes sense: the density is largest in the top-right corner.

### Example 3: Two Parallel Jobs

**Problem.** Two jobs run in parallel. Their finishing times (in seconds) have joint CDF

$$
F(x, y) = (1 - e^{-x})(1 - e^{-2y}) \quad \text{for } x, y \ge 0
$$

and $F = 0$ otherwise. (a) What is the probability that the result is ready by 1
second? (b) Find the marginal CDF of $X$. (c) Find the joint density.

**Solution.**

1. (a) "Ready by 1 second" means both are done: $X \le 1$ and $Y \le 1$.

$$
F(1, 1) = (1 - e^{-1})(1 - e^{-2}) \approx 0.6321 \times 0.8647 \approx 0.5466
$$

2. (b) Let $y \to \infty$. Then $e^{-2y} \to 0$, so

$$
F_X(x) = \lim_{y \to \infty} F(x, y) = 1 - e^{-x}, \quad x \ge 0
$$

   This is the CDF of $\text{Exp}(1)$, the exponential distribution with rate 1 (mean 1 second).
3. (c) Differentiate in $x$, then in $y$:

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = e^{-x} (1 - e^{-2y}), \qquad f(x, y) = \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x \thinspace \partial y} = e^{-x} \cdot 2e^{-2y} = 2e^{-x - 2y}
$$

**Answer.** (a) about $0.547$, so about 45% of the time the result is **not** ready
after 1 second. (b) $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$. (c) $f(x, y) = 2e^{-x-2y}$ for $x, y \ge 0$.

**Check.** $F(1, 1)$ is smaller than both $P(X \le 1) \approx 0.632$ and
$P(Y \le 1) \approx 0.865$, as it must be: "both" is harder than "one". In general
$P(\max(X, Y) \le t) = F(t, t)$, an idea used again in
[13.6 Max and Min](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.6-Max-and-Min.md).

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 3. Here $F(x, y)$ is a product of the two marginal CDFs, so
we can draw $X$ and $Y$ separately (that is independence, see
[10.5 Independence of Random Variables](10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Finishing times of two parallel jobs, with joint CDF
# F(x, y) = (1 - e^(-x)) (1 - e^(-2y)).
x = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=n_trials)   # rate 1, mean 1 second
y = rng.exponential(scale=0.5, size=n_trials)   # rate 2, mean 0.5 second

def F(a, b):
    return (1 - np.exp(-a)) * (1 - np.exp(-2 * b))

# Both jobs done by 1 second: F(1, 1).
both_done = np.mean((x <= 1) & (y <= 1))
print(f"F(1, 1):   simulation {both_done:.4f}, exact {F(1, 1):.4f}")

# Rectangle 0.5 < X <= 1, 0.5 < Y <= 1 by the rectangle rule.
in_rect = np.mean((x > 0.5) & (x <= 1) & (y > 0.5) & (y <= 1))
rect = F(1, 1) - F(0.5, 1) - F(1, 0.5) + F(0.5, 0.5)
print(f"Rectangle: simulation {in_rect:.4f}, exact {rect:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
F(1, 1):   simulation 0.5469, exact 0.5466
Rectangle: simulation 0.0553, exact 0.0555
```

Both the CDF value and the rectangle rule agree with the simulation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using $F(b, d) - F(a, c)$ for a rectangle.** That is the one-variable habit, and
  it is wrong in two dimensions. It removes only the corner, not the two strips. Use
  all four terms.
- **Reading the comma as "or".** $F(x, y)$ is the probability that $X \le x$ **and** $Y \le y$. The "or"
  event $\lbrace X \le x \rbrace \cup \lbrace Y \le y \rbrace$ has probability
  $F_X(x) + F_Y(y) - F(x, y)$.
- **Mixing up strict and non-strict inequalities for discrete variables.** For a
  table, $P(X \lt 2)$ and $P(X \le 2)$ differ. The rectangle rule uses $a \lt X \le b$.
- **Thinking properties 1 to 3 are enough.** A function can have the right limits and
  never decrease, and still not be a joint CDF. The rectangle rule must give
  non-negative answers too (Practice 5).

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** the time until a parallel job or a distributed query is done
  is the maximum of several times, and $P(\max \le t)$ is a joint CDF value.
- **Reliability engineering:** $F(t, t)$ is the probability that two parts have both
  failed by time $t$.
- **Finance:** the chance that two stocks both fall below their thresholds, a
  key input when banks measure the risk of joint losses.
- **Statistics:** **copulas** describe dependence by writing a joint CDF in terms of
  the two marginal CDFs.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In the shop table, find $F(1, 1)$ and $F(3, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$F(1, 1) = p(1, 0) + p(1, 1) = 0.20 + 0.10 = 0.30$$

$F(3, 1)$ includes every cell, so $F(3, 1) = 1$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A point is uniform on the unit square, so $F(x, y) = xy$ for
$0 \le x, y \le 1$. Find $P(X \le 0.5, Y \le 0.4)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \le 0.5, Y \le 0.4) = F(0.5, 0.4) = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** With $F(x, y) = \frac{xy(x + y)}{2}$ from Example 2, find
$P(X \le \frac{1}{2}, Y \gt \frac{1}{2})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take everything with $X \le \frac{1}{2}$, then remove the part with $Y \le \frac{1}{2}$:

$$P(X \le \tfrac{1}{2}, Y \gt \tfrac{1}{2}) = F(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1) - F(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) = 0.375 - 0.125 = 0.25$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For the joint CDF of Example 3, find the marginal CDF of $Y$ and
name its distribution. Then find $P(X \le 1 \text{ or } Y \le 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $x \to \infty$, so $1 - e^{-x} \to 1$ and $F_Y(y) = 1 - e^{-2y}$ for $y \ge 0$. This is $\text{Exp}(2)$, rate 2, mean $0.5$ seconds. For the "or" event:

$$P(X \le 1 \text{ or } Y \le 1) = F_X(1) + F_Y(1) - F(1, 1) \approx 0.6321 + 0.8647 - 0.5466 = 0.9502$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $G(x, y) = 1$ if $x + y \ge 0$ and $G(x, y) = 0$ otherwise. Show
that $G$ has the right limits and never decreases, but is **not** a joint CDF.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If $x \to -\infty$ (with $y$ fixed), then $x + y \lt 0$ eventually, so $G \to 0$; the same for $y$. If both go to $\infty$, then $G = 1$. Making $x$ or $y$ bigger can only turn $x + y \lt 0$ into $x + y \ge 0$, so $G$ never decreases. Now apply the rectangle rule to $-1 \lt X \le 1, \ -1 \lt Y \le 1$:

$$G(1, 1) - G(-1, 1) - G(1, -1) + G(-1, -1) = 1 - 1 - 1 + 0 = -1$$

A probability cannot be $-1$, so $G$ is not a joint CDF.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the answer $0.375$ of Example 2 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Sample f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square by keeping points under the surface.
u = rng.random(n_trials)
v = rng.random(n_trials)
keep = 2 * rng.random(n_trials) < u + v
u, v = u[keep], v[keep]

F = lambda a, b: a * b * (a + b) / 2
exact = F(1, 1) - F(0.5, 1) - F(1, 0.5) + F(0.5, 0.5)
print(f"P(X > 1/2, Y > 1/2): simulation {np.mean((u > 0.5) & (v > 0.5)):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X > 1/2, Y > 1/2): simulation 0.3737, exact 0.3750
```

</details>

## Summary

- The joint CDF is $F(x, y) = P(X \le x, Y \le y)$: the probability of the
  south-west region of the corner $(x, y)$.
- It is defined the same way for discrete, continuous and mixed variables.
- Let one variable go to infinity to get a marginal CDF.
- Any rectangle needs four CDF values: big region, minus two strips, plus the corner.
- For a continuous pair, differentiate once in $x$ and once in $y$ to get the density.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(a \lt X \le b, \ c \lt Y \le d) = F(b, d) - F(a, d) - F(b, c) + F(a, c)}
$$
