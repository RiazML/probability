# 10.7 Bayes for Random Variables

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 10: Joint Distributions](README.md) / 10.7 Bayes for Random Variables

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [10.6 IID](10.6-IID.md)

> **Big idea:** Bayes' theorem for random variables turns "the distribution of the data given the unknown" into "the distribution of the unknown given the data", and the rule is always posterior proportional to likelihood times prior.

---

## Why It Matters

In [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)
the unknown was one event, like "sick". Real unknowns are often numbers: a
click-through rate, the mean of a sensor, the class of an image. And real data are
often numbers too: a blood test level, a count of failed logins. Bayes for random
variables handles every combination of discrete and continuous. It is the engine of
Bayesian statistics, of spam filters, and of every probabilistic classifier.

## Intuition

A doctor measures the level of a protein in the blood. In healthy people the level
is usually around 0. In sick people it is usually around 2. Only 10% of patients
are sick. A patient comes back with level $1.5$. Is the patient sick?

Think of it as two steps, as in [10.4 Conditional Distributions](10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md).
**Nature first picks the hidden value** (sick or healthy) from the **prior**. Then
**the data are drawn** from the conditional distribution given that hidden value, the
**likelihood**. We see only the data. Bayes reverses the arrow: given the data, how
likely is each hidden value?

The answer weighs two things. A level of $1.5$ is closer to 2 than to 0, so the data
point toward "sick". But sick people are rare, so the prior points toward "healthy".
The **posterior** combines both. You will see below that it is about 23%: more than
the 10% prior, but still far from certain.

## Picture

```text
   the model goes forward               Bayes goes backward

    hidden X          data Y              data Y            hidden X
   +--------+      +---------+          +---------+      +-----------+
   | prior  | ---> | likeli- |          | observed| ---> | posterior |
   | f_X(x) |      | hood    |          |  y      |      | f(x | y)  |
   +--------+      | f(y | x)|          +---------+      +-----------+
                   +---------+

                 f(y | x) f_X(x)
   f(x | y) = ---------------------  =  likelihood x prior / evidence
                     f_Y(y)

   f_Y(y) = sum or integral over all x of f(y | x) f_X(x)
```

The model runs from the hidden value to the data. Bayes runs from the data back to the hidden value.

## Definition

Let $X$ be the unknown and $Y$ the data. We know the **prior** of $X$ and the
**likelihood** (the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X$). **Bayes' theorem for
random variables** gives the **posterior**, the conditional distribution of $X$ given
$Y = y$:

$$
f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace f_X(x)}{f_Y(y)}, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x') \thinspace f_X(x') \thinspace dx'
$$

where:

- $f_X(x)$ is the **prior**: what we believe about $X$ before seeing data,
- $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$ is the **likelihood**: how probable the data $y$ are if $X = x$,
- $f_Y(y)$ is the **evidence**: the marginal of $Y$, found by integrating out $x$
  ([10.3](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md)). It must be greater than 0.

**In words:** posterior = likelihood times prior, divided by the total over all
possible unknowns.

The same formula holds for every mix of types. Use a PMF for a discrete variable and
a PDF for a continuous one, and a sum over $x'$ when $X$ is discrete:

| Unknown $X$ | Data $Y$ | Posterior |
|---|---|---|
| discrete | discrete | $p(x \mid y) = p(y \mid x) p_X(x) / \sum_{x'} p(y \mid x') p_X(x')$ |
| discrete | continuous | $p(x \mid y) = f(y \mid x) p_X(x) / \sum_{x'} f(y \mid x') p_X(x')$ |
| continuous | discrete | $f(x \mid y) = p(y \mid x) f_X(x) / \int p(y \mid x') f_X(x') \thinspace dx'$ |
| continuous | continuous | $f(x \mid y) = f(y \mid x) f_X(x) / \int f(y \mid x') f_X(x') \thinspace dx'$ |

The bottom does not depend on $x$, so we often write the short form:

$$
\text{posterior} \propto \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}, \qquad f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) \propto f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace f_X(x)
$$

**In words:** as a function of $x$, the posterior has the shape of likelihood times
prior. Rescale that shape so it sums or integrates to 1.

## Why It Is True

Write the joint distribution in two ways with the multiplication rule from
[10.4](10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md): first $X$ then $Y$, or first $Y$ then $X$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) \thinspace f_Y(y) &= f_{X,Y}(x, y) && \text{(multiplication rule, } Y \text{ first)} \\
&= f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace f_X(x) && \text{(multiplication rule, } X \text{ first)} \\
f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) &= \frac{f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace f_X(x)}{f_Y(y)} && \text{(divide by } f_Y(y) \gt 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The evidence is the marginal of $Y$: integrate the joint
$f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x') f_X(x')$ over all $x'$.

**Mixed case.** When $X$ is discrete and $Y$ is continuous, $P(Y = y) = 0$, so
condition on a thin strip $y \le Y \le y + \Delta$ instead (as in
[10.4](10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md)). By Bayes' theorem for events:

$$
P(X = x \mid y \le Y \le y + \Delta) = \frac{P(y \le Y \le y + \Delta \mid X = x) \thinspace p_X(x)}{P(y \le Y \le y + \Delta)} \approx \frac{f(y \mid x) \thinspace \Delta \thinspace p_X(x)}{f_Y(y) \thinspace \Delta}
$$

The $\Delta$ cancels, which leaves the mixed formula of the table.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Blood Test with a Number

**Problem.** Let $X = 1$ if a patient is sick, with prior $P(X = 1) = 0.1$. The
protein level $Y$ is $\mathcal{N}(2, 1)$ for sick patients and $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ for
healthy ones (mean 2 or 0, variance 1). A patient has $Y = 1.5$. Find
$P(X = 1 \mid Y = 1.5)$.

**Solution.**

1. The likelihoods are normal densities ([9.2 Normal Gaussian](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)):

$$
f(1.5 \mid \text{sick}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(1.5 - 2)^2 / 2} = \frac{e^{-0.125}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}, \qquad f(1.5 \mid \text{healthy}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-1.5^2 / 2} = \frac{e^{-1.125}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
$$

2. Their ratio is simple, because the factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ cancels:

$$
\frac{f(1.5 \mid \text{sick})}{f(1.5 \mid \text{healthy})} = e^{-0.125 + 1.125} = e^{1} \approx 2.718
$$

3. Use the mixed formula and divide top and bottom by $f(1.5 \mid \text{healthy})$:

$$
P(X = 1 \mid Y = 1.5) = \frac{0.1 \times e}{0.1 \times e + 0.9 \times 1} = \frac{0.2718}{0.2718 + 0.9} = \frac{0.2718}{1.1718} \approx 0.232
$$

**Answer.** About 23%. The test result raised the chance from 10% to 23%.

**Check.** The data favor "sick" by a factor of $e \approx 2.7$, but the prior favors
"healthy" by a factor of 9. Since 9 is bigger than 2.7, the posterior should still
be below one half. It is. The simulation gets $0.2327$.

### Example 2: Failed Logins

**Problem.** A normal user makes $Y \sim \text{Pois}(2)$ failed logins per day, and an
attacker makes $Y \sim \text{Pois}(8)$ (here the parameter is the mean count). One
account in 100 is an attacker. An account has 7 failed logins today. What is the
probability that it is an attacker?

**Solution.**

1. The Poisson PMF is $P(Y = k) = e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k / k!$ ([7.5 Poisson](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.5-Poisson.md)).
   The ratio of the likelihoods at $k = 7$ (the $7!$ cancels):

$$
\frac{P(Y = 7 \mid \text{attacker})}{P(Y = 7 \mid \text{normal})} = \frac{e^{-8} \thinspace 8^7}{e^{-2} \thinspace 2^7} = e^{-6} \thinspace 4^7 = 16384 \times 0.002479 \approx 40.6
$$

2. Discrete Bayes, again divided through by the "normal" likelihood:

$$
P(\text{attacker} \mid Y = 7) = \frac{0.01 \times 40.6}{0.01 \times 40.6 + 0.99 \times 1} = \frac{0.406}{1.396} \approx 0.291
$$

**Answer.** About 29%.

**Check.** Seven failures are 40 times more likely for an attacker. But attackers
are 99 times rarer, so the posterior stays below one half. A security team should
look at the account, not lock it automatically. This is the base rate effect from
Module 04 again.

### Example 3: Learning a Click-Through Rate

**Problem.** A new ad has an unknown click-through rate $p$. Before any data, every
value is equally plausible: $p \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Then 10 visitors see it and
7 click, so $Y \mid p \sim \text{Bin}(10, p)$ with $Y = 7$. Find the posterior density of
$p$, its mean, and $P(p \gt 0.5 \mid Y = 7)$.

**Solution.**

1. Prior $f(p) = 1$ on $[0, 1]$. Likelihood $P(Y = 7 \mid p) = \binom{10}{7} p^7 (1 - p)^3$.
2. The posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Drop the constant $\binom{10}{7}$:

$$
f(p \mid Y = 7) \propto p^7 (1 - p)^3
$$

3. Find the normalizing constant. Expand $(1 - p)^3 = 1 - 3p + 3p^2 - p^3$ and integrate term by term:

$$
\int_0^1 p^7 (1 - p)^3 \thinspace dp = \frac{1}{8} - \frac{3}{9} + \frac{3}{10} - \frac{1}{11} = \frac{1}{1320}
$$

   So $f(p \mid Y = 7) = 1320 \thinspace p^7 (1 - p)^3$ on $[0, 1]$. This is the
   $\text{Beta}(8, 4)$ density ([9.5 Beta](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md)).
4. The mean of $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ is $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$:

$$
E[p \mid Y = 7] = \frac{8}{8 + 4} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667
$$

5. $P(p \gt 0.5 \mid Y = 7) = \int_{0.5}^{1} 1320 \thinspace p^7 (1 - p)^3 \thinspace dp$. The same
   term-by-term method (or `scipy.stats.beta(8, 4).sf(0.5)`) gives $\frac{227}{256} \approx 0.887$.

**Answer.** The posterior is $\text{Beta}(8, 4)$, with mean $\frac{2}{3}$, and we are
about 89% sure that the rate is above one half.

**Check.** The posterior mean $0.667$ lies between the prior mean $0.5$ and the
observed rate $0.7$, closer to the data. With only 10 visitors the prior still pulls a
little. Also, the evidence is $P(Y = 7) = \binom{10}{7} \cdot \frac{1}{1320} = \frac{120}{1320} = \frac{1}{11}$:
with a uniform prior every count from 0 to 10 is equally likely.

## Simulate It

The code runs the two-step model forward many times, then keeps only the runs that
match the data. The kept hidden values follow the posterior. For a continuous data
value, "match" means "inside a thin strip".

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 4_000_000

# Example 3: prior p ~ Unif(0, 1), then Y ~ Bin(10, p) clicks.
p = rng.random(n_trials)
clicks = rng.binomial(10, p)
print(f"P(Y = 7):           simulation {np.mean(clicks == 7):.4f}, exact {1 / 11:.4f}")

# Condition on the data: keep only the worlds where we saw 7 clicks.
posterior = p[clicks == 7]
print(f"E[p | Y = 7]:       simulation {posterior.mean():.4f}, exact {8 / 12:.4f}")
print(f"P(p > 0.5 | Y = 7): simulation {np.mean(posterior > 0.5):.4f}, exact {227 / 256:.4f}")

# Example 1: sick with prob 0.1; level ~ N(2, 1) if sick, N(0, 1) if healthy.
sick = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.1
level = rng.normal(np.where(sick, 2.0, 0.0), 1.0)
near = np.abs(level - 1.5) < 0.02          # thin strip around Y = 1.5
exact = 0.1 * np.e / (0.1 * np.e + 0.9)
print(f"P(sick | Y = 1.5):  simulation {sick[near].mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(Y = 7):           simulation 0.0909, exact 0.0909
E[p | Y = 7]:       simulation 0.6665, exact 0.6667
P(p > 0.5 | Y = 7): simulation 0.8860, exact 0.8867
P(sick | Y = 1.5):  simulation 0.2327, exact 0.2320
```

> **Tip:** "Simulate forward, keep what matches the data" is a correct but slow way
> to sample a posterior, because most runs are thrown away. Better methods are in
> [19.7 MCMC](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/README.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing the likelihood with the posterior.** $f(y \mid x)$ as a function of $x$
  is not a distribution over $x$: it need not integrate to 1. Only after multiplying
  by the prior and normalizing do you get the posterior.
- **Forgetting the prior.** Seven failed logins look damning, but with a 1% base rate
  the posterior is only 29% (Example 2).
- **Normalizing over the wrong variable.** The evidence sums or integrates over the
  **unknown** $x$, with the data $y$ held fixed.
- **Being afraid of $P(Y = y) = 0$.** For continuous data, use densities in the
  likelihood. The tiny strip width cancels (see the mixed case above).
- **Dropping a factor that depends on the unknown.** In the "proportional to" form
  you may drop factors without $x$, such as $\binom{10}{7}$, but never factors with
  $x$ in them.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a probabilistic classifier computes
  $P(\text{class} \mid \text{features}) \propto p(\text{features} \mid \text{class}) P(\text{class})$
  ([21.1.1 Probabilistic Classifiers and Naive Bayes](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md)).
  Bayesian neural networks put a prior on the weights.
- **Computer science:** A/B testing with a Beta posterior on each click rate (Example
  3), and anomaly detection on login counts (Example 2).
- **Medicine:** turning a continuous test result into a probability of disease
  (Example 1).
- **Engineering:** robots and GPS filters update a posterior over position after every
  sensor reading. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior
  ([4.6 Sequential Bayesian Updating](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a new restaurant with 5 good reviews out of 5 is probably good,
  but not as surely as one with 450 good reviews out of 500.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In the shop, $P(X = 1) = 0.30$, $P(X = 2) = 0.35$, $P(X = 3) = 0.35$
(items in the cart), and the chance of paying is $\frac{1}{3}$, $\frac{4}{7}$,
$\frac{6}{7}$ for 1, 2, 3 items. A customer paid. Find the probability that they had
3 items.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Likelihood times prior for each $x$: $\frac{1}{3} \times 0.30 = 0.10$, $\frac{4}{7} \times 0.35 = 0.20$, $\frac{6}{7} \times 0.35 = 0.30$. The evidence is $0.10 + 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.60$.

$$P(X = 3 \mid \text{paid}) = \frac{0.30}{0.60} = 0.5$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A coin has unknown heads probability $p \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. One flip
shows heads. Find the posterior density of $p$ and $P(p \gt \frac{1}{2} \mid \text{H})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Posterior $\propto p \times 1 = p$ on $[0, 1]$. Since $\int_0^1 p \thinspace dp = \frac{1}{2}$, the posterior is $f(p \mid \text{H}) = 2p$:

$$P(p \gt \tfrac{1}{2} \mid \text{H}) = \int_{1/2}^{1} 2p \thinspace dp = 1 - \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4}$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Light bulbs come from supplier A or B with probability $0.5$ each.
Lifetimes (in years) are $\text{Exp}(1)$ for A and $\text{Exp}(0.5)$ for B (rates, so
means 1 and 2 years). A bulb dies at exactly 3 years. Find $P(\text{A} \mid \text{lifetime} = 3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The likelihoods are densities: $f(3 \mid \text{A}) = e^{-3}$ and $f(3 \mid \text{B}) = 0.5 e^{-1.5}$. The equal priors cancel:

$$P(\text{A} \mid 3) = \frac{e^{-3}}{e^{-3} + 0.5 e^{-1.5}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.5 e^{1.5}} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 2.241} \approx 0.309$$

A long life points toward the longer-lived supplier B.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Your prior for a click rate is $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$, with density
proportional to $p(1 - p)$. Then 3 of 5 visitors click. Find the posterior and its mean.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Likelihood $\propto p^3 (1 - p)^2$. Posterior $\propto p^3 (1 - p)^2 \cdot p (1 - p) = p^4 (1 - p)^3$, which is $\text{Beta}(5, 4)$:

$$E[p \mid \text{data}] = \frac{5}{5 + 4} = \frac{5}{9} \approx 0.556$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** The true mean $\mu$ of a sensor has prior $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. One reading
is $Y \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)$, and you observe $Y = 2$. Show that the posterior
is $\mathcal{N}(1, \frac{1}{2})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Keep only the factors with $\mu$ (posterior $\propto$ likelihood times prior), then complete the square:

$$f(\mu \mid 2) \propto e^{-(2 - \mu)^2 / 2} \thinspace e^{-\mu^2 / 2} = e^{-(2\mu^2 - 4\mu + 4) / 2} = e^{-(\mu - 1)^2 - 1} \propto e^{-(\mu - 1)^2 / (2 \cdot \frac{1}{2})}$$

That is the shape of a normal density with mean 1 and variance $\frac{1}{2}$, so the posterior is $\mathcal{N}(1, \frac{1}{2})$. The estimate is halfway between the prior mean 0 and the reading 2, and it is more certain than either one alone. The general case is in [12.7 Conditionals](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.7-Conditionals.md).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 4_000_000

# Supplier A (rate 1 per year) or B (rate 0.5 per year), each with prior 0.5.
from_a = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.5
life = rng.exponential(np.where(from_a, 1.0, 2.0))   # scale = 1 / rate
near = np.abs(life - 3) < 0.05                        # thin strip around 3 years

exact = 1 / (1 + 0.5 * np.exp(1.5))
print(f"P(A | lifetime = 3): simulation {from_a[near].mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

```text
P(A | lifetime = 3): simulation 0.3079, exact 0.3086
```

</details>

## Summary

- The **prior** is what you believe about the unknown, the **likelihood** is how the
  data depend on it, and the **posterior** is what you believe after the data.
- Bayes for random variables: posterior = likelihood times prior / evidence. The
  evidence is a sum or an integral over the unknown.
- It works for every mix of discrete and continuous. Use densities for continuous
  data; the strip width cancels.
- In practice, write posterior $\propto$ likelihood times prior, recognize the shape,
  and normalize at the end.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace f_X(x)}{\int f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x') \thinspace f_X(x') \thinspace dx'}}
$$
