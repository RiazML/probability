# 10.4 Conditional Distributions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 10: Joint Distributions](README.md) / 10.4 Conditional Distributions

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [10.3 Marginal Distributions](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md)

> **Big idea:** The conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$ is one slice of the joint distribution, rescaled so that it adds up to 1.

---

## Why It Matters

Most useful questions are conditional. Given that a customer has 3 items in the
cart, how likely are they to pay? Given a patient's blood pressure, what is the
distribution of their heart rate? Given the words in an email, how likely is it
to be spam? The **conditional distribution** answers all of these. It is also the
key to building joint distributions step by step, to Bayes' theorem for random
variables ([10.7](10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)), and to conditional
expectation ([Module 14](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/README.md)).

## Intuition

Go back to the shop. Of all customers, 35 out of every 100 have 3 items in the cart.
Among those 35, 5 leave and 30 pay. So **among customers with 3 items**, the chance
of paying is $\frac{30}{35} \approx 0.857$. We kept only one row of the table and
divided by its total, so that the row adds up to 1 again.

That is all a conditional distribution is: **keep the slice you know you are in,
then rescale it**. Knowing $X = 3$ makes the other rows impossible, so we throw
them away.

For a continuous pair, the slice is a cut through the density surface along the
line $X = x$. The cut gives a curve. Its area is not 1 in general, so we divide by
its area. The area of the slice is exactly the marginal density $f_X(x)$ from
[10.3 Marginal Distributions](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md).

## Picture

```text
   Joint table                         Condition on X = 3

             Y = 0   Y = 1                       Y = 0   Y = 1
          +-------+-------+                   +-------+-------+
   X = 1  | 0.20  | 0.10  |  (thrown away)    |       |       |
          +-------+-------+                   +-------+-------+
   X = 2  | 0.15  | 0.20  |  (thrown away)    |       |       |
          +-------+-------+                   +-------+-------+
   X = 3  | 0.05  | 0.30  |  row sum 0.35 --> | 0.143 | 0.857 |  sum = 1
          +-------+-------+                   +-------+-------+
                                     0.05/0.35 = 0.143    0.30/0.35 = 0.857
```

To condition on $X = 3$, keep that row and divide each cell by the row sum.

## Definition

**Discrete case.** For any $x$ with $p_X(x) \gt 0$, the **conditional PMF** of $Y$
given $X = x$ is:

$$
p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = P(Y = y \mid X = x) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_X(x)}
$$

**Continuous case.** For any $x$ with $f_X(x) \gt 0$, the **conditional PDF** of $Y$
given $X = x$ is:

$$
f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_X(x)}
$$

where:

- $f_{X,Y}(x, y)$ is the joint density, seen as a function of $y$ with $x$ fixed (the slice),
- $f_X(x)$ is the marginal density of $X$ (the area of the slice), which must be greater than 0.

**In words:** conditional = joint divided by marginal. Take the slice at $x$ and
rescale it to total probability 1.

With $x$ fixed, $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$ is an ordinary density in $y$. You use it
like any other density:

$$
P(Y \in B \mid X = x) = \int_B f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace dy
$$

Turning the definition around gives the **multiplication rule** for random
variables, which builds a joint distribution in two steps:

$$
f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x) \thinspace f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)
$$

**In words:** first choose $x$ from its marginal, then choose $y$ from its
conditional given that $x$.

## Why It Is True

**Discrete.** This is the old definition of conditional probability
([4.1 Conditional Probability](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.1-Conditional-Probability.md))
applied to the events $A = \lbrace Y = y \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace X = x \rbrace$:
$P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B)$. It is a valid PMF, because it adds to 1:

$$
\sum_{y} p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{p_X(x)} \sum_{y} p_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{p_X(x)}{p_X(x)} = 1
$$

**Continuous.** Here there is a real problem: $P(X = x) = 0$, so we cannot divide by
it. The fix is to condition on a thin strip $x \le X \le x + \Delta$ instead, which
has positive probability, and then let $\Delta$ shrink. For a small $\Delta$ the
density hardly changes across the strip, so:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y \le y \mid x \le X \le x + \Delta) &= \frac{P(x \le X \le x + \Delta, \ Y \le y)}{P(x \le X \le x + \Delta)} && \text{(conditional probability)} \\
&\approx \frac{\Delta \int_{-\infty}^{y} f_{X,Y}(x, t) \thinspace dt}{\Delta \thinspace f_X(x)} && \text{(strip of width } \Delta \text{)} \\
&= \int_{-\infty}^{y} \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, t)}{f_X(x)} \thinspace dt && \text{(the } \Delta \text{ cancels)}
\end{aligned}
$$

As $\Delta \to 0$ the approximation becomes exact. The function inside the last
integral is the conditional density. It integrates to 1 over all $y$, because
$\int f_{X,Y}(x, t) \thinspace dt = f_X(x)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Who Pays

**Problem.** Use the shop table ($X$ = items, $Y = 1$ if the customer pays). Find the
conditional distribution of $Y$ given each value of $X$, and the conditional
distribution of $X$ given $Y = 1$.

**Solution.**

1. The row sums are $p_X(1) = 0.30$, $p_X(2) = 0.35$, $p_X(3) = 0.35$
   ([10.3](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md)). Divide the $Y = 1$ cell of each row by
   its row sum:

$$
P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = \frac{0.10}{0.30} \approx 0.333, \quad P(Y = 1 \mid X = 2) = \frac{0.20}{0.35} \approx 0.571, \quad P(Y = 1 \mid X = 3) = \frac{0.30}{0.35} \approx 0.857
$$

   In each case $P(Y = 0 \mid X = x) = 1 - P(Y = 1 \mid X = x)$.
2. Now condition the other way. The $Y = 1$ column sums to $p_Y(1) = 0.60$:

$$
P(X = 1 \mid Y = 1) = \frac{0.10}{0.60} = \frac{1}{6}, \quad P(X = 2 \mid Y = 1) = \frac{0.20}{0.60} = \frac{1}{3}, \quad P(X = 3 \mid Y = 1) = \frac{0.30}{0.60} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

**Answer.** The chance of paying rises from about 33% (1 item) to 57% (2 items) to
86% (3 items). Among paying customers, half had 3 items.

**Check.** $\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$. Notice that
$P(Y = 1 \mid X = 3) \approx 0.857$ and $P(X = 3 \mid Y = 1) = 0.5$ are different
numbers. The order of conditioning matters.

### Example 2: A Slice of a Density

**Problem.** Let $f(x, y) = x + y$ on the unit square. Find $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$,
then find $P(Y \le \frac{1}{2} \mid X = \frac{1}{4})$.

**Solution.**

1. From [10.3](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md), $f_X(x) = x + \frac{1}{2}$. Divide:

$$
f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{x + y}{x + \frac{1}{2}}, \quad 0 \le y \le 1
$$

2. Put $x = \frac{1}{4}$. The bottom becomes $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{4}$:

$$
f_{Y \mid X}\left(y \mid \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{\frac{1}{4} + y}{\frac{3}{4}} = \frac{4}{3} \left( \frac{1}{4} + y \right)
$$

3. Integrate from 0 to $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$
P\left(Y \le \tfrac{1}{2} \mid X = \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{4}{3} \int_0^{1/2} \left( \frac{1}{4} + y \right) dy = \frac{4}{3} \left( \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{8} \right) = \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

**Answer.** $P(Y \le \frac{1}{2} \mid X = \frac{1}{4}) = \frac{1}{3}$.

**Check.** Without the condition, $P(Y \le \frac{1}{2}) = \int_0^{1/2} (y + \frac{1}{2}) \thinspace dy = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375$.
Knowing that $X$ is small changes the answer, so $X$ and $Y$ are related here.

### Example 3: Building a Joint Distribution in Two Steps

**Problem.** A program picks a random buffer size $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ (in MB,
uniform on $[0, 1]$). Then it picks a random position $Y$ inside the buffer:
given $X = x$, $Y \sim \text{Unif}(0, x)$. Find the joint density, the marginal
density of $Y$, and $P(Y \le \frac{1}{4})$.

**Solution.**

1. The marginal is $f_X(x) = 1$ on $(0, 1)$. The conditional is $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{x}$
   on $(0, x)$. Use the multiplication rule:

$$
f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x) \thinspace f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x}, \quad 0 \lt y \lt x \lt 1
$$

2. Integrate out $x$. For a fixed $y$, $x$ runs from $y$ to 1:

$$
f_Y(y) = \int_y^1 \frac{1}{x} \thinspace dx = \ln 1 - \ln y = -\ln y, \quad 0 \lt y \lt 1
$$

3. Integrate $f_Y$ from 0 to $\frac{1}{4}$, using $\int -\ln y \thinspace dy = y - y \ln y$
   (and $y \ln y \to 0$ as $y \to 0$):

$$
P\left(Y \le \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = \Big[ y - y \ln y \Big]_0^{1/4} = \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{4} \ln \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{\ln 4}{4} \approx 0.25 + 0.3466 = 0.5966
$$

**Answer.** $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{x}$ on $0 \lt y \lt x \lt 1$, $f_Y(y) = -\ln y$,
and $P(Y \le \frac{1}{4}) \approx 0.597$.

**Check.** $Y$ is at most $X$, so it tends to be small. Much more than a quarter of
the probability is below $\frac{1}{4}$. The simulation agrees.

## Simulate It

Conditioning on $X = \frac{1}{4}$ exactly is impossible in a simulation (it has
probability 0). So the code does what the proof does: it keeps the points in a thin
strip around $X = \frac{1}{4}$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 2_000_000

# Example 2: f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square (keep points under the surface).
u = rng.random(n_trials)
v = rng.random(n_trials)
keep = 2 * rng.random(n_trials) < u + v
u, v = u[keep], v[keep]
# "Given X = 1/4" is a zero-probability event, so use a thin strip around it.
strip = np.abs(u - 0.25) < 0.01
estimate = np.mean(v[strip] <= 0.5)
print(f"P(Y <= 1/2 | X = 1/4): simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {1 / 3:.4f}")

# Example 3: X ~ Unif(0, 1), then Y given X = x is Unif(0, x).
x = rng.random(n_trials)
y = x * rng.random(n_trials)
exact = 0.25 + 0.25 * np.log(4)
print(f"P(Y <= 1/4):           simulation {np.mean(y <= 0.25):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(Y <= 1/2 | X = 1/4): simulation 0.3347, exact 0.3333
P(Y <= 1/4):           simulation 0.5967, exact 0.5966
```

The second part shows the multiplication rule in code: draw $X$ from its marginal,
then draw $Y$ from its conditional.

## Common Mistakes

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** To condition on $X = x$, divide by the **row**
  sum $p_X(x)$, not by 1 and not by a column sum.
- **Swapping the condition.** $P(Y = 1 \mid X = 3)$ and $P(X = 3 \mid Y = 1)$ are
  different (Example 1). Going from one to the other needs Bayes' theorem
  ([10.7](10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)).
- **Forgetting that the support can depend on $x$.** In Example 3, $Y$ given $X = x$
  lives on $(0, x)$, not on $(0, 1)$. Write the range next to every conditional density.
- **Thinking $P(X = x) = 0$ makes conditioning meaningless.** For continuous $X$ the
  conditional density is still well defined: it is the limit over thin strips.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a classifier models $p(\text{label} \mid \text{features})$,
  and a language model gives $p(\text{next word} \mid \text{previous words})$. Both are
  conditional distributions.
- **Computer science:** the response time of a server **given** its current load is
  what capacity planning needs.
- **Simulation:** complex models are sampled step by step with the multiplication
  rule, as in Example 3. This is the idea behind
  [19.7.2 Gibbs Sampling](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/19.7.2-Gibbs-Sampling.md).
- **Medicine:** growth charts show the distribution of a child's height **given**
  their age.
- **Everyday life:** "given that it is raining now, how long will it last?"

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** For the table with $p(0, 0) = 0.10$, $p(0, 1) = 0.20$, $p(1, 0) = 0.30$,
$p(1, 1) = 0.40$, find $P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1)$ and $P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = \frac{0.40}{0.30 + 0.40} = \frac{4}{7} \approx 0.571$$

$$P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = \frac{0.20}{0.10 + 0.20} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A point is uniform on the unit disk. Given $X = 0.6$, what is the
conditional distribution of $Y$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The joint density is the constant $\frac{1}{\pi}$, so the slice at $x = 0.6$ is flat. It runs from $-\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = -0.8$ to $0.8$. After rescaling, $Y$ given $X = 0.6$ is $\text{Unif}(-0.8, 0.8)$, with density $\frac{1}{1.6} = 0.625$ there.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For $f(x, y) = 8xy$ on $0 \lt y \lt x \lt 1$ (marginal
$f_X(x) = 4x^3$), find $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$ and $P(Y \le \frac{x}{2} \mid X = x)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{8xy}{4x^3} = \frac{2y}{x^2}, \quad 0 \lt y \lt x$$

$$P(Y \le \tfrac{x}{2} \mid X = x) = \int_0^{x/2} \frac{2y}{x^2} \thinspace dy = \frac{(x/2)^2}{x^2} = \frac{1}{4}$$

The answer does not depend on $x$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Roll a fair die to get $X$. Then flip $X$ fair coins and let $Y$ be
the number of heads. Find $P(Y = 0)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Given $X = x$, $Y \sim \text{Bin}(x, \frac{1}{2})$, so $P(Y = 0 \mid X = x) = (\frac{1}{2})^x$. By the multiplication rule $p(x, 0) = \frac{1}{6} (\frac{1}{2})^x$. Add over $x$ (the marginal):

$$P(Y = 0) = \tfrac{1}{6} \left( \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \tfrac{1}{32} + \tfrac{1}{64} \right) = \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot \tfrac{63}{64} = \tfrac{21}{128} \approx 0.164$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $f(x, y) = e^{-y}$ for $0 \lt x \lt y$. Its marginals are
$f_X(x) = e^{-x}$ and $f_Y(y) = y e^{-y}$ ([10.3](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md)).
Find both conditional densities and describe them in words.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{e^{-y}}{e^{-x}} = e^{-(y - x)}, \quad y \gt x$$

So given $X = x$, the extra amount $Y - x$ is $\text{Exp}(1)$: $Y$ is $x$ plus an exponential wait.

$$f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{e^{-y}}{y e^{-y}} = \frac{1}{y}, \quad 0 \lt x \lt y$$

So given $Y = y$, $X$ is $\text{Unif}(0, y)$: the first time is equally likely to be anywhere before $y$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 4 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Roll a die to get X, then flip X fair coins; Y is the number of heads.
x = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
y = rng.binomial(x, 0.5)

print(f"P(Y = 0): simulation {np.mean(y == 0):.4f}, exact {21 / 128:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(Y = 0): simulation 0.1641, exact 0.1641
```

</details>

## Summary

- The conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$ is the slice of the joint at $x$,
  divided by its total so it sums or integrates to 1.
- Conditional = joint / marginal. Joint = marginal times conditional.
- For continuous $X$, conditioning on $X = x$ means the limit over thin strips, and
  it is still well defined.
- The order matters: $Y$ given $X$ is not $X$ given $Y$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_X(x)}}
$$
