# 10.6 IID

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 10: Joint Distributions](README.md) / 10.6 IID

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [10.5 Independence of Random Variables](10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)

> **Big idea:** IID random variables are independent copies of the same random experiment, so their joint distribution is just the product of one distribution with itself, $n$ times.

---

## Why It Matters

"IID" is the most common assumption in probability, statistics and machine
learning. Repeated die rolls, a random sample of 1,000 users, the training examples
of a model: all are usually modeled as IID. It is the assumption behind the law of
large numbers and the central limit theorem
([Module 17](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/README.md)). It makes $n$-variable problems as
easy as one-variable problems.

## Intuition

Roll a fair die 5 times. Each roll has the same chances, $\frac{1}{6}$ for each face:
the rolls are **identically distributed**. And no roll affects another: they are
**independent**. Together: **independent and identically distributed**, or **IID**.

What is the chance that all 5 rolls are at most 4? One roll is at most 4 with
probability $\frac{4}{6}$. The rolls do not influence each other, so multiply five
times: $(\frac{4}{6})^5 \approx 0.13$. That multiplication is the whole power of IID:
a question about $n$ variables becomes a question about one variable, raised to the
power $n$.

Now the computer science version. A web page asks 20 back-end servers for data, in
parallel, and waits for all of them. Each server is fast 99% of the time. If the
servers behave like IID copies, the page is fast only when all 20 are fast:
$0.99^{20} \approx 0.82$. So almost 1 page in 5 is slow, even though each server is
slow only 1 time in 100. This effect is called **tail latency**, and it is a real
problem in large systems.

## Picture

```text
   one experiment, repeated n times, with no memory between repeats

   +---------+    +---------+    +---------+          +---------+
   |  F      |    |  F      |    |  F      |   ...    |  F      |
   | (same)  |    | (same)  |    | (same)  |          | (same)  |
   +----+----+    +----+----+    +----+----+          +----+----+
        |              |              |                    |
        v              v              v                    v
       X_1            X_2            X_3                  X_n
        \              \             /                    /
         +--------------+-----------+--------------------+
                               |
                               v
         joint density = f(x_1) * f(x_2) * ... * f(x_n)
```

Each $X_i$ comes from its own copy of the same distribution $F$, and no copy affects another.

## Definition

Random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are **independent and identically
distributed** (IID) with distribution $F$, written

$$
X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F
$$

if both of these hold:

1. **Identically distributed:** every $X_i$ has the same distribution $F$.
2. **Mutually independent:** for all sets $A_1, \dots, A_n$,
   $P(X_1 \in A_1, \dots, X_n \in A_n) = P(X_1 \in A_1) \cdots P(X_n \in A_n)$.
   (For $n$ variables, independence of every pair is not enough; see
   [4.8 Pairwise vs Mutual Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.8-Pairwise-vs-Mutual-Independence.md).)

Then the joint PMF or joint PDF is a product of $n$ copies of one function:

$$
f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i) = f(x_1) \thinspace f(x_2) \cdots f(x_n)
$$

where $f$ is the PMF or PDF of $F$, the same for every $i$.

**In words:** the probability of a whole list of values is the product of the
probabilities (or densities) of each value on its own.

## Why It Is True

The product formula is the definition of independence for $n$ variables, applied
with the same $f$ each time. Three useful results follow from it.

**1. All of them below a level.** Put $A_i = (-\infty, t]$ for every $i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X_1 \le t, \dots, X_n \le t) &= P(X_1 \le t) \cdots P(X_n \le t) && \text{(independent)} \\
&= F(t) \cdots F(t) = F(t)^n && \text{(identically distributed)}
\end{aligned}
$$

All of them being at most $t$ is the same as the largest being at most $t$. So this is the CDF of
the maximum: $P(\max_i X_i \le t) = F(t)^n$.

**2. All of them above a level.** In the same way,
$P(\min_i X_i \gt t) = (1 - F(t))^n$. Maximum and minimum get their own topic in
[13.6 Max and Min](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.6-Max-and-Min.md).

**3. Symmetry.** The product $f(x_1) \cdots f(x_n)$ does not change if you swap the
names of two variables. So any statement about the order of IID variables is equally
likely for every labeling. For continuous IID variables (where ties have probability
0), each of the $n$ variables is equally likely to be the largest:

$$
P(X_1 \text{ is the largest}) = \frac{1}{n}
$$

**In words:** with no information to separate them, every IID variable has the same
chance to win. Practice 5 asks for the full proof.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Five Dice

**Problem.** Roll 5 fair dice, $X_1, \dots, X_5 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}$ uniform on
$\lbrace 1, \dots, 6 \rbrace$. Find (a) the probability that all are at most 4, (b) the
probability that at least one shows 6, (c) the probability of the exact sequence
6, 6, 1, 2, 3.

**Solution.**

1. (a) One die is at most 4 with probability $\frac{4}{6}$. Use result 1 with $F(4) = \frac{4}{6}$:

$$
P(\text{all} \le 4) = \left( \frac{4}{6} \right)^5 = \frac{1024}{7776} \approx 0.1317
$$

2. (b) "At least one 6" is the complement of "all at most 5":

$$
P(\text{at least one } 6) = 1 - \left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^5 = 1 - \frac{3125}{7776} = \frac{4651}{7776} \approx 0.5981
$$

3. (c) Multiply the five single probabilities:

$$
P(6, 6, 1, 2, 3) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{7776} \approx 0.000129
$$

**Answer.** (a) about $0.132$, (b) about $0.598$, (c) $\frac{1}{7776}$.

**Check.** Every one of the $6^5 = 7776$ sequences has the same probability
$\frac{1}{7776}$, so (a) is just "count the sequences with entries 1 to 4", which is
$4^5 = 1024$. The two ways agree.

### Example 2: Tail Latency

**Problem.** A web page waits for 20 back-end servers. Each one answers within 100 ms
with probability $0.99$, and the servers are IID. What is the probability that the
page is ready within 100 ms? What if the page needs 100 servers?

**Solution.**

1. Let $X_i = 1$ if server $i$ is fast. Then $X_1, \dots, X_{20} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(0.99)$,
   where $0.99$ is the probability of success (fast).
2. The page is fast when every server is fast:

$$
P(\text{page fast}) = P(X_1 = 1) \cdots P(X_{20} = 1) = 0.99^{20} \approx 0.8179
$$

3. With 100 servers:

$$
0.99^{100} \approx 0.3660
$$

**Answer.** About 82% of pages are fast with 20 servers, but only about 37% with 100
servers.

**Check.** Each extra server multiplies the chance by $0.99$, so the chance shrinks
fast as the page grows. This is why big systems send backup requests to cut the tail.

### Example 3: Identical but Not Independent

**Problem.** Deal 2 cards from a shuffled deck without putting the first back. Let
$X_i = 1$ if card $i$ is an ace. Are $X_1$ and $X_2$ IID?

**Solution.**

1. $P(X_1 = 1) = \frac{4}{52}$. For the second card, use the law of total probability:

$$
P(X_2 = 1) = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{3}{51} + \frac{48}{52} \cdot \frac{4}{51} = \frac{12 + 192}{2652} = \frac{204}{2652} = \frac{4}{52}
$$

   So they are identically distributed.
2. Independence fails:

$$
P(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 1) = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{3}{51} = \frac{12}{2652} \approx 0.0045 \ne \left( \frac{4}{52} \right)^2 \approx 0.0059
$$

**Answer.** Identically distributed, but **not** independent, so **not** IID.

**Check.** Once an ace is gone, fewer aces remain, so the second card "remembers" the
first. With replacement (put the card back and shuffle), the draws would be IID.

## Simulate It

The code checks Examples 1 and 2, and the symmetry result: among 10 days of IID
rainfall, the first day is the wettest about 1 time in 10.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# Tail latency: 20 iid servers, each slow (over 100 ms) with probability 0.01.
slow = rng.random((n_trials, 20)) < 0.01
page_fast = ~slow.any(axis=1)                 # all 20 servers were fast
print(f"P(page fast):      simulation {page_fast.mean():.4f}, exact {0.99**20:.4f}")

# Dice: 5 iid rolls, all at most 4.
dice = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(n_trials, 5))
print(f"P(all dice <= 4):  simulation {np.mean(dice.max(axis=1) <= 4):.4f}, exact {(4 / 6)**5:.4f}")

# Symmetry: among 10 iid continuous values, day 1 is the largest 1/10 of the time.
rain = rng.exponential(5.0, size=(n_trials, 10))
first_is_max = rain.argmax(axis=1) == 0
print(f"P(X1 is largest):  simulation {first_is_max.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 10:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(page fast):      simulation 0.8172, exact 0.8179
P(all dice <= 4):  simulation 0.1321, exact 0.1317
P(X1 is largest):  simulation 0.1001, exact 0.1000
```

Note how each row of the arrays is one IID sample of size $n$, and each column is
one variable. The rainfall distribution does not matter for the last line: any
continuous IID distribution gives $\frac{1}{10}$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling a sample IID because each item "looks the same".** Identical
  distribution is only half. Drawing without replacement, or measurements over
  time (today's temperature depends on yesterday's), are not independent.
- **Adding instead of multiplying.** $P(\text{all 20 fast})$ is $0.99^{20}$, not
  $1 - 20 \times 0.01$. The sum is only an approximation (a union bound, see
  [3.6 Union Bound](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)), and it fails
  for large $n$.
- **Thinking IID means "all equal".** IID variables have the same **distribution**,
  but different **values**. Five dice rarely show the same number.
- **Forgetting that the order of IID values is random too.** In Example 1 (c) the
  exact sequence has probability $\frac{1}{7776}$, but "two sixes and a 1, 2, 3 in
  any order" is 60 times more likely.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** training data is assumed IID from one distribution.
  The **likelihood** of a dataset is then a product, $\prod_i p(x_i \mid \theta)$, and
  its logarithm is a sum. Most loss functions come from this
  ([21.1.2 Loss Functions as Probability](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)).
  When test data come from a different distribution than training data, the
  "identically distributed" part fails, and models break.
- **Computer science:** tail latency (Example 2), the analysis of hashing with random
  hash values, and IID random bits in randomized algorithms.
- **Statistics:** a random sample is IID, and the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ of IID
  variables settles near the true mean ([17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).
- **Reliability:** a system that needs all $n$ IID parts to work survives with
  probability $p^n$.
- **Everyday life:** a lottery draw each week is IID: past numbers do not make any
  number "due".

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X_1, X_2, X_3 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(0.2)$. Find
$P(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 0, X_3 = 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(1, 0, 1) = 0.2 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.032$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $U_1, \dots, U_4 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find
$P(\text{all} \le 0.5)$ and $P(\max_i U_i \le 0.9)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$, $F(t) = t$ on $[0, 1]$. So:

$$P(\text{all} \le 0.5) = 0.5^4 = 0.0625, \qquad P(\max_i U_i \le 0.9) = 0.9^4 = 0.6561$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A machine has 5 parts. Their lifetimes are IID $\text{Exp}(0.01)$
in hours (rate $0.01$ per hour, mean 100 hours). The machine stops at the first
part failure. Find the probability that it runs at least 10 hours, and name the
distribution of the time to the first failure.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

One part survives past $t$ with probability $e^{-0.01t}$. All 5 survive with probability:

$$P(\min_i X_i \gt t) = \left( e^{-0.01t} \right)^5 = e^{-0.05t}$$

This is the survival function of $\text{Exp}(0.05)$, so the first failure has mean $\frac{1}{0.05} = 20$ hours. For $t = 10$: $e^{-0.5} \approx 0.607$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A student says: "My 7 daily temperature readings are IID because they
come from the same thermometer in the same city." Explain the problem.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The readings may share a distribution only roughly (seasons change it), and they are not independent: a hot day is usually followed by another hot day. Knowing Monday's value changes what you expect for Tuesday. So the IID assumption is doubtful, and results that rely on it (such as error bars) may be too optimistic.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for $X_1, \dots, X_n$ IID with a density $f$,
$P(X_1 \text{ is the largest}) = \frac{1}{n}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $E_i$ be the event that $X_i$ is strictly larger than all the others. Ties have probability 0 for continuous variables, so with probability 1 exactly one $E_i$ happens: the events are disjoint and their probabilities add to 1. Next, $P(E_i)$ is the integral of $f(x_1) \cdots f(x_n)$ over the region where $x_i$ is the biggest coordinate. Rename the variables by swapping $x_i$ and $x_1$. The product $f(x_1) \cdots f(x_n)$ does not change, and the region becomes the region where $x_1$ is the biggest. So $P(E_i) = P(E_1)$ for every $i$. Then:

$$1 = P(E_1) + \dots + P(E_n) = n P(E_1) \implies P(E_1) = \tfrac{1}{n}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 3 by simulation: estimate $P(X_2 = 1)$ and
$P(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 1)$ for two cards dealt without replacement.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Deal 2 cards from a shuffled deck (cards 0-3 are the aces), without replacement.
first = rng.integers(0, 52, n_trials)
second = rng.integers(0, 51, n_trials)
second = second + (second >= first)       # skip the card already dealt
ace1, ace2 = first < 4, second < 4

print(f"P(X2 = 1):         simulation {ace2.mean():.4f}, exact {4 / 52:.4f}")
print(f"P(X1 = 1, X2 = 1): simulation {np.mean(ace1 & ace2):.4f}, exact {12 / 2652:.4f}")
print(f"P(X1 = 1) P(X2 = 1) would be      {(4 / 52)**2:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X2 = 1):         simulation 0.0767, exact 0.0769
P(X1 = 1, X2 = 1): simulation 0.0046, exact 0.0045
P(X1 = 1) P(X2 = 1) would be      0.0059
```

</details>

## Summary

- **IID** means mutually independent **and** the same distribution for every variable.
- The joint PMF or PDF is a product: $\prod_i f(x_i)$.
- "All of them" events become powers: $P(\max \le t) = F(t)^n$ and
  $P(\min \gt t) = (1 - F(t))^n$.
- IID variables are symmetric: each is equally likely to be the largest.
- Identically distributed is not enough. Drawing without replacement is the classic
  counterexample.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} f \implies f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)}
$$
