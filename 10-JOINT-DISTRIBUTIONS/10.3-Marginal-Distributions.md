# 10.3 Marginal Distributions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 10: Joint Distributions](README.md) / 10.3 Marginal Distributions

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [10.2 Joint CDF](10.2-Joint-CDF.md)

> **Big idea:** To get the distribution of one variable from a joint distribution, add up (or integrate) over all values of the other variable.

---

## Why It Matters

Often you know the joint distribution, but you care about only one variable. A shop
may model cart size and payment together, then ask: "what fraction of customers pay?"
That one-variable distribution is called the **marginal distribution**. You need it
for every conditional probability and every independence check in this module, and
it is also how hidden variables are removed in machine learning models.

## Intuition

Look at the shop table again: $X$ is the number of items in the cart and $Y$ is 1 if
the customer pays. To find the probability that a customer pays, you do not care
about the cart size. So you add down the $Y = 1$ column: $0.10 + 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.60$.
Sixty out of every 100 customers pay.

People used to write these sums in the **margins** of the table: row sums on the
right, column sums at the bottom. That is where the name "marginal" comes from.

For a continuous pair, imagine the density surface as a pile of sand on a table.
Now push all the sand in the $y$ direction onto the $x$-axis. The height of the
sand pile at each $x$ is the marginal density of $X$. Pushing is integrating over $y$.

## Picture

```text
                 Y = 0    Y = 1    |  row sum = P(X = x)
              +--------+--------+  |
       X = 1  |  0.20  |  0.10  |  |  0.20 + 0.10 = 0.30
              +--------+--------+  |
       X = 2  |  0.15  |  0.20  |  |  0.15 + 0.20 = 0.35
              +--------+--------+  |
       X = 3  |  0.05  |  0.30  |  |  0.05 + 0.30 = 0.35
              +--------+--------+  |
       -------------------------------------------------
       column    0.40     0.60     |  1.00
       sum = P(Y = y)
```

The marginal of $X$ is in the right margin (row sums), the marginal of $Y$ is in the bottom margin (column sums).

## Definition

Let $X$ and $Y$ have a joint distribution. The **marginal distribution** of $X$ is
the distribution of $X$ alone, when we ignore $Y$.

**Discrete case.** The **marginal PMF** of $X$ is:

$$
p_X(x) = \sum_{y} p_{X,Y}(x, y)
$$

**In words:** for each value $x$, add up its whole row of the table.

**Continuous case.** The **marginal PDF** of $X$ is:

$$
f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dy
$$

**In words:** for each value $x$, integrate the joint density along the vertical line
at $x$. We say we **integrate out** $y$.

The marginal of $Y$ is the same with the roles swapped: $p_Y(y) = \sum_{x} p_{X,Y}(x, y)$
and $f_Y(y) = \int f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dx$.

> **Warning:** The marginals do **not** determine the joint distribution. Many
> different tables have the same row sums and column sums (Practice 4). The joint
> distribution holds extra information: how $X$ and $Y$ move together.

## Why It Is True

**Discrete.** The events $\lbrace Y = y \rbrace$, one for each possible value $y$, do
not overlap and together cover $\Omega$. So they form a **partition**, and the law of
total probability ([4.3 Law of Total Probability](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md))
splits $\lbrace X = x \rbrace$ into pieces:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_X(x) = P(X = x) &= P\Big(\bigcup_{y} \lbrace X = x, Y = y \rbrace\Big) && \text{(the } Y \text{ values partition } \Omega \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{y} P(X = x, Y = y) && \text{(the pieces are disjoint)} \\
&= \sum_{y} p_{X,Y}(x, y) && \text{(definition of joint PMF)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Continuous.** Take any interval $B$ on the $x$-axis. The event $\lbrace X \in B \rbrace$
is the vertical band $\lbrace (x, y) : x \in B \rbrace$, with $y$ free:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X \in B) &= \int_{B} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dy \thinspace dx && \text{(probability of the band)} \\
&= \int_{B} \left( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dy \right) dx && \text{(group the inner integral)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The probability of every interval $B$ is the integral over $B$ of the bracket. That
is exactly what it means for the bracket to be the density of $X$. So
$f_X(x) = \int f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dy$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Marginals of the Shop Table

**Problem.** Find the marginal PMFs of $X$ (items in the cart) and $Y$ (pays or not)
from the shop table in the Picture.

**Solution.**

1. Add each row to get $p_X$:

$$
p_X(1) = 0.20 + 0.10 = 0.30, \quad p_X(2) = 0.15 + 0.20 = 0.35, \quad p_X(3) = 0.05 + 0.30 = 0.35
$$

2. Add each column to get $p_Y$:

$$
p_Y(0) = 0.20 + 0.15 + 0.05 = 0.40, \quad p_Y(1) = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.60
$$

**Answer.** $X$ takes the values 1, 2, 3 with probabilities $0.30, 0.35, 0.35$.
$Y = 1$ (pays) with probability $0.60$.

**Check.** Each marginal adds to 1: $0.30 + 0.35 + 0.35 = 1$ and $0.40 + 0.60 = 1$.

### Example 2: Integrating Out a Variable

**Problem.** Let $f(x, y) = x + y$ on the unit square. Find $f_X(x)$, and check that
it is a valid density.

**Solution.**

1. Fix $x$ in $[0, 1]$. The vertical line at $x$ crosses the square for $0 \le y \le 1$:

$$
f_X(x) = \int_0^1 (x + y) \thinspace dy = \Big[ xy + \frac{y^2}{2} \Big]_0^1 = x + \frac{1}{2}
$$

2. Check the total:

$$
\int_0^1 \left( x + \frac{1}{2} \right) dx = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1
$$

**Answer.** $f_X(x) = x + \frac{1}{2}$ for $0 \le x \le 1$ (and 0 elsewhere). By the
symmetry of $x + y$, also $f_Y(y) = y + \frac{1}{2}$.

**Check.** $f_X$ grows with $x$, because the joint density is larger for larger $x$.

### Example 3: Watch the Limits on a Triangle

**Problem.** Let $f(x, y) = 8xy$ on the triangle $0 \lt y \lt x \lt 1$ (from
[10.1](10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md)). Find both marginal densities.

**Solution.**

1. **Marginal of $X$.** Fix $x$. The vertical line at $x$ is inside the triangle for
   $0 \lt y \lt x$:

$$
f_X(x) = \int_0^x 8xy \thinspace dy = 8x \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} = 4x^3, \quad 0 \lt x \lt 1
$$

2. **Marginal of $Y$.** Fix $y$. The horizontal line at height $y$ is inside the
   triangle for $y \lt x \lt 1$:

$$
f_Y(y) = \int_y^1 8xy \thinspace dx = 8y \cdot \frac{1 - y^2}{2} = 4y(1 - y^2), \quad 0 \lt y \lt 1
$$

**Answer.** $f_X(x) = 4x^3$ and $f_Y(y) = 4y(1 - y^2)$, both on $(0, 1)$.

**Check.** $\int_0^1 4x^3 \thinspace dx = 1$ and $\int_0^1 (4y - 4y^3) \thinspace dy = 2 - 1 = 1$.
Both are valid densities. Using $0$ to $1$ as the limits in step 2 would give
$4y$, which integrates to 2. That is the classic mistake.

### Example 4: A Dart on a Round Board

**Problem.** A dart lands at a uniform random point $(X, Y)$ in the unit disk
$x^2 + y^2 \le 1$. Find the marginal density of $X$, and $P(\lvert X \rvert \le \frac{1}{2})$.

**Solution.**

1. The disk has area $\pi$, so the joint density is $f(x, y) = \frac{1}{\pi}$ inside it.
2. Fix $x$ in $[-1, 1]$. The vertical line at $x$ is inside the disk for
   $-\sqrt{1 - x^2} \le y \le \sqrt{1 - x^2}$, a segment of length $2\sqrt{1 - x^2}$:

$$
f_X(x) = \int_{-\sqrt{1 - x^2}}^{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} \frac{1}{\pi} \thinspace dy = \frac{2\sqrt{1 - x^2}}{\pi}, \quad -1 \le x \le 1
$$

3. Use the standard integral $\int \sqrt{1 - x^2} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{2} \left( x\sqrt{1 - x^2} + \operatorname{arcsin} x \right)$.
   By symmetry, double the integral from 0 to $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$
P\left(\lvert X \rvert \le \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 2 \cdot \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi} + \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.2757 + 0.3333 = 0.6090
$$

**Answer.** $f_X(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1 - x^2}$, a half-circle shape, **not** a
uniform density. $P(\lvert X \rvert \le \frac{1}{2}) \approx 0.609$.

**Check.** If $X$ were uniform on $[-1, 1]$, the answer would be $0.5$. It is bigger,
because the disk is tall in the middle and thin at the sides: more of the area is
near $x = 0$.

## Simulate It

The code throws uniform darts into the disk, forgets $Y$, and checks the marginal
density of $X$ from Example 4 at three points.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 2_000_000

# Uniform point in the unit disk: uniform in the square, keep if inside.
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n_trials)
y = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n_trials)
inside = x**2 + y**2 <= 1
x = x[inside]                       # forget Y: this is the marginal of X

def f_X(t):
    return 2 * np.sqrt(1 - t**2) / np.pi

# Density of X near a few points: fraction in a thin bin / bin width.
width = 0.02
for t in [0.0, 0.5, 0.9]:
    estimate = np.mean(np.abs(x - t) < width / 2) / width
    print(f"f_X({t}): simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {f_X(t):.4f}")

exact = 1 / 3 + np.sqrt(3) / (2 * np.pi)
print(f"P(|X| <= 1/2): simulation {np.mean(np.abs(x) <= 0.5):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
f_X(0.0): simulation 0.6370, exact 0.6366
f_X(0.5): simulation 0.5457, exact 0.5513
f_X(0.9): simulation 0.2762, exact 0.2775
P(|X| <= 1/2): simulation 0.6088, exact 0.6090
```

The density estimates agree to about two decimal places (a thin bin holds few
points, so they are a little noisy). The density really falls from about $0.64$ in
the middle to about $0.28$ near the edge.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the full range for the inner limits.** On a triangle or a disk, the range
  of $y$ depends on $x$. Draw the region and read the limits for a fixed $x$.
- **Forgetting where the marginal is zero.** Always write the support next to the
  answer, for example: $4x^3$ for $0 \lt x \lt 1$, and 0 elsewhere.
- **Thinking "uniform joint" means "uniform marginals".** The dart on the disk is
  uniform, but $X$ is not. A uniform joint has uniform marginals only on a rectangle.
- **Trying to rebuild the joint from the marginals.** In general you cannot. You can
  only do it when the variables are independent, see
  [10.5 Independence of Random Variables](10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a model with a hidden variable $Z$ gives the data
  probability by summing it out: $p(x) = \sum_{z} p(x, z)$. Mixture models
  ([10.8 Mixture Distributions](10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)) and language models
  do this all the time.
- **Computer science:** from a joint table of (browser, error type) in logs, the
  marginal tells you the overall error rate of each type.
- **Medicine:** the overall rate of a disease is the marginal of a (disease, age
  group) table.
- **Everyday life:** a survey of (city, opinion) gives the national opinion by adding
  over cities.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Find both marginals of this table:

| | $Y = 0$ | $Y = 1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $X = 0$ | 0.10 | 0.20 |
| $X = 1$ | 0.30 | 0.40 |

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rows: $p_X(0) = 0.10 + 0.20 = 0.30$ and $p_X(1) = 0.30 + 0.40 = 0.70$.

Columns: $p_Y(0) = 0.10 + 0.30 = 0.40$ and $p_Y(1) = 0.20 + 0.40 = 0.60$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $f(x, y) = \frac{6}{5}(x^2 + y)$ on the unit square. Find $f_X(x)$
and $f_Y(y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f_X(x) = \tfrac{6}{5} \int_0^1 (x^2 + y) \thinspace dy = \tfrac{6}{5} \left( x^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} \right), \quad 0 \le x \le 1$$

$$f_Y(y) = \tfrac{6}{5} \int_0^1 (x^2 + y) \thinspace dx = \tfrac{6}{5} \left( \tfrac{1}{3} + y \right), \quad 0 \le y \le 1$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A point is uniform on the triangle $0 \lt y \lt x \lt 1$, so
$f(x, y) = 2$ there. Find both marginals.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For fixed $x$, $y$ runs from 0 to $x$. For fixed $y$, $x$ runs from $y$ to 1:

$$f_X(x) = \int_0^x 2 \thinspace dy = 2x, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_y^1 2 \thinspace dx = 2(1 - y)$$

Both are on $(0, 1)$, and both integrate to 1.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Give two different joint tables for $X, Y \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$
whose marginals are both $(0.5, 0.5)$. What does this show?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Table A: every cell is $0.25$. Table B: $p(0, 0) = p(1, 1) = 0.5$ and $p(0, 1) = p(1, 0) = 0$. In both, every row and every column adds to $0.5$. In A the variables are unrelated; in B they are always equal. So the marginals do not determine the joint distribution.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $f(x, y) = e^{-y}$ for $0 \lt x \lt y$. Check that it is a valid
joint PDF and find both marginals. Name the distribution of $X$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For fixed $x \gt 0$, $y$ runs from $x$ to $\infty$. For fixed $y \gt 0$, $x$ runs from 0 to $y$:

$$f_X(x) = \int_x^{\infty} e^{-y} \thinspace dy = e^{-x}, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_0^y e^{-y} \thinspace dx = y e^{-y}$$

Since $\int_0^{\infty} e^{-x} \thinspace dx = 1$, the joint density integrates to 1, so it is valid. $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1). $Y$ has density $y e^{-y}$, which is $\text{Gamma}(2, 1)$ (shape 2, rate 1).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** One way to produce the density of Practice 5: draw
$X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$, then set $Y = X + E$ with a new $E \sim \text{Exp}(1)$.
Use this to check $P(X \le 1) = 1 - e^{-1}$ and $P(Y \le 1) = \int_0^1 y e^{-y} \thinspace dy = 1 - 2e^{-1}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# X ~ Exp(1), then Y = X + (another Exp(1)) gives f(x, y) = e^(-y) on 0 < x < y.
x = rng.exponential(1.0, n_trials)
y = x + rng.exponential(1.0, n_trials)

print(f"P(X <= 1): simulation {np.mean(x <= 1):.4f}, exact {1 - np.exp(-1):.4f}")
print(f"P(Y <= 1): simulation {np.mean(y <= 1):.4f}, exact {1 - 2 * np.exp(-1):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X <= 1): simulation 0.6324, exact 0.6321
P(Y <= 1): simulation 0.2647, exact 0.2642
```

</details>

## Summary

- The **marginal** distribution of $X$ is the distribution of $X$ alone.
- Discrete: add each row (or column) of the joint table.
- Continuous: integrate the joint density over the other variable, with limits read
  from the region for a **fixed** value.
- Marginals are always valid distributions, but they lose the information about how
  $X$ and $Y$ are related.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dy \qquad p_X(x) = \sum_{y} p_{X,Y}(x, y)}
$$
