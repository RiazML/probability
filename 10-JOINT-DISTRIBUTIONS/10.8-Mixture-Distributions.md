# 10.8 Mixture Distributions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 10: Joint Distributions](README.md) / 10.8 Mixture Distributions

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [10.7 Bayes for Random Variables](10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)

> **Big idea:** A mixture distribution comes from a two-step experiment: first pick one of several groups at random, then draw a value from that group's distribution.

---

## Why It Matters

Real data often come from several groups mixed together: fast and slow requests,
two species of fish, honest and fake reviews. No single standard distribution fits
such data, but a **mixture** of simple ones does. Mixtures explain data with two
peaks, data with heavy tails, and data with too many zeros. They are also the base
of clustering methods such as Gaussian mixture models.

## Intuition

A web server answers requests. If the data is in the cache (a **hit**, 80% of
requests), the answer takes about 1 ms. If not (a **miss**, 20%), the server must
read from disk, and the answer takes about 10 ms. Each response time is made in two
steps: first nature decides "hit or miss", then the time is drawn from the right
distribution.

If you look only at the times, without knowing which were hits, you see one
distribution with a big peak near 1 ms and a long tail out to 10 ms and beyond.
That combined distribution is a **mixture**. Its density is 80% of the "hit"
density plus 20% of the "miss" density.

Given a time, you can also ask backwards: "this request took 6 ms; was it a miss?"
That is Bayes' theorem from [10.7](10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md), with the
group as the unknown.

## Picture

```text
   step 1: pick a group C           step 2: draw X from that group

            +-- 0.7 --> group 1 -->  X ~ N(0, 1)
   start ---+
            +-- 0.3 --> group 2 -->  X ~ N(4, 1)

   result: f(x) = 0.7 x (N(0,1) density) + 0.3 x (N(4,1) density)

   f(x)
    |           ###
    |          #####
    |         #######
    |        #########
    |       ###########         ###
    |      #############     #########
    |    ###############################
    +----+-------+-------+-------+-------+------> x
        -2       0       2       4       6
                      ^
                mean 1.2 (not at either peak)
```

A mixture of two normal bells gives a density with two peaks, a tall one at 0 and a lower one at 4. Its mean, 1.2, is at neither peak.

## Definition

Let $f_1, \dots, f_K$ be densities (or PMFs), called the **components**, and let
$\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K$ be **mixing weights**. A random variable $X$ has a **mixture
distribution** if its density is:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k f_k(x), \qquad \pi_k \ge 0, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1
$$

where $\pi_k$ is the probability of picking component $k$ and $f_k$ is the
distribution of $X$ inside component $k$.

**In words:** the mixture density is a weighted average of the component densities,
with weights that add up to 1.

The **two-step story** says the same thing with a hidden label $C$:

1. Pick the component: $P(C = k) = \pi_k$.
2. Given $C = k$, draw $X$ from $f_k$: $f_{X \mid C}(x \mid k) = f_k(x)$.

Then $f(x)$ is the **marginal** of $X$ after summing out $C$. Three useful formulas
follow, where $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k^2$ are the mean and variance of component $k$:

$$
E[X] = \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \sum_{k} \pi_k \sigma_k^2 + \sum_{k} \pi_k (\mu_k - \mu)^2, \qquad P(C = k \mid X = x) = \frac{\pi_k f_k(x)}{f(x)}
$$

where $\mu = E[X]$.

**In words:** the mean is the weighted mean of the group means. The variance is the
average spread **inside** the groups plus the spread **between** the group means.
The last formula, called the **responsibility** of group $k$, is Bayes' theorem: how
likely each group is, given the value.

The components can be of any type. A point mass at 0 mixed with a continuous
density gives the **mixed distributions** of
[8.9 Mixed Distributions](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md).

## Why It Is True

**The density.** By the multiplication rule, the joint of $(C, X)$ is
$P(C = k) f_{X \mid C}(x \mid k) = \pi_k f_k(x)$. Sum out $C$ to get the marginal of $X$
([10.3](10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md)):
$f(x) = \sum_{k} \pi_k f_k(x)$. It is a valid density, because
$\int f = \sum_{k} \pi_k \int f_k = \sum_{k} \pi_k = 1$.

**The mean and the variance.** Use the expectation of a density
([8.5 Expectation and Variance with Integrals](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md)),
and swap the sum and the integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \int x \sum_{k} \pi_k f_k(x) \thinspace dx = \sum_{k} \pi_k \int x f_k(x) \thinspace dx = \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k && \text{(mean of each part)} \\
E[X^2] &= \sum_{k} \pi_k \int x^2 f_k(x) \thinspace dx = \sum_{k} \pi_k (\sigma_k^2 + \mu_k^2) && \text{(since } E[Y^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2 \text{)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X^2] - \mu^2 = \sum_{k} \pi_k \sigma_k^2 + \Big( \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k^2 - \mu^2 \Big) && \text{(regroup)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The bracket equals $\sum_{k} \pi_k (\mu_k - \mu)^2$; Practice 5 shows this. That gives
the "inside plus between" form. It is a special case of the law of total variance,
[14.4 Law of Total Variance](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md).

**The responsibilities** are Bayes' theorem with prior $\pi_k$ and likelihood $f_k(x)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Cache Hits and Misses

**Problem.** A response time $T$ (in ms) is $\text{Exp}(1)$ for a cache hit
(probability $0.8$) and $\text{Exp}(0.1)$ for a miss (probability $0.2$). Here the
parameter is the rate, so the means are 1 ms and 10 ms. Find $E[T]$, $P(T \gt 5)$,
and $P(\text{miss} \mid T \gt 5)$.

**Solution.**

1. Mean, as a weighted mean of the group means:

$$
E[T] = 0.8 \times 1 + 0.2 \times 10 = 0.8 + 2 = 2.8 \text{ ms}
$$

2. Tail probability. For $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, $P(T \gt t) = e^{-\lambda t}$. Mix the two tails:

$$
P(T \gt 5) = 0.8 \thinspace e^{-5} + 0.2 \thinspace e^{-0.5} \approx 0.8 \times 0.006738 + 0.2 \times 0.6065 = 0.00539 + 0.12131 = 0.1267
$$

3. Bayes, with the event $T \gt 5$ as the data:

$$
P(\text{miss} \mid T \gt 5) = \frac{0.2 \thinspace e^{-0.5}}{P(T \gt 5)} = \frac{0.12131}{0.12670} \approx 0.957
$$

**Answer.** The mean is $2.8$ ms, about 12.7% of requests take over 5 ms, and about
96% of those slow requests are cache misses.

**Check.** Misses are only 20% of requests, but they cause almost all the slow ones.
To make the tail faster, fix the misses. The simulation in Practice 6 agrees.

### Example 2: Two Peaks

**Problem.** A machine sensor reads $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ when the machine is idle
(70% of the time) and $\mathcal{N}(4, 1)$ when it runs (30%). (The second parameter is
the variance.) Find the mean, the variance and $P(X \gt 2)$ of a reading.

**Solution.**

1. Mean:

$$
\mu = 0.7 \times 0 + 0.3 \times 4 = 1.2
$$

2. Variance, inside plus between. Both groups have $\sigma_k^2 = 1$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = (0.7 \times 1 + 0.3 \times 1) + \left( 0.7 \times (0 - 1.2)^2 + 0.3 \times (4 - 1.2)^2 \right) = 1 + (1.008 + 2.352) = 4.36
$$

3. Tail. Use the standard normal CDF $\Phi$: $P(\mathcal{N}(0, 1) \gt 2) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 0.02275$,
   and $P(\mathcal{N}(4, 1) \gt 2) = 1 - \Phi(-2) \approx 0.97725$:

$$
P(X \gt 2) = 0.7 \times 0.02275 + 0.3 \times 0.97725 = 0.01593 + 0.29318 \approx 0.3091
$$

**Answer.** $E[X] = 1.2$, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4.36$, $P(X \gt 2) \approx 0.309$.

**Check.** The variance $4.36$ is much bigger than the variance 1 inside each group.
Most of it comes from the gap between the two peaks. And at $x = 2$, exactly halfway
between the peaks, both densities are equal, so the responsibility of "running" there
is just its prior, $0.3$.

## Simulate It

The code samples the mixture of Example 2 with the two-step story. It also shows the
classic mistake: a weighted **sum** $0.7 X_1 + 0.3 X_2$ is not a mixture.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Two-step story: pick the component, then draw from it.
running = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.3              # component 2 with weight 0.3
x = rng.normal(np.where(running, 4.0, 0.0), 1.0)  # N(4, 1) or N(0, 1)

exact_tail = 0.7 * norm.sf(2, 0, 1) + 0.3 * norm.sf(2, 4, 1)
print(f"E[X]:     simulation {x.mean():.4f}, exact {1.2:.4f}")
print(f"Var(X):   simulation {x.var():.4f}, exact {4.36:.4f}")
print(f"P(X > 2): simulation {np.mean(x > 2):.4f}, exact {exact_tail:.4f}")

# The common mistake: a weighted SUM of the two variables is a different thing.
wrong = 0.7 * rng.normal(0, 1, n_trials) + 0.3 * rng.normal(4, 1, n_trials)
print(f"Weighted sum: mean {wrong.mean():.4f}, variance {wrong.var():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[X]:     simulation 1.2007, exact 1.2000
Var(X):   simulation 4.3584, exact 4.3600
P(X > 2): simulation 0.3091, exact 0.3091
Weighted sum: mean 1.2008, variance 0.5798
```

The weighted sum has the same mean, but its variance is only about $0.58$ and it has
one peak, not two. A mixture **chooses** one component; a sum **adds** them.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing densities versus adding variables.** $f(x) = 0.7 f_1(x) + 0.3 f_2(x)$ is a
  mixture. $X = 0.7 X_1 + 0.3 X_2$ is a weighted sum, with a different distribution
  (see the simulation). Sums are the topic of
  [13.4 Convolution Sums of Random Variables](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md).
- **Mixing the variances like the means.** $\operatorname{Var}(X) \ne \sum_{k} \pi_k \sigma_k^2$.
  You must add the "between groups" part, which is often the bigger part.
- **Thinking the mean is the most likely value.** In Example 2 the mean is $1.2$,
  but the most likely readings are near 0 and near 4. A single number cannot
  describe a distribution with two peaks.
- **Weights that do not add to 1.** Then $f$ is not a density. Always check
  $\sum_{k} \pi_k = 1$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Gaussian mixture models cluster data by fitting
  $\sum_{k} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$, and the responsibilities give soft
  cluster labels. Mixture density networks and many generative models use mixtures
  ([21.1.4 Generative Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** response times (Example 1), packet sizes on a network (small
  control packets and large data packets), and spam scores that come from two kinds
  of email.
- **Statistics:** a mixture of normals with different variances has heavier tails
  than one normal (Practice 4). See
  [9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md).
- **Insurance:** most customers claim 0 in a year, a few claim a random amount. That
  is a mixture of a point mass at 0 and a continuous claim size.
- **Everyday life:** your commute time is a mixture of "normal day" and "accident on
  the road" days.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** With probability $0.5$, $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, otherwise
$X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 2)$. Find $P(X \le 0.5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \le 0.5) = 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times \tfrac{0.5}{2} = 0.25 + 0.125 = 0.375$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A visitor buys nothing with probability $0.6$ (just browsing).
Otherwise the number of purchases is $\text{Pois}(2)$ (mean 2). Find $P(\text{0 purchases})$
and the mean number of purchases.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The browsing group always gives 0, and $\text{Pois}(2)$ gives 0 with probability $e^{-2}$:

$$P(0) = 0.6 \times 1 + 0.4 \times e^{-2} \approx 0.6 + 0.0541 = 0.654$$

$$E[\text{purchases}] = 0.6 \times 0 + 0.4 \times 2 = 0.8$$

This is called a **zero-inflated** Poisson.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the cache of Example 1, find $\operatorname{Var}(T)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, $\sigma^2 = 1 / \lambda^2$, so the variances are 1 and 100. Inside part: $0.8 \times 1 + 0.2 \times 100 = 20.8$. Between part, with $\mu = 2.8$:

$$0.8 \times (1 - 2.8)^2 + 0.2 \times (10 - 2.8)^2 = 0.8 \times 3.24 + 0.2 \times 51.84 = 2.592 + 10.368 = 12.96$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(T) = 20.8 + 12.96 = 33.76$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X$ be $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ or $\mathcal{N}(0, 9)$ with probability
$\frac{1}{2}$ each. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Then compare $P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 6)$ with
the same probability for $\mathcal{N}(0, 5)$. (Use $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 2) \approx 0.0455$,
$P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 2.683) \approx 0.0073$, and $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 6) \approx 0$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Both means are 0, so there is no "between" part: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{2} \times 1 + \frac{1}{2} \times 9 = 5$. For the tail, $\lvert X \rvert \gt 6$ means $\lvert Z \rvert \gt 6$ in the first group and $\lvert Z \rvert \gt 2$ in the second (standard deviation 3):

$$P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 6) \approx 0.5 \times 0 + 0.5 \times 0.0455 \approx 0.0228$$

For $\mathcal{N}(0, 5)$, $P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 6) = P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 6 / \sqrt{5}) = P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 2.683) \approx 0.0073$. Same variance, but the mixture is about 3 times more likely to give a value beyond 6: it has heavier tails.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $\sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k^2 - \mu^2 = \sum_{k} \pi_k (\mu_k - \mu)^2$, where
$\mu = \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k$. This completes the proof of the variance formula.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand the square and use $\sum_{k} \pi_k = 1$ and $\sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k = \mu$:

$$\sum_{k} \pi_k (\mu_k - \mu)^2 = \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k^2 - 2\mu \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k + \mu^2 \sum_{k} \pi_k = \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k^2 - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 = \sum_{k} \pi_k \mu_k^2 - \mu^2$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 1 and Practice 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Cache hit (prob 0.8): Exp with mean 1 ms. Miss (prob 0.2): Exp with mean 10 ms.
miss = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.2
t = rng.exponential(np.where(miss, 10.0, 1.0))

slow = t > 5
exact_slow = 0.8 * np.exp(-5) + 0.2 * np.exp(-0.5)
print(f"P(T > 5):        simulation {slow.mean():.4f}, exact {exact_slow:.4f}")
print(f"P(miss | T > 5): simulation {miss[slow].mean():.4f}, exact {0.2 * np.exp(-0.5) / exact_slow:.4f}")
print(f"Var(T):          simulation {t.var():.2f}, exact {33.76:.2f}")
```

```text
P(T > 5):        simulation 0.1265, exact 0.1267
P(miss | T > 5): simulation 0.9578, exact 0.9575
Var(T):          simulation 33.83, exact 33.76
```

</details>

## Summary

- A **mixture** is a two-step experiment: pick component $k$ with probability
  $\pi_k$, then draw from $f_k$. Its density is the weighted average $\sum_{k} \pi_k f_k$.
- It is the marginal of $X$ in the joint distribution of (label, value).
- Mean: weighted mean of the means. Variance: inside part plus between part.
- Given a value, the chance of each component (its responsibility) comes from Bayes.
- A mixture of distributions is **not** a weighted sum of random variables.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k f_k(x), \qquad \pi_k \ge 0, \ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1}
$$
