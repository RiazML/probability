# 15.4 Uniqueness Theorem

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 15: Generating Functions](README.md) / 15.4 Uniqueness Theorem

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [15.3 MGF of Sums](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)

> **Big idea:** If two random variables have the same MGF on an interval around $0$, they have the same distribution; so an MGF is a fingerprint, and if MGFs converge then distributions converge, which proves the Central Limit Theorem.

---

## Why It Matters

In [15.3](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md) we multiplied MGFs and then said, "this is the MGF of a
Poisson, so the sum is Poisson." That last step needs a theorem: **an MGF belongs to
only one distribution**. Without it, a product of MGFs would tell us nothing about the
sum. A second theorem, the **continuity theorem**, says that when MGFs get closer and
closer to one MGF, the distributions get closer to one distribution. Together they give
a short proof of the Poisson limit and of the Central Limit Theorem. Module 17 uses the
proof in Example 3 below.

## Intuition

A fingerprint identifies a person: two different people never have the same one. We want
the same for distributions. What could play the role of the fingerprint?

The mean and variance are too short: many shapes share them. What about **all** the
moments $E[X], E[X^2], E[X^3], \dots$ together? Surprisingly, that is still not
enough. There are two different distributions with exactly the same moments of every
order. The famous example is built from the log-normal (we construct it below).

The MGF is a better fingerprint, because it records the distribution in a stronger way.
Look at a discrete variable on $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$:

$$
M(t) = p_0 + p_1 e^{t} + p_2 e^{2t} + p_3 e^{3t} + \cdots
$$

This is a polynomial (or power series) in $u = e^t$, with the probabilities as
coefficients. Two polynomials that agree on a whole interval of values of $u$ must have
the same coefficients. So the probabilities are recovered, one by one. For continuous
variables the same idea holds, but the proof is harder.

## Picture

```text
   information about X                  fixes the distribution of X?
   -----------------------------------  ------------------------------------
   mean and variance                    no   (many shapes share them)
   all moments E[X^n]                   not always   (log-normal, below)
   MGF finite near t = 0                yes          (this topic)
   characteristic function (15.6)       yes, always
   the CDF F(x)                         yes          (it is the definition)
```

Each row is a stronger piece of information than the one above it. The MGF, when it
exists, is strong enough.

## Definition

**Theorem 1 (uniqueness).** Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables whose MGFs are finite on
an open interval $(-h, h)$ with $h \gt 0$. If

$$
M_X(t) = M_Y(t) \quad \text{for all } t \in (-h, h)
$$

then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution: $F_X(x) = F_Y(x)$ for all $x$.

**Theorem 2 (continuity).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ and $X$ be random variables whose MGFs are finite on a
common interval $(-h, h)$. If $M_{X_n}(t) \to M_X(t)$ for every $t$ in $(-h, h)$, then $X_n$
**converges in distribution** to $X$, written $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$. This means
$F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x)$ at every point $x$ where $F_X$ is continuous
([17.1](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)).

**In words:** the MGF determines the distribution, and if the MGFs converge, so do the
distributions.

> **Note:** Both theorems need the MGF to exist near $0$. Equality at the single point
> $t = 0$ means nothing, since $M(0) = 1$ for every variable. For random variables
> without an MGF, use the characteristic function ([15.6](15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)),
> for which the same two theorems hold with no condition.

## Why It Is True

### A Complete Proof for Whole-Number Values

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ take values in $\lbrace 0, 1, \dots, m \rbrace$, with PMFs $p_k$ and $q_k$. Put $u = e^t$.
The condition $M_X(t) = M_Y(t)$ for all $t \in (-h, h)$ says

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{m} p_k u^k = \sum_{k=0}^{m} q_k u^k \quad \text{for all } u \in (e^{-h}, e^{h})
$$

Subtract: the polynomial $\sum_{k=0}^{m} (p_k - q_k) u^k$ of degree at most $m$ is zero at every point of
an interval, so it has infinitely many roots. A non-zero polynomial of degree $m$ has at
most $m$ roots, so all its coefficients are zero: $p_k = q_k$ for every $k$. For infinitely many
values the same argument works with power series (a power series that is zero on an
interval has all coefficients zero), which is also how the PGF works
([15.5](15.5-Probability-Generating-Function.md)).

### The General Case (Idea)

For a general random variable the proof needs complex numbers. The function $t \mapsto M_X(t)$
can be extended to complex $t = s + iu$, and the part with $s = 0$ is the characteristic
function $E[e^{iuX}]$ ([15.6](15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)), which determines the distribution
through an inversion formula. We do not prove it here. The proof of the continuity
theorem is also beyond this book.

### Moments Alone Are Not Enough

Let $X$ be log-normal: $X = e^{Y}$ with $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Its moments are
$E[X^k] = E[e^{kY}] = e^{k^2/2}$ ([15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)). Let $\varphi(y)$ be the standard normal
density. Define a second variable $W = e^{V}$, where $V$ has the density

$$
f_V(y) = \varphi(y) \big( 1 + \sin(2\pi y) \big)
$$

This is a valid density: it is never negative, because $\lvert \sin \rvert \le 1$. We show that $W$ has the
same moments as $X$ for every whole number $k \ge 0$. The extra part contributes

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{ky} \varphi(y) \sin(2\pi y) \thinspace dy
$$

Complete the square: $e^{ky} \varphi(y) = e^{k^2/2} \varphi(y - k)$. Substitute $u = y - k$:

$$
e^{k^2/2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \varphi(u) \sin(2\pi u + 2\pi k) \thinspace du = e^{k^2/2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \varphi(u) \sin(2\pi u) \thinspace du = 0
$$

The first equality holds because $k$ is a whole number, so adding $2\pi k$ inside the sine
changes nothing. The integral is $0$ because $\varphi(u) \sin(2\pi u)$ is an odd function. For
$k = 0$ the same calculation shows the density has total area 1. So $E[W^k] = E[X^k]$ for
every $k$, yet $W$ and $X$ have different distributions. No MGF exists for either
of them, which is why Theorem 1 does not apply.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Reading a Distribution from Its MGF

**Problem.** Each MGF below belongs to a known distribution. Name it and answer the question.
(a) $M(t) = (0.3 + 0.7e^t)^4$: find $P(X = 2)$. (b) $M(t) = 0.2 + 0.5e^{t} + 0.3e^{3t}$:
find the PMF and $E[X]$. (c) $M(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-3}$ for $t \lt \frac{1}{2}$: find the mean and variance.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The Binomial MGF is $(1 - p + pe^t)^n$. Match: $1 - p = 0.3$, so $p = 0.7$ and $n = 4$. So $X \sim \text{Bin}(4, 0.7)$:

$$
P(X = 2) = \binom{4}{2} (0.7)^2 (0.3)^2 = 6 \times 0.49 \times 0.09 = 0.2646
$$

2. (b) The MGF is a sum of terms $p_k e^{kt}$. So $X$ takes the value $0$ with probability $0.2$, the value $1$
   with probability $0.5$, and the value $3$ with probability $0.3$. The probabilities add up to $1$. The mean is
   $E[X] = 0 \times 0.2 + 1 \times 0.5 + 3 \times 0.3 = 1.4$.
3. (c) The Gamma MGF is $\left( \frac{\beta}{\beta - t} \right)^{\alpha} = \left( \frac{1}{1 - t/\beta} \right)^{\alpha}$. Here $\frac{1}{\beta} = 2$, so
   $\beta = \frac{1}{2}$, and $\alpha = 3$. So $X \sim \text{Gamma}(3, \frac{1}{2})$ with rate $\frac{1}{2}$. Mean $\frac{\alpha}{\beta} = 6$ and variance
   $\frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} = 12$.

**Answer.** (a) $0.2646$. (b) $P(X = 0, 1, 3) = 0.2, 0.5, 0.3$ and $E[X] = 1.4$. (c) Mean $6$, variance $12$.

**Check.** In (a) and (b) we can also differentiate: for (b), $M'(0) = 0.5 + 0.9 = 1.4$. In (c),
$M(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-3}$ gives $M'(0) = 3 \times 2 = 6$, the same mean.

### Example 2: The Poisson Limit with MGFs

**Problem.** Let $X_n \sim \text{Bin}(n, \frac{\lambda}{n})$. Show that $X_n \xrightarrow{d} \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ as $n \to \infty$.
This is the "law of rare events" ([7.10](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.10-Poisson-Approximation-to-Binomial.md),
[17.10](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md)).

**Solution.**

1. The Binomial MGF with $p = \frac{\lambda}{n}$ is, after writing $1 - p + pe^t = 1 + p(e^t - 1)$:

$$
M_{X_n}(t) = \left( 1 + \frac{\lambda (e^t - 1)}{n} \right)^n
$$

2. Use the limit $\left( 1 + \frac{a}{n} \right)^n \to e^{a}$ with $a = \lambda(e^t - 1)$, for every fixed $t$:

$$
M_{X_n}(t) \to e^{\lambda (e^t - 1)}
$$

3. This is the MGF of $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$, finite for all $t$. By the continuity theorem, $X_n \xrightarrow{d} \text{Pois}(\lambda)$.

**Answer.** The binomial with $n$ large and $p = \frac{\lambda}{n}$ converges to the Poisson with mean $\lambda$.

**Check.** Take $\lambda = 3$ and $t = 0.5$. The limit is $\exp(3(e^{0.5} - 1)) = 7.0018$. The MGF of
$\text{Bin}(n, 3/n)$ at $0.5$ is $5.919$ for $n = 10$, $6.872$ for $n = 100$ and $6.989$ for
$n = 1000$: it creeps up to $7.0018$.

### Example 3: Proof of the Central Limit Theorem with MGFs

**Problem.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 \gt 0$, and assume their MGF
exists near $0$. Let $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ and
$Z_n = \dfrac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}}$. Prove that $Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

**Solution.**

1. **Standardize each term.** Let $Y_i = \frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma}$. These are i.i.d. with $E[Y_i] = 0$ and
   $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = 1$, so $E[Y_i^2] = 1$. Let $M_Y$ be their common MGF. Then
   $Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} (Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n)$.
2. **Use the product rule** from [15.3](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md), with the scaling $a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$:

$$
M_{Z_n}(t) = \left[ M_Y\left( \frac{t}{\sqrt{n}} \right) \right]^n
$$

3. **Expand $M_Y$ near 0** using [15.2](15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md): $M_Y(0) = 1$, $M_Y'(0) = E[Y] = 0$ and
   $M_Y''(0) = E[Y^2] = 1$. By Taylor's theorem

$$
M_Y(s) = 1 + \frac{s^2}{2} + r(s), \qquad \text{where } \frac{r(s)}{s^2} \to 0 \text{ as } s \to 0
$$

4. **Put $s = \frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}$.** Then $s^2 = \frac{t^2}{n}$, so
   $M_Y\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\big) = 1 + \frac{1}{n}\left( \frac{t^2}{2} + \varepsilon_n \right)$, where
   $\varepsilon_n = n \thinspace r\big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\big) = t^2 \cdot \frac{r(s)}{s^2} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ (because $s \to 0$).
5. **Take the limit.** With $a_n = \frac{t^2}{2} + \varepsilon_n \to \frac{t^2}{2}$, and the fact that
   $\left( 1 + \frac{a_n}{n} \right)^n \to e^{a}$ when $a_n \to a$:

$$
M_{Z_n}(t) = \left( 1 + \frac{a_n}{n} \right)^n \to e^{t^2/2}
$$

6. **Recognize the limit.** $e^{t^2/2}$ is the MGF of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ ([15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)),
   and it is finite for all $t$. By the continuity theorem, $Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

**Answer.** The standardized sum of i.i.d. variables with a finite variance (and an MGF) tends to the standard normal.

**Check.** Take $X_i \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and $t = 0.5$. Here $M_{Z_n}(0.5) = \left( \frac{e^{-s}}{1 - s} \right)^n$ with $s = \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{n}}$.
It is $1.2131$ for $n = 1$, $1.1502$ for $n = 10$ and $1.1381$ for $n = 100$, and tends to
$e^{0.125} = 1.1331$. The simulation below shows the same numbers.

> **Note:** This proof assumes the MGF exists. The final CLT in
> [17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md) needs only a finite variance, and the same
> six steps work with the characteristic function ([15.6](15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)) in place of the MGF.

## Simulate It

Part 1 computes the exact MGF of the standardized sum of $n$ exponential variables and
watches it approach $e^{t^2/2}$. Part 2 checks numerically that the log-normal and the
"wiggly" density share their moments (with $y = \ln x$, so the moment is $\int e^{ky} f(y) \thinspace dy$).

```python
import numpy as np

# Part 1: MGF of the standardized sum of n Exp(1) draws converges to exp(t^2/2).
t = 0.5
for n in [1, 10, 100, 10_000]:
    s = t / np.sqrt(n)
    print(f"n = {n:>6}: M_Zn(0.5) = {(np.exp(-s) / (1 - s)) ** n:.4f}")
print(f"limit exp(t^2/2)     = {np.exp(t**2 / 2):.4f}")

# Part 2: the log-normal and the "wiggly" density have the same moments (y = ln x).
y = np.linspace(-12, 12, 2_400_001)
phi = np.exp(-y**2 / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)
dy = y[1] - y[0]
for k in range(5):
    plain = np.sum(np.exp(k * y) * phi) * dy
    wiggly = np.sum(np.exp(k * y) * phi * (1 + np.sin(2 * np.pi * y))) * dy
    print(f"k = {k}: log-normal {plain:9.3f}, wiggly {wiggly:9.3f}, exact {np.exp(k**2 / 2):9.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =      1: M_Zn(0.5) = 1.2131
n =     10: M_Zn(0.5) = 1.1502
n =    100: M_Zn(0.5) = 1.1381
n =  10000: M_Zn(0.5) = 1.1336
limit exp(t^2/2)     = 1.1331
k = 0: log-normal     1.000, wiggly     1.000, exact     1.000
k = 1: log-normal     1.649, wiggly     1.649, exact     1.649
k = 2: log-normal     7.389, wiggly     7.389, exact     7.389
k = 3: log-normal    90.017, wiggly    90.017, exact    90.017
k = 4: log-normal  2980.958, wiggly  2980.958, exact  2980.958
```

The two densities give identical moments for $k = 0, \dots, 4$ (and for all $k$), even though
they are different distributions. That is why moments alone are not a fingerprint.

## Common Mistakes

- **Believing that equal moments mean equal distributions.** The log-normal example shows
  this is false in general. It is true when an MGF exists, because then the moments
  give back the MGF through its series ([15.2](15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md)).
- **Matching the MGFs at a few points only.** The theorem needs equality on a whole interval
  around $0$. Equality at $t = 0$ is automatic, since $M(0) = 1$.
- **Identifying a distribution without checking all parameters.** A product like
  $e^{3(e^t-1)} e^{5(e^t-1)}$ is a Poisson MGF only after you add the exponents to get
  $\lambda = 8$. Match the form and the numbers.
- **Using the MGF proof of the CLT when no MGF exists.** For heavy-tailed variables, switch
  to the characteristic function. (For a Cauchy variable the CLT fails altogether, since
  the variance is infinite.)
- **Dropping the error term in the limit.** The step $\left(1 + \frac{a_n}{n}\right)^n \to e^{a}$ needs
  $a_n \to a$. In the CLT proof, $a_n$ includes the small term $\varepsilon_n$, which is why we use Taylor's
  theorem with a remainder.

## Where It Is Used

- **Identifying sums:** every "add the parameters" result in
  [13.5](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md) uses
  Theorem 1 as its last step.
- **Limit theorems:** the Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) and the
  Poisson limit ([17.10](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** in an exponential family, the log-MGF of the sufficient statistic
  determines the model, so two parameter values that give the same log-MGF are the same model.
- **Computer science:** proofs that a random algorithm's output (a sum of random pieces)
  has a given distribution, such as the sum of independent hash-bucket counts.
- **Statistics:** matching an observed distribution to a known family by its transform.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A variable has $M(t) = \left( \frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} e^t \right)^5$. Name its distribution and find $P(X = 5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is the Binomial MGF $(1 - p + pe^t)^n$ with $p = \frac{2}{3}$ and $n = 5$. So $X \sim \text{Bin}(5, \frac{2}{3})$, and

$$P(X = 5) = \left( \tfrac{2}{3} \right)^5 = \frac{32}{243} \approx 0.1317$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A variable has $M(t) = 0.25e^{-t} + 0.5 + 0.25e^{t}$. Find its PMF, its mean and its variance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each term $p_k e^{kt}$ gives a value $k$ with probability $p_k$: the values are $-1, 0, 1$ with probabilities $0.25, 0.5, 0.25$. The mean is $-0.25 + 0 + 0.25 = 0$ and $E[X^2] = 0.25 + 0 + 0.25 = 0.5$, so

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.5 - 0^2 = 0.5$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = 2X + 3$. Use MGFs to identify the distribution of $Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the shift-and-scale rule, $M_Y(t) = e^{3t} M_X(2t) = e^{3t} e^{(2t)^2/2} = e^{3t + 2t^2}$. A normal $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has $M(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}$. Matching: $\mu = 3$ and $\frac{\sigma^2}{2} = 2$, so $\sigma^2 = 4$:

$$Y \sim \mathcal{N}(3, 4)$$

</details>

**4. (Hard)** For each $n$, let $X_n \sim \text{Geom}(p_n)$ with $p_n = \frac{\lambda}{n}$ (trials until the first success). Show that $\frac{X_n}{n} \xrightarrow{d} \text{Exp}(\lambda)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The geometric MGF is $M_{X_n}(s) = \frac{p_n e^s}{1 - (1 - p_n)e^s}$. By the scaling rule, $M_{X_n/n}(t) = M_{X_n}(t/n)$. Put $s = \frac{t}{n}$ and use $e^{s} = 1 + \frac{t}{n} + O(n^{-2})$.

Numerator: $p_n e^{s} = \frac{\lambda}{n} \left( 1 + O(\tfrac{1}{n}) \right)$.

Denominator: $1 - \left( 1 - \frac{\lambda}{n} \right) \left( 1 + \frac{t}{n} + O(n^{-2}) \right) = \frac{\lambda - t}{n} + O(n^{-2})$.

Divide, for fixed $t \lt \lambda$:

$$M_{X_n/n}(t) = \frac{\frac{\lambda}{n}\left(1 + O(\frac{1}{n})\right)}{\frac{\lambda - t}{n}\left(1 + O(\frac{1}{n})\right)} \to \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}$$

This is the MGF of $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ for $t \lt \lambda$, so by the continuity theorem $\frac{X_n}{n} \xrightarrow{d} \text{Exp}(\lambda)$. Meaning: waiting for a rare event, measured in units of $n$ trials, becomes an exponential waiting time.

</details>

**5. (Simulate)** Let $S_n$ be the number of heads in $n$ fair coin flips and $Z_n = \frac{S_n - n/2}{\sqrt{n}/2}$. The exact MGF is
$M_{Z_n}(t) = \operatorname{cosh}(t/\sqrt{n})^n$. For $t = 1$ and $n = 1, 4, 16, 100$, compare the average of $e^{Z_n}$ with this formula and with the limit $e^{1/2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, t = 500_000, 1.0

for n in [1, 4, 16, 100]:
    heads = rng.binomial(n, 0.5, n_trials)
    z = (heads - n / 2) / (np.sqrt(n) / 2)          # standardized number of heads
    exact = np.cosh(t / np.sqrt(n)) ** n            # M_Zn(t) = cosh(t / sqrt(n))^n
    print(f"n = {n:>3}: simulation {np.mean(np.exp(t * z)):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"limit exp(t^2/2) = {np.exp(t**2 / 2):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =   1: simulation 1.5411, exact 1.5431
n =   4: simulation 1.6187, exact 1.6168
n =  16: simulation 1.6368, exact 1.6403
n = 100: simulation 1.6479, exact 1.6474
limit exp(t^2/2) = 1.6487
```

</details>

## Summary

- **Uniqueness:** if two MGFs are equal on an open interval around $0$, the distributions are equal.
- **Continuity:** if MGFs converge on such an interval, the distributions converge.
- Moments alone do not fix a distribution (the log-normal has a twin with the same moments); the MGF does.
- Together with the product rule, this proves the Poisson limit and the Central Limit Theorem:
  $M_{Z_n}(t) = M_Y(t/\sqrt{n})^n \to e^{t^2/2}$.
- Without an MGF, use the characteristic function, which always exists.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{M_X = M_Y \text{ near } 0 \implies X \text{ and } Y \text{ have the same distribution}}
$$
