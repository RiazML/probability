# 15.3 MGF of Sums

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 15: Generating Functions](README.md) / 15.3 MGF of Sums

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [15.2 Moments from the MGF](15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md)

> **Big idea:** For independent $X$ and $Y$, the MGF of the sum is the product of the MGFs, $M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(t)$, so the hard operation (convolution) becomes easy multiplication.

---

## Why It Matters

Totals are everywhere: the sum of dice, the total delay over several network hops,
the total score of a team. In [13.4](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md)
we found the distribution of $X + Y$ with a convolution, a sum or an integral that gets
long fast. In [13.5](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md)
we listed which families stay in the family when you add them, but the proofs were
long. With MGFs, every proof on that list takes two or three lines. The same
product rule is the first half of the proof of the Central Limit Theorem
([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)).

## Intuition

Roll two dice. To count the ways to get a total of 7, we added up pairs: $(1,6), (2,5), \dots, (6,1)$.
Now write each die as a sum of exponentials: the MGF of one die is
$\frac{1}{6}(e^{t} + e^{2t} + \cdots + e^{6t})$, where each term $\frac{1}{6} e^{kt}$ stands for the value $k$ with probability $\frac{1}{6}$.

Multiply two such terms: $\frac{1}{6}e^{at} \cdot \frac{1}{6}e^{bt} = \frac{1}{36} e^{(a+b)t}$. The exponents
**add** (the values add) and the coefficients **multiply** (the probabilities multiply,
because the dice are independent). That is exactly what a convolution does by hand.
When we multiply the two MGFs and collect the terms, the coefficient of $e^{7t}$ is
$\frac{1}{36}$ times the number of pairs $(a, b)$ with $a + b = 7$, which is $\frac{6}{36}$.
The algebra of exponents does the bookkeeping for us.

## Picture

```text
              the hard way (13.4)
   f_X, f_Y -------------------------> f_(X+Y)
      |            convolution              ^
      | take MGFs                           | uniqueness (15.4):
      v                                     | same MGF, same distribution
   M_X, M_Y -------------------------> M_X(t) * M_Y(t)  =  M_(X+Y)(t)
                   multiply
              the easy way (this topic)
```

Going around the bottom path (take MGFs, multiply, recognize the answer) is usually
shorter than the top path.

## Definition

**Theorem (MGF of a sum).** Let $X$ and $Y$ be **independent** random variables whose MGFs
exist on a common interval around $0$. Then, on that interval,

$$
M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t) \thinspace M_Y(t)
$$

By induction, for independent $X_1, \dots, X_n$:

$$
M_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} M_{X_i}(t)
$$

If they are also identically distributed (i.i.d.) with common MGF $M$, then
$M_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = M(t)^n$, and the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ satisfies
$M_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = M(t/n)^n$. For constants $a$ and $b$ (and $X$, $Y$ independent):

$$
M_{aX + bY}(t) = M_X(at) \thinspace M_Y(bt)
$$

**In words:** to add independent variables, multiply their MGFs. To scale a
variable by $a$, replace $t$ by $at$ inside its MGF.

## Why It Is True

**The product rule.** Write $e^{t(X+Y)} = e^{tX} e^{tY}$. Because $X$ and $Y$ are independent,
the new variables $e^{tX}$ and $e^{tY}$ are independent too (functions of independent
variables stay independent, [10.5](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)),
and for independent variables the expectation of a product is the product of the
expectations ([11.4](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{X+Y}(t) &= E\big[e^{t(X+Y)}\big] && \text{(definition)} \\
&= E\big[e^{tX} e^{tY}\big] && \text{(} e^{a+b} = e^a e^b \text{)} \\
&= E\big[e^{tX}\big] \thinspace E\big[e^{tY}\big] && \text{(independence)} \\
&= M_X(t) \thinspace M_Y(t) && \text{(definition)}
\end{aligned}
$$

If both factors are finite on an interval, so is the product. The formula for
$n$ variables follows by repeating the step. For the scaled version, apply the
shift-and-scale rule of [15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md) to each term:
$M_{aX}(t) = M_X(at)$ and $M_{bY}(t) = M_Y(bt)$, then multiply.

**Why convolution becomes multiplication (discrete case).** For independent integer-valued
$X$ and $Y$, the PMF of the sum is the convolution $p_{X+Y}(z) = \sum_x p_X(x) p_Y(z - x)$. Then:

$$
\sum_z e^{tz} p_{X+Y}(z) = \sum_x \sum_y e^{t(x+y)} p_X(x) p_Y(y) = \left( \sum_x e^{tx} p_X(x) \right) \left( \sum_y e^{ty} p_Y(y) \right)
$$

The double sum runs over all pairs $(x, y)$, which is the same as running over every
$z = x + y$ and every split of $z$. It factors because the weights $p_X(x) p_Y(y)$ factor.

**The Gamma MGF.** We need it for the examples. Let $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ with shape $\alpha$
and rate $\beta$, and take $t \lt \beta$. The density is $\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}$ for $x \gt 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X(t) &= \int_0^{\infty} e^{tx} \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x} \thinspace dx && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{(\beta - t)^{\alpha}} \int_0^{\infty} \frac{(\beta - t)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-(\beta - t)x} \thinspace dx && \text{(combine } e^{tx} e^{-\beta x} \text{)} \\
&= \left( \frac{\beta}{\beta - t} \right)^{\alpha} && \text{(a Gamma}(\alpha, \beta - t) \text{ density has area 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\alpha = 1$ this is the exponential MGF $\frac{\beta}{\beta - t}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Sum of Two Dice

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice. Use MGFs to find $P(\text{sum} = 7)$ and $P(\text{sum} = 2)$.

**Solution.**

1. One die has $M(t) = \frac{1}{6}(e^{t} + e^{2t} + e^{3t} + e^{4t} + e^{5t} + e^{6t})$.
2. The dice are independent, so the sum $S$ has

$$
M_S(t) = M(t)^2 = \frac{1}{36} \left( e^{t} + e^{2t} + \cdots + e^{6t} \right)^2
$$

3. Expand the square. A term $e^{at} \cdot e^{bt} = e^{(a+b)t}$ appears once for every ordered pair
   $(a, b)$ with $a, b \in \lbrace 1, \dots, 6 \rbrace$. The exponent $7t$ needs $a + b = 7$: the pairs
   $(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)$, so 6 pairs. The exponent $2t$ needs $a = b = 1$: 1 pair.
4. The coefficient of $e^{kt}$ is $P(S = k)$:

$$
P(S = 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad P(S = 2) = \frac{1}{36}
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{sum} = 7) = \frac{1}{6}$ and $P(\text{sum} = 2) = \frac{1}{36}$.

**Check.** The same answers as the counting in [1.1](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.1-What-is-Probability.md).
Also $M_S(0) = \frac{36}{36} = 1$, as it must be.

### Example 2: Calls at Two Centers

**Problem.** Calls reach center A as a $\text{Pois}(3)$ count per minute and center B as a
$\text{Pois}(5)$ count, independently (3 and 5 are the means). Show that the total is $\text{Pois}(8)$ and find the probability of exactly 10 calls in a minute.

**Solution.**

1. The MGFs are $M_A(t) = e^{3(e^t - 1)}$ and $M_B(t) = e^{5(e^t - 1)}$.
2. Multiply, and add the exponents:

$$
M_{A+B}(t) = e^{3(e^t - 1)} \cdot e^{5(e^t - 1)} = e^{(3 + 5)(e^t - 1)} = e^{8(e^t - 1)}
$$

3. This is the MGF of $\text{Pois}(8)$. By uniqueness ([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)), the
   total is $\text{Pois}(8)$.
4. Now the PMF with $\lambda = 8$ and $k = 10$:

$$
P(\text{total} = 10) = e^{-8} \frac{8^{10}}{10!} = 0.00033546 \times \frac{1{,}073{,}741{,}824}{3{,}628{,}800} \approx 0.00033546 \times 295.89 \approx 0.0993
$$

**Answer.** The total is $\text{Pois}(8)$, and $P(\text{total} = 10) \approx 0.0993$.

**Check.** The mean $3 + 5 = 8$ is right, and the variance should be $3 + 5 = 8$ as well, which
is the Poisson property. The simulation gives $0.0999$.

### Example 3: Three Tasks in a Row

**Problem.** A job runs three tasks one after another. Each task takes an $\text{Exp}(2)$ time
in seconds (rate 2, mean $0.5$ seconds), independently. Find the distribution of the
total time $T$ and the probability that $T \gt 2$ seconds.

**Solution.**

1. Each task has $M(t) = \frac{2}{2 - t}$ for $t \lt 2$.
2. The three are i.i.d., so

$$
M_T(t) = \left( \frac{2}{2 - t} \right)^3
$$

3. Compare with the Gamma MGF $\left( \frac{\beta}{\beta - t} \right)^{\alpha}$: it matches with $\alpha = 3$ and $\beta = 2$. So $T \sim \text{Gamma}(3, 2)$.
4. For a whole-number shape the tail is the Erlang formula ([9.4](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md)):

$$
P(T \gt x) = e^{-2x} \left( 1 + 2x + \frac{(2x)^2}{2} \right)
$$

5. At $x = 2$: $P(T \gt 2) = e^{-4}(1 + 4 + 8) = 13 e^{-4} \approx 13 \times 0.018316 \approx 0.2381$.

**Answer.** $T \sim \text{Gamma}(3, 2)$, and $P(T \gt 2) \approx 0.2381$.

**Check.** The mean total is $3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$ seconds, so exceeding 2 seconds is
not rare: about 24%. The simulation agrees.

## Simulate It

The code checks Examples 1 to 3 together and then shows why independence is needed.
For $U_1, U_2$ independent uniform on $(0, 1)$ the MGF of $U_1 + U_2$ is $M_U(0.5)^2$, but
for $U + U = 2U$ it is not.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import exp, factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Three independent tasks, each Exp(2): the sum is Gamma(3, 2).
total = rng.exponential(1 / 2, (n_trials, 3)).sum(axis=1)
print(f"M_sum(0.5):   simulation {np.mean(np.exp(0.5 * total)):.4f}, exact {(2 / 1.5) ** 3:.4f}")
print(f"P(sum > 2):   simulation {np.mean(total > 2):.4f}, exact {13 * exp(-4):.4f}")

# Pois(3) + Pois(5) should be Pois(8).
calls = rng.poisson(3, n_trials) + rng.poisson(5, n_trials)
print(f"P(calls = 10): simulation {np.mean(calls == 10):.4f}, exact {exp(-8) * 8**10 / factorial(10):.4f}")

# Two dice: the product of the two MGFs has the coefficient 6/36 at e^(7t).
dice = rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials) + rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials)
print(f"P(dice = 7):   simulation {np.mean(dice == 7):.4f}, exact {6 / 36:.4f}")

# X + X is NOT X + Y: for X ~ Bern(0.5), M_{2X}(t) differs from M_X(t)^2.
t = 0.5
print(f"M_X(t)^2 = {((1 + exp(t)) / 2) ** 2:.4f}   M_(2X)(t) = {(1 + exp(2 * t)) / 2:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
M_sum(0.5):   simulation 2.3685, exact 2.3704
P(sum > 2):   simulation 0.2384, exact 0.2381
P(calls = 10): simulation 0.0999, exact 0.0993
P(dice = 7):   simulation 0.1671, exact 0.1667
M_X(t)^2 = 1.7539   M_(2X)(t) = 1.8591
```

The last line shows the warning: a variable added to **itself** is not an independent
sum, and the product rule fails.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the product rule without independence.** For $X + X = 2X$ the correct MGF is
  $M_X(2t)$, not $M_X(t)^2$. In the last line of the output they are $1.8591$ and $1.7539$.
- **Adding MGFs instead of multiplying.** $M_X + M_Y$ is not the MGF of anything (at $t = 0$
  it equals 2). The average $\frac{1}{2}M_X + \frac{1}{2}M_Y$ is the MGF of a
  **mixture** ([10.8](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)), which is a different thing from a sum.
- **Forgetting to scale $t$ for a difference.** For independent $X, Y$:
  $M_{X-Y}(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(-t)$. It is not $M_X(t) / M_Y(t)$.
- **Adding the parameters that should match.** The product rule for Gamma variables works
  only for the same rate $\beta$: $\left(\frac{\beta_1}{\beta_1 - t}\right)^{\alpha_1} \left(\frac{\beta_2}{\beta_2 - t}\right)^{\alpha_2}$
  is not a Gamma MGF when $\beta_1 \ne \beta_2$.
- **Forgetting the common interval.** The product is valid only where both MGFs are finite.
  Check, for instance, $t \lt \lambda$ for exponentials.

## Where It Is Used

- **Probability:** the short proofs for all the rows of
  [13.5](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md) (binomial, Poisson,
  normal, Gamma, chi-square) and for [12.9](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md).
- **Limit theorems:** $M_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = M(t/n)^n$ is the starting point of the proof of the
  Central Limit Theorem ([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)).
- **Computer science:** the total latency of a pipeline of independent stages, the total
  work of many independent jobs, and the number of requests from many clients are all sums.
- **AI and machine learning:** a sum of independent Gaussian noise terms in a diffusion model
  is Gaussian, with the variances added, by this rule.
- **Insurance and finance:** the total claims of a portfolio of independent policies.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent $\text{Bern}(0.2)$. Find $M_{X+Y}(t)$ and name the distribution of $X + Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$M_{X+Y}(t) = (0.8 + 0.2e^t)(0.8 + 0.2e^t) = (0.8 + 0.2e^t)^2$$

This is the MGF of $\text{Bin}(2, 0.2)$, so $X + Y \sim \text{Bin}(2, 0.2)$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Pois}(2)$ and $Y \sim \text{Pois}(7)$ are independent. What is the distribution of
$X + Y$, and what is $P(X + Y = 0)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$M_{X+Y}(t) = e^{2(e^t - 1)} e^{7(e^t - 1)} = e^{9(e^t - 1)}$, so $X + Y \sim \text{Pois}(9)$. Then

$$P(X + Y = 0) = e^{-9} \approx 0.000123$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find the distribution of $X - Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$M_{X-Y}(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(-t) = e^{t^2/2} e^{(-t)^2/2} = e^{t^2}$. A normal $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ has MGF $e^{\sigma^2 t^2/2}$, so $\frac{\sigma^2}{2} = 1$ and $\sigma^2 = 2$:

$$X - Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$$

The variance is $1 + 1 = 2$, as the variance of a difference of independent variables should be.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and $\bar{X}_n$ their mean. Find
the distribution of $\bar{X}_n$, and its mean and variance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$M_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = M(t/n)^n = \left( \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t/n} \right)^n = \left( \frac{n\lambda}{n\lambda - t} \right)^n$$

This is the Gamma MGF with shape $n$ and rate $n\lambda$, so $\bar{X}_n \sim \text{Gamma}(n, n\lambda)$. Its mean is $\frac{n}{n\lambda} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$ and its variance is $\frac{n}{(n\lambda)^2} = \frac{1}{n\lambda^2}$. The variance of the mean is the variance of one term divided by $n$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Show that $M_{Z^2}(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-1/2}$ for $t \lt \frac{1}{2}$. Then show that
$Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2$, for independent standard normals, has MGF $(1 - 2t)^{-k/2}$, and that this is
$\text{Gamma}(\frac{k}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, the chi-square distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $t \lt \frac{1}{2}$, put $s^2 = \frac{1}{1 - 2t}$ (so $s \gt 0$):

$$M_{Z^2}(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tz^2} \frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \thinspace dz = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-(1-2t)z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \thinspace dz = s \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-z^2/(2s^2)}}{s\sqrt{2\pi}} \thinspace dz = s$$

because the last integrand is the $\mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$ density, with area 1. So $M_{Z^2}(t) = s = (1 - 2t)^{-1/2}$. The $k$ squares are independent, so the MGF of the sum is the product of $k$ copies: $(1 - 2t)^{-k/2}$. The Gamma MGF with $\alpha = \frac{k}{2}$ and $\beta = \frac{1}{2}$ is

$$\left( \frac{1/2}{1/2 - t} \right)^{k/2} = \left( \frac{1}{1 - 2t} \right)^{k/2} = (1 - 2t)^{-k/2}$$

The two match, so the sum is $\text{Gamma}(\frac{k}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$ by uniqueness.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check by simulation that $E[e^{0.5(U_1 + U_2)}] = M_U(0.5)^2$ for independent uniforms, but that
$E[e^{0.5 \cdot 2U}] \ne M_U(0.5)^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
u1, u2 = rng.random(n_trials), rng.random(n_trials)

m = (np.exp(0.5) - 1) / 0.5                  # M_U(0.5) for U ~ Unif(0, 1)
print(f"Independent U1 + U2: simulation {np.mean(np.exp(0.5 * (u1 + u2))):.4f}, M(0.5)^2 = {m**2:.4f}")
print(f"Same U twice, U + U: simulation {np.mean(np.exp(0.5 * (u1 + u1))):.4f}, M(0.5)^2 = {m**2:.4f}")
print(f"Exact for 2U: M_U(1) = {np.exp(1) - 1:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Independent U1 + U2: simulation 1.6832, M(0.5)^2 = 1.6834
Same U twice, U + U: simulation 1.7183, M(0.5)^2 = 1.6834
Exact for 2U: M_U(1) = 1.7183
```

</details>

## Summary

- For independent variables, $M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(t)$; for $n$ i.i.d. terms, $M(t)^n$.
- The reason: $e^{t(X+Y)} = e^{tX} e^{tY}$, and independent variables give a product of expectations.
- Scaling: $M_{aX}(t) = M_X(at)$, so $M_{X-Y}(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(-t)$ and $M_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = M(t/n)^n$.
- Recognize the product as a known MGF to get the distribution: Poisson sums, normal sums,
  exponentials to Gamma, squares of normals to chi-square.
- Independence is required: $X + X$ is not an independent sum.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t) \thinspace M_Y(t) \quad \text{for independent } X, Y}
$$
