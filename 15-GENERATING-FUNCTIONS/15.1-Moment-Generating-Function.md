# 15.1 Moment Generating Function

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 15: Generating Functions](README.md) / 15.1 Moment Generating Function

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 14: Conditional Expectation](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/README.md)

> **Big idea:** The moment generating function $M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}]$ packs all the moments of $X$ into one function of $t$, and, when it exists, it also fixes the whole distribution.

---

## Why It Matters

So far we met the moments of a random variable one at a time: $E[X]$, then $E[X^2]$,
then $E[X^3]$, and each one needed its own sum or integral ([6.7](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)).
A **generating function** is a different idea: store a whole list of numbers inside
one function, and let calculus take them out when we need them. The moment generating
function turns "find all moments" into "find one expectation". It also turns sums of
independent variables into products ([15.3](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)), and it is the tool
behind the Central Limit Theorem proof ([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)) and the
Chernoff bounds ([16.6](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)).
It does not always exist, and knowing when it fails is part of the lesson.

## Intuition

Let $X$ be the result of a fair coin: 1 for heads, 0 for tails. Its moments are easy:
$X^n = X$ for every $n \ge 1$ (because $0^n = 0$ and $1^n = 1$), so $E[X^n] = \frac{1}{2}$
for all $n \ge 1$. The list is $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \dots$ forever.

Now try this. Pick a number $t$ and compute the average of $e^{tX}$. The coin gives
$e^{t \cdot 0} = 1$ or $e^{t \cdot 1} = e^t$, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$
M(t) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} e^t
$$

Expand $e^t = 1 + t + \frac{t^2}{2!} + \frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots$ and collect the terms:

$$
M(t) = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{t}{1!} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{t^2}{2!} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots
$$

Look at the coefficients of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$: they are $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \dots$,
exactly the moments. One short formula contains the whole infinite list. That is no
accident. The series of $e^{tX}$ is $1 + tX + \frac{t^2 X^2}{2!} + \cdots$, and averaging
it term by term averages each power of $X$. Topic [15.2](15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md)
uses this to get moments by differentiating.

Here is another way to read $M(t)$. Say a delay of $x$ seconds costs $e^{tx}$ units.
Then $M(t)$ is the **average cost**. A bigger $t$ punishes long delays more and more.
The MGF asks how the whole shape of $X$ reacts to this exponential penalty.

## Picture

```text
   x  --->  e^(tx)  --->  average over all x  --->  M(t)
  value     one number     (weights p(x) or f(x))     one number for each t

   Example: X ~ Exp(1), so M(t) = 1/(1 - t) for t < 1

   M(t)
      6 |                                     ***|
      5 |                                    *   |
      4 |                                   *    |
      3 |                                ***     |
      2 |                           *****        |
      1 |***************************             |
      0 |                                        |
        +-----------------------------------------
        -1                 0                    1   t
```

The curve passes through $M(0) = 1$ and grows with $t$. It blows up as $t$ nears
$1$: the MGF may exist only on part of the number line.

## Definition

The **moment generating function** (MGF) of a random variable $X$ is

$$
M_X(t) = E\big[e^{tX}\big]
$$

defined for every real number $t$ where this expectation is finite. For a discrete
$X$ and a continuous $X$, LOTUS ([6.3](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.3-LOTUS.md),
[8.6](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md)) gives:

$$
M_X(t) = \sum_{x} e^{tx} \thinspace p_X(x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad M_X(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tx} \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx
$$

Here $t$ is an ordinary real number that you choose, and $x$ runs over the values of $X$.
For each $t$ the answer is one number, so $M_X$ is a function of $t$.

We say that **the MGF exists** if $M_X(t)$ is finite for every $t$ in some open
interval $(-h, h)$ around $0$, with $h \gt 0$. Only then is it useful.

**In words:** the MGF at $t$ is the average of $e^{tX}$; it is a new function built
from the distribution of $X$.

Some MGFs you will use again and again (parameters as in [Appendix B of the Syllabus](../SYLLABUS.md#appendix-b-distribution-cheat-sheet);
$\text{Geom}(p)$ counts trials, $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ has rate $\lambda$, $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ has rate $\beta$):

| Distribution | $M_X(t)$ | Finite for |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{Bern}(p)$ | $1 - p + pe^t$ | all $t$ |
| $\text{Bin}(n, p)$ | $(1 - p + pe^t)^n$ | all $t$ |
| $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ | $\exp\big(\lambda(e^t - 1)\big)$ | all $t$ |
| $\text{Geom}(p)$ | $\dfrac{pe^t}{1 - (1 - p)e^t}$ | $e^t \lt \frac{1}{1 - p}$ |
| $\text{Unif}(a, b)$ | $\dfrac{e^{tb} - e^{ta}}{t(b - a)}$ | all $t \ne 0$; $M(0) = 1$ |
| $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ | $\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}$ | $t \lt \lambda$ |
| $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ | $\left( \dfrac{\beta}{\beta - t} \right)^{\alpha}$ | $t \lt \beta$ |
| $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\exp\left( \mu t + \dfrac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} \right)$ | all $t$ |

## Why It Is True

### Three Basic Rules

1. **$M_X(0) = 1$ for every $X$.** Because $e^{0 \cdot X} = 1$, and the average of the
   constant 1 is 1. This is a free check on every MGF you compute.
2. **$M_X(t) \gt 0$ for every $t$.** Because $e^{tX}$ is always positive.
3. **Shift and scale.** For constants $a$ and $b$:

$$
M_{aX+b}(t) = E\big[e^{t(aX+b)}\big] = e^{bt} E\big[e^{(at)X}\big] = e^{bt} M_X(at)
$$

**In words:** adding $b$ to $X$ multiplies the MGF by $e^{bt}$; multiplying $X$ by $a$
replaces $t$ by $at$.

### Where the Formulas Come From

**Poisson.** With $p(k) = e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k / k!$ and the series $e^u = \sum_k u^k / k!$:

$$
M(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} e^{tk} e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\lambda e^t)^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda e^t} = e^{\lambda (e^t - 1)}
$$

**Binomial.** Use the binomial theorem $\sum_k \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k} = (a + b)^n$ with
$a = pe^t$ and $b = 1 - p$:

$$
M(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} e^{tk} \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n-k} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} (pe^t)^k (1 - p)^{n-k} = (1 - p + pe^t)^n
$$

**Standard normal.** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Complete the square in the exponent,
$tz - \frac{z^2}{2} = -\frac{(z - t)^2}{2} + \frac{t^2}{2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_Z(t) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tz} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2} \thinspace dz && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= e^{t^2/2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(z-t)^2/2} \thinspace dz && \text{(complete the square)} \\
&= e^{t^2/2} && \text{(a normal density has area 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For $X = \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ use the shift-and-scale rule with $b = \mu$ and
$a = \sigma$: $M_X(t) = e^{\mu t} e^{\sigma^2 t^2 / 2}$. (Topic [9.8](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.8-Log-Normal.md)
did the same calculation for a general normal.)

### What an Existing MGF Tells You

If $M_X(t)$ is finite on $(-h, h)$, then every moment of $X$ is finite. Pick any
$0 \lt s \lt h$. The series of $e^{s\lvert x \rvert}$ has all positive terms, so
$e^{s\lvert x \rvert} \ge \frac{(s\lvert x \rvert)^n}{n!}$, and also $e^{s\lvert x \rvert} \le e^{sx} + e^{-sx}$. Together:

$$
E\big[\lvert X \rvert^n\big] \le \frac{n!}{s^n} E\big[e^{s\lvert X \rvert}\big] \le \frac{n!}{s^n} \big( M_X(s) + M_X(-s) \big) \lt \infty
$$

**In words:** an MGF can exist only if the tails of $X$ shrink at least as fast as an
exponential. This is the "light tail" test of [9.13](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md).

### Where the MGF Does Not Exist

**Cauchy.** Take the standard Cauchy density $f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$ from
[9.9](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md) and any $t \gt 0$. For $x \gt 0$ the
series gives $e^{tx} \ge \frac{t^2 x^2}{2}$, so

$$
\int_0^{\infty} \frac{e^{tx}}{\pi(1 + x^2)} \thinspace dx \ge \int_0^{\infty} \frac{t^2}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{x^2}{1 + x^2} \thinspace dx = \infty
$$

because $\frac{x^2}{1 + x^2}$ tends to $1$, and a constant has an infinite integral over
$[0, \infty)$. The case $t \lt 0$ is the mirror image on the left side. So
$E[e^{tX}] = \infty$ for every $t \ne 0$: only $M(0) = 1$ is finite, and the MGF
does not exist. This agrees with the fact that the Cauchy has no mean.

**Log-normal.** This one is more surprising, because **every moment of the log-normal
is finite** ([9.8](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.8-Log-Normal.md)). Let $X = e^Y$ with
$Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and take $t \gt 0$:

$$
E\big[e^{tX}\big] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left( t e^y - \frac{y^2}{2} \right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \thinspace dy
$$

For $y \gt 0$ the series gives $e^y \ge \frac{y^3}{6}$, so the exponent is at least
$\frac{t y^3}{6} - \frac{y^2}{2}$, which tends to $+\infty$. The integrand itself tends to
infinity, so the integral diverges. Thus $M_X(t) = \infty$ for every $t \gt 0$: no
interval around $0$ works, and the MGF does not exist.

> **Warning:** Finite moments do **not** guarantee an MGF. The log-normal has all
> moments but no MGF. An MGF needs a tail that shrinks at least as fast as an
> exponential. Topic [15.6](15.6-Characteristic-Function.md) gives a relative of the MGF
> that exists for every random variable.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The MGF of a Fair Die

**Problem.** Let $X$ be a roll of a fair six-sided die. Find $M_X(t)$, check that
$M_X(0) = 1$, compute $M_X(0.1)$, and say how to read the probabilities from $M_X$.

**Solution.**

1. The values $1, \dots, 6$ each have probability $\frac{1}{6}$. By the discrete formula:

$$
M_X(t) = \frac{1}{6} \left( e^{t} + e^{2t} + e^{3t} + e^{4t} + e^{5t} + e^{6t} \right)
$$

2. At $t = 0$ each term is $e^0 = 1$, so $M_X(0) = \frac{6}{6} = 1$. The check passes.
3. At $t = 0.1$ compute the six terms: $e^{0.1} \approx 1.1052$, $e^{0.2} \approx 1.2214$,
   $e^{0.3} \approx 1.3499$, $e^{0.4} \approx 1.4918$, $e^{0.5} \approx 1.6487$, $e^{0.6} \approx 1.8221$.
   Their sum is about $8.6391$, so

$$
M_X(0.1) \approx \frac{8.6391}{6} \approx 1.4399
$$

4. Reading the probabilities: the coefficient of $e^{kt}$ in $M_X(t)$ is $P(X = k)$.
   For example the coefficient of $e^{4t}$ is $\frac{1}{6} = P(X = 4)$.

**Answer.** $M_X(t) = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{6} e^{kt}$ and $M_X(0.1) \approx 1.4399$.

**Check.** A die has mean $3.5$, so $e^{0.1X}$ is near $e^{0.35} \approx 1.419$. The MGF value
$1.4399$ is a little larger, because $e^{tx}$ is curved upward and the spread of
$X$ adds a bit more (this is Jensen's inequality, [16.3](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)).

### Example 2: An Exponential Penalty on Delays

**Problem.** A packet delay $X$ (in seconds) is exponential with rate $\lambda = 2$ (mean
$0.5$ seconds). A delay of $x$ seconds costs $e^{0.5x}$ units. (a) Find the expected cost.
(b) What happens if the cost is $e^{2x}$, or $e^{3x}$?

**Solution.**

1. The expected cost is $M_X(0.5) = E[e^{0.5X}]$. Compute it with the density $f(x) = 2e^{-2x}$ for $x \ge 0$:

$$
M_X(t) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{tx} \cdot 2e^{-2x} \thinspace dx = 2 \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(2-t)x} \thinspace dx = \frac{2}{2 - t} \qquad \text{(valid when } t \lt 2 \text{)}
$$

2. At $t = 0.5$: $M_X(0.5) = \frac{2}{1.5} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333$.
3. For $t = 2$ the integrand is $2e^{0} = 2$ for every $x$, and $\int_0^{\infty} 2 \thinspace dx = \infty$.
   For $t = 3$ the integrand is $2e^{x}$, which grows, and the integral is also infinite.

**Answer.** (a) The expected cost is $\frac{4}{3}$ units. (b) For cost $e^{tx}$ with $t \ge 2$ the
expected cost is infinite.

**Check.** The cost grows by a factor of $e^{t}$ per second, while the probability of a long
delay shrinks only by a factor of $e^{-2}$ per second. If $t \ge 2$, the cost wins. The
condition $t \lt \lambda$ is exactly this race. The simulation below agrees for $t = 0.5$.

### Example 3: A Normal Variable

**Problem.** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(2, 3^2)$, so $\mu = 2$ and $\sigma^2 = 9$. Find $E[e^{0.5X}]$.

**Solution.**

1. The MGF of a normal is $M_X(t) = \exp(\mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2})$.
2. Put $\mu = 2$, $\sigma^2 = 9$ and $t = 0.5$:

$$
M_X(0.5) = \exp\left( 2(0.5) + \frac{9(0.25)}{2} \right) = \exp(1 + 1.125) = e^{2.125} \approx 8.373
$$

**Answer.** $E[e^{0.5X}] \approx 8.373$.

**Check.** It is much larger than $e^{0.5 \cdot 2} = e^{1} \approx 2.718$, the value you would get by
(wrongly) putting the mean inside. The large spread ($\sigma = 3$) makes the rare big
values of $X$ count a lot. The simulation agrees.

## Simulate It

The first part estimates three MGFs by averaging $e^{tX}$ over many samples and compares
them with the exact formulas. The second part shows the log-normal MGF failing: the
integral of $e^{0.1x}$ against the log-normal density, cut off at $y = \ln x \le c$,
grows without bound as $c$ grows.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
t = 0.5

# The MGF at t is the average of e^(tX). Compare with the exact formulas.
x_exp = rng.exponential(1 / 2, n_trials)             # Exp(2): rate 2, mean 0.5
x_pois = rng.poisson(3, n_trials)                    # Pois(3)
x_norm = rng.normal(2, 3, n_trials)                  # N(2, 3^2)

print(f"Exp(2):  simulation {np.mean(np.exp(t * x_exp)):.4f}, exact {2 / (2 - t):.4f}")
print(f"Pois(3): simulation {np.mean(np.exp(t * x_pois)):.4f}, exact {np.exp(3 * (np.exp(t) - 1)):.4f}")
print(f"N(2,9):  simulation {np.mean(np.exp(t * x_norm)):.4f}, exact {np.exp(2 * t + 4.5 * t**2):.4f}")

# Log-normal: E[exp(0.1 X)] is infinite. Cut the integral off at y = ln(x) <= c.
y = np.linspace(-10, 8, 1_800_001)
for c in [2, 4, 6, 8]:
    yy = y[y <= c]
    log10_integrand = (0.1 * np.exp(yy) - yy**2 / 2) / np.log(10) - np.log10(np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
    peak = log10_integrand.max()
    total = (10 ** (log10_integrand - peak)).sum() * (yy[1] - yy[0])
    print(f"log-normal, y up to {c}: integral is about 10^{peak + np.log10(total):.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
Exp(2):  simulation 1.3333, exact 1.3333
Pois(3): simulation 6.9864, exact 7.0018
N(2,9):  simulation 8.3841, exact 8.3729
log-normal, y up to 2: integral is about 10^0.1
log-normal, y up to 4: integral is about 10^0.1
log-normal, y up to 6: integral is about 10^7.8
log-normal, y up to 8: integral is about 10^112.7
```

The three simulated values match the formulas. For the log-normal the cut-off integral
stays near $1$ for a while, then explodes: no finite limit exists. The code works with
logarithms (powers of 10) only to avoid computer overflow.

## Common Mistakes

- **Putting the mean inside.** $E[e^{tX}]$ is not $e^{tE[X]}$. In Example 3 the first is
  $8.373$ and the second is $2.718$. The exponential is curved, so averaging after
  or before gives different answers.
- **Forgetting the domain.** $M_X(t) = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}$ holds only for $t \lt \lambda$.
  Beyond that the expectation is infinite, and the formula is meaningless.
- **Assuming every random variable has an MGF.** The Cauchy and the log-normal do not, and
  neither does any power-law tail such as the Pareto. Check the tail first.
- **Thinking that finite moments are enough.** The log-normal has all moments and still has
  no MGF. An MGF is a stronger condition.
- **Mixing up $t$ and $x$.** $x$ is the value of the random variable and is summed or
  integrated away. $t$ is the free number that remains. The answer depends on $t$ only.

## Where It Is Used

- **Probability theory:** moments by differentiation ([15.2](15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md)),
  sums of independent variables ([15.3](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)) and the Central Limit
  Theorem ([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md), [17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)).
- **Concentration:** the Chernoff bound $P(X \ge a) \le e^{-ta} M_X(t)$ is built directly
  from the MGF ([16.6](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** the logarithm of an MGF, $\ln E[e^{tX}]$, is the "log-partition
  function" of exponential families, and the log-sum-exp function used in softmax is the
  logarithm of a sum of exponentials, the same shape ([15.7](15.7-Cumulants.md)).
- **Finance and risk:** if $X$ is a log-return, $E[e^{X}]$ is the expected growth factor, and
  $\frac{1}{\theta} \ln E[e^{-\theta X}]$ is a risk measure for a gain $X$ that punishes large losses.
- **Computer science:** the expected cost of exponentially growing penalties, such as
  retry back-off, is an MGF value, as in Example 2.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X \sim \text{Bern}(0.3)$. Find $M_X(t)$ and compute $E[2^X]$ by evaluating
$M_X(\ln 2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$M_X(t) = 0.7 + 0.3e^t$$

Since $e^{\ln 2} = 2$, we have $2^X = e^{(\ln 2)X}$, so

$$E[2^X] = M_X(\ln 2) = 0.7 + 0.3 \times 2 = 1.3$$

Direct check: $X = 0$ gives $2^0 = 1$ with probability $0.7$, and $X = 1$ gives $2$ with probability $0.3$, so $0.7 + 0.6 = 1.3$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A random sign $X$ is $+1$ or $-1$, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Find
$M_X(t)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$M_X(t) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{t} + \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-t} = \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2}$$

This function is known as the hyperbolic cosine. It equals $1$ at $t = 0$, as every MGF must.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and $Y = 3X - 2$. Find $M_Y(t)$ and say for which $t$ it is finite.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $\text{Exp}(1)$ the MGF is $M_X(s) = \frac{1}{1 - s}$ for $s \lt 1$. By the shift-and-scale rule with $a = 3$ and $b = -2$:

$$M_Y(t) = e^{-2t} M_X(3t) = \frac{e^{-2t}}{1 - 3t}$$

It is finite when $3t \lt 1$, that is for $t \lt \frac{1}{3}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Show that $M_U(t) = \frac{e^t - 1}{t}$ for $t \ne 0$, and
find $E[e^{U}]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The density is $1$ on $[0, 1]$:

$$M_U(t) = \int_0^1 e^{tx} \thinspace dx = \left[ \frac{e^{tx}}{t} \right]_0^1 = \frac{e^t - 1}{t}$$

Now $E[e^U] = M_U(1) = e - 1 \approx 1.7183$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that the standard Cauchy distribution has no MGF: $E[e^{tX}] = \infty$ for
every $t \ne 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $t \gt 0$ (for $t \lt 0$ use the symmetry $x \to -x$). For $x \gt 0$, the series of $e^{u}$ has only positive terms, so $e^{tx} \ge \frac{(tx)^2}{2}$. Then

$$E[e^{tX}] \ge \int_0^{\infty} \frac{t^2 x^2}{2\pi(1 + x^2)} \thinspace dx$$

For $x \ge 1$ the fraction $\frac{x^2}{1 + x^2}$ is at least $\frac{1}{2}$, so the integral is at least $\int_1^{\infty} \frac{t^2}{4\pi} \thinspace dx = \infty$. The part of the expectation from $x \lt 0$ is positive and cannot cancel it. So $E[e^{tX}] = \infty$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate $E[e^{0.5U}]$ for $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and compare with the exact value
$M_U(0.5) = \frac{e^{0.5} - 1}{0.5}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
u = rng.random(1_000_000)
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(np.exp(0.5 * u)):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {(np.exp(0.5) - 1) / 0.5:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 1.2975
Exact:      1.2974
```

</details>

## Summary

- The MGF $M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}]$ is a function of $t$; it always satisfies $M_X(0) = 1$.
- It exists only if it is finite on an open interval around $0$; this needs a tail that
  shrinks at least as fast as an exponential.
- Shift and scale: $M_{aX+b}(t) = e^{bt} M_X(at)$.
- The Cauchy and the log-normal have no MGF (the log-normal even has all its moments).
- The MGF stores all moments at once; the next topic shows how to take them out.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{M_X(t) = E\big[e^{tX}\big]}
$$
