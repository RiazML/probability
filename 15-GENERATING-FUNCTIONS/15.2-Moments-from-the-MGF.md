# 15.2 Moments from the MGF

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 15: Generating Functions](README.md) / 15.2 Moments from the MGF

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [15.1 Moment Generating Function](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)

> **Big idea:** Differentiate the MGF $n$ times and set $t = 0$ to get the $n$-th moment, $E[X^n] = M_X^{(n)}(0)$; or read it from the power series, where $E[X^n]$ is the coefficient of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$.

---

## Why It Matters

Computing $E[X^3]$ or $E[X^4]$ straight from the definition can be painful. For a Poisson
variable, even $E[X^2]$ needs a trick with the sum. The MGF replaces each of these
sums by one calculation that you already did: find $M_X(t)$ once, and every moment
comes from calculus or from a power series. This is how the moments of the normal,
exponential and Gamma are found quickly, and it gives the skewness and kurtosis
of [6.7](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)
almost for free.

## Intuition

In [15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md) we saw that the MGF of a fair 0/1 coin has
the series $1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{t}{1!} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots$, and that
the numbers in front of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$ are the moments. Now think of that series as a
**machine with one output slot for each power of $t$**.

Take the term $\frac{m_2 t^2}{2!}$. If you differentiate it twice you get the constant
$m_2$, and if you then put $t = 0$ the constant is all that is left. Terms with a lower
power vanish after two derivatives (they become $0$). Terms with a higher power still
have a factor $t$, and they vanish when $t = 0$. So the recipe of differentiating twice and then setting $t = 0$ picks out exactly
$m_2$, the second moment. The same works for every $n$.

Another way to see it: the slope of $M$ at $t = 0$ is the mean, and the bending
(curvature) of $M$ at $t = 0$ is the second moment. A distribution with a large
spread has an MGF that bends upward quickly.

## Picture

```text
   M(t) = 1 + m1 t + m2 t^2/2! + m3 t^3/3! + m4 t^4/4! + ...

   differentiate n times, then put t = 0:

   n = 0 :  M(0)      = 1      always
   n = 1 :  M'(0)     = m1  =  E[X]      the mean
   n = 2 :  M''(0)    = m2  =  E[X^2]    Var(X) = m2 - m1^2
   n = 3 :  M'''(0)   = m3  =  E[X^3]    needed for skewness
   n = 4 :  M''''(0)  = m4  =  E[X^4]    needed for kurtosis
```

Each derivative at $0$ gives the next moment. The variance comes from the first two.

## Definition

**Theorem (moments from the MGF).** Suppose $M_X(t)$ exists, that is, it is finite for
all $t$ in some interval $(-h, h)$ around $0$. Then $M_X$ can be differentiated as often as we like,
every moment of $X$ exists, and

$$
E[X^n] = M_X^{(n)}(0) = \frac{d^n}{dt^n} M_X(t) \Big\vert_{t=0}
$$

where $M_X^{(n)}$ is the $n$-th derivative. Equivalently, for $\lvert t \rvert \lt h$ the MGF is the power series

$$
M_X(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} E[X^n] \frac{t^n}{n!} = 1 + E[X]\thinspace t + E[X^2] \frac{t^2}{2} + E[X^3] \frac{t^3}{6} + \cdots
$$

**In words:** the $n$-th moment is the $n$-th derivative of the MGF at zero, and also
$n!$ times the coefficient of $t^n$ in its series.

Two special cases give the mean and variance:

$$
E[X] = M_X'(0), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = M_X''(0) - \big( M_X'(0) \big)^2
$$

There is a shortcut with the logarithm. Let $K_X(t) = \ln M_X(t)$. Then

$$
E[X] = K_X'(0), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = K_X''(0)
$$

**In words:** the log of the MGF gives the mean and the variance directly, with no
subtraction at the end. Topic [15.7](15.7-Cumulants.md) shows that this $K_X$ is the
cumulant generating function.

## Why It Is True

**The power series.** Start from the series $e^{u} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{u^n}{n!}$ with $u = tX$:

$$
e^{tX} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n X^n}{n!}
$$

We want to take the average term by term. That is allowed when the sum of the absolute
values has a finite average (a standard fact about swapping a sum and an expectation).
For $\lvert t \rvert \lt h$ it does:

$$
E\left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lvert t \rvert^n \lvert X \rvert^n}{n!} \right] = E\big[e^{\lvert t \rvert \lvert X \rvert}\big] \le M_X(t) + M_X(-t) \lt \infty
$$

(the last step uses $e^{\lvert u \rvert} \le e^{u} + e^{-u}$). So we may swap, and linearity of expectation gives

$$
M_X(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!} E[X^n]
$$

**Differentiating a power series.** The derivative of $\frac{t^k}{k!}$, taken $n$ times, is
$\frac{t^{k-n}}{(k-n)!}$ when $k \ge n$ and $0$ when $k \lt n$. At $t = 0$ only the term
$k = n$ survives, and it equals $1$. So $M_X^{(n)}(0) = E[X^n]$. A power series may be
differentiated term by term inside its interval of convergence, so this is correct.

**The variance formula.** It is the usual identity $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$ with
$E[X^2] = M''(0)$ and $E[X] = M'(0)$.

**The logarithm shortcut.** Use the chain rule and the quotient rule with $M(0) = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K'(t) &= \frac{M'(t)}{M(t)} && \text{(chain rule for } \ln \text{)} \\
K''(t) &= \frac{M''(t) M(t) - M'(t)^2}{M(t)^2} && \text{(quotient rule)} \\
K'(0) = M'(0), \quad K''(0) &= M''(0) - M'(0)^2 && \text{(put } t = 0 \text{ and } M(0) = 1 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: All Moments of the Exponential

**Problem.** Let $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ (rate $\lambda$, mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$). Find $E[X^n]$ for every $n$,
and the variance.

**Solution.**

1. From [15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md), $M(t) = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}$ for $t \lt \lambda$. Write it as
   $M(t) = \lambda (\lambda - t)^{-1}$ and differentiate:

$$
M'(t) = \lambda (\lambda - t)^{-2}, \qquad M''(t) = 2\lambda (\lambda - t)^{-3}, \qquad M'''(t) = 6\lambda (\lambda - t)^{-4}
$$

2. Put $t = 0$: $E[X] = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$, $E[X^2] = \frac{2\lambda}{\lambda^3} = \frac{2}{\lambda^2}$,
   $E[X^3] = \frac{6\lambda}{\lambda^4} = \frac{6}{\lambda^3}$.
3. Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{2}{\lambda^2} - \left( \frac{1}{\lambda} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.
4. For every $n$, use the geometric series $\frac{1}{1 - u} = \sum_n u^n$ with $u = \frac{t}{\lambda}$:

$$
M(t) = \frac{1}{1 - t/\lambda} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{\lambda^n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{n!}{\lambda^n} \cdot \frac{t^n}{n!}
$$

   The coefficient of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$ is $\frac{n!}{\lambda^n}$.

**Answer.** $E[X^n] = \dfrac{n!}{\lambda^n}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \dfrac{1}{\lambda^2}$.

**Check.** For $n = 1, 2, 3$ the formula gives $\frac{1}{\lambda}, \frac{2}{\lambda^2}, \frac{6}{\lambda^3}$, the same as the
derivatives. The variance matches [9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md).

### Example 2: Moments of the Poisson

**Problem.** Let $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ (here $\lambda$ is the mean). Find $E[X]$, $E[X^2]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and
$E[X^3]$.

**Solution.**

1. $M(t) = \exp\big(\lambda (e^t - 1)\big)$. Differentiate with the chain rule. Each time the derivative of the
   exponent is $\lambda e^t$:

$$
M'(t) = \lambda e^t M(t)
$$

2. Differentiate again with the product rule, using $M' = \lambda e^t M$:

$$
M''(t) = \lambda e^t M(t) + \lambda e^t \cdot \lambda e^t M(t) = \big( \lambda e^t + \lambda^2 e^{2t} \big) M(t)
$$

3. Once more:

$$
M'''(t) = \big( \lambda e^t + 2\lambda^2 e^{2t} \big) M(t) + \big( \lambda e^t + \lambda^2 e^{2t} \big) \lambda e^t M(t)
$$

4. Put $t = 0$, where $M(0) = 1$ and $e^0 = 1$:

$$
E[X] = \lambda, \qquad E[X^2] = \lambda + \lambda^2, \qquad E[X^3] = (\lambda + 2\lambda^2) + (\lambda + \lambda^2)\lambda = \lambda^3 + 3\lambda^2 + \lambda
$$

5. Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = (\lambda + \lambda^2) - \lambda^2 = \lambda$.

**Answer.** $E[X] = \lambda$, $E[X^2] = \lambda^2 + \lambda$, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda$, $E[X^3] = \lambda^3 + 3\lambda^2 + \lambda$.

**Check.** Mean equals variance, the well-known Poisson property
([7.5](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.5-Poisson.md)). For $\lambda = 2$: $E[X^3] = 8 + 12 + 2 = 22$,
and the simulation below gives $21.98$. For $\lambda = 1$ we get $1 + 3 + 1 = 5$, a Bell number
(the number of ways to split a set of 3 items into groups).

### Example 3: The Moments of the Normal

**Problem.** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find $E[Z^n]$ for every $n$, and the kurtosis.

**Solution.**

1. From [15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md), $M_Z(t) = e^{t^2/2}$. Expand with $e^u = \sum_k \frac{u^k}{k!}$ and $u = \frac{t^2}{2}$:

$$
M_Z(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k!} \left( \frac{t^2}{2} \right)^k = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^{2k}}{2^k k!} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(2k)!}{2^k k!} \cdot \frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}
$$

2. Only even powers of $t$ appear, so every odd moment is $0$. The coefficient of $\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}$ is the even moment:

$$
E[Z^{2k}] = \frac{(2k)!}{2^k \thinspace k!}
$$

3. Compute the first few: $k = 1$: $\frac{2}{2 \cdot 1} = 1$. $k = 2$: $\frac{24}{4 \cdot 2} = 3$.
   $k = 3$: $\frac{720}{8 \cdot 6} = 15$. $k = 4$: $\frac{40320}{16 \cdot 24} = 105$.
4. The kurtosis of $Z$ is $\frac{E[Z^4]}{\sigma^4} = \frac{3}{1} = 3$.

**Answer.** $E[Z^{2k}] = \frac{(2k)!}{2^k k!} = 1, 3, 15, 105, \dots$ and $E[Z^{2k+1}] = 0$. The kurtosis is $3$.

**Check.** This explains the statement in [6.7](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)
that the normal has kurtosis 3 and skewness 0. The simulation in Practice 6 confirms $3$ and $15$.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 1 and Example 2 with random samples, and it checks that
differentiating the exact MGF numerically gives the same moments (a derivative is the
limit of a difference, here with a small step $h$).

```python
import numpy as np
from math import factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 2_000_000
lam = 2.0

# Exp(2): the n-th moment should be n! / lam^n.
x = rng.exponential(1 / lam, n_trials)
for n in range(1, 5):
    print(f"E[X^{n}]: simulation {np.mean(x**n):.4f}, exact {factorial(n) / lam**n:.4f}")

# Pois(2): E[X^3] = lam^3 + 3 lam^2 + lam = 22.
k = rng.poisson(lam, n_trials)
print(f"Pois(2) E[X^3]: simulation {np.mean(k**3):.4f}, exact {lam**3 + 3 * lam**2 + lam:.4f}")

# Derivatives of the exact MGF Exp(2) at 0, by finite differences.
M = lambda t: lam / (lam - t)
h = 1e-3
print(f"M'(0)  = {(M(h) - M(-h)) / (2 * h):.4f}   (E[X]   = 0.5)")
print(f"M''(0) = {(M(h) - 2 * M(0) + M(-h)) / h**2:.4f}   (E[X^2] = 0.5)")
```

Output:

```text
E[X^1]: simulation 0.4997, exact 0.5000
E[X^2]: simulation 0.4995, exact 0.5000
E[X^3]: simulation 0.7498, exact 0.7500
E[X^4]: simulation 1.5055, exact 1.5000
Pois(2) E[X^3]: simulation 21.9815, exact 22.0000
M'(0)  = 0.5000   (E[X]   = 0.5)
M''(0) = 0.5000   (E[X^2] = 0.5)
```

The higher the moment, the noisier the estimate (see $E[X^4]$), because rare large values
count more and more.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to set $t = 0$.** The derivative $M'(t)$ is a function of $t$; the moment is
  its value at $0$. Always finish with $t = 0$.
- **Forgetting the factorial.** From the series, $E[X^n]$ is $n!$ times the coefficient of
  $t^n$, not the coefficient itself. In Example 1 the coefficient of $t^2$ is $\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$, but
  $E[X^2] = \frac{2}{\lambda^2}$.
- **Reporting $M''(0)$ as the variance.** $M''(0)$ is the second **raw** moment $E[X^2]$.
  Subtract $\big(M'(0)\big)^2$ to get the variance.
- **Using the method when the MGF does not exist.** The Cauchy has no MGF, and the log-normal
  has none either, even though its moments are finite. For them compute the moments by
  integration.
- **Differentiating at the wrong point.** Moments about the mean (central moments) are
  not $M^{(n)}(0)$. Use $K_X$ and [15.7](15.7-Cumulants.md), or expand $E[(X - \mu)^n]$ with raw moments.

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics and ML:** the method of moments matches the first few sample moments to
  $M^{(n)}(0)$ to estimate parameters. The mean and variance of an
  exponential-family model are derivatives of its log-partition function.
- **Probability theory:** the Poisson, Gamma and normal moments in
  [7.5](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.5-Poisson.md), [9.4](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md) and
  [9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md) all follow from this one idea.
- **Finance and insurance:** skewness and kurtosis of losses, from the third and fourth
  moments, show how risky the tail is.
- **Physics and engineering:** the moments of a noise signal ($E[X^2]$ is its power) come
  from the MGF of the noise distribution.
- **Limit theorems:** the proof of the CLT expands the MGF to second order
  ([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$. Use $M_X(t) = 1 - p + pe^t$ to find $E[X]$, $E[X^2]$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$M'(t) = pe^t$ and $M''(t) = pe^t$. At $t = 0$ both equal $p$:

$$E[X] = p, \qquad E[X^2] = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = p(1 - p)$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A random sign $X$ is $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, with
$M_X(t) = \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2}$. Find $E[X]$ and $E[X^2]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$M'(t) = \frac{e^t - e^{-t}}{2}$ and $M''(t) = \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2}$. At $t = 0$:

$$E[X] = \frac{1 - 1}{2} = 0, \qquad E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 1}{2} = 1$$

Check: $X^2 = 1$ always, and $E[X] = 0$ by symmetry.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Use $K(t) = \ln M(t)$ to find the mean and variance of $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$K(t) = n \ln(1 - p + pe^t)$. Differentiate:

$$K'(t) = \frac{n p e^t}{1 - p + pe^t}$$

At $t = 0$ the denominator is $1$, so $K'(0) = np$. For the second derivative use the quotient rule:

$$K''(t) = \frac{n p e^t (1 - p + pe^t) - n p e^t \cdot pe^t}{(1 - p + pe^t)^2} = \frac{n p (1 - p) e^t}{(1 - p + pe^t)^2}$$

At $t = 0$: $K''(0) = np(1 - p)$. So $E[X] = np$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1 - p)$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $M(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}$, find $E[X]$,
$E[X^2]$ and $E[X^3]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $u(t) = \mu + \sigma^2 t$, so $u' = \sigma^2$ and $M' = uM$. Then $M'' = (\sigma^2 + u^2)M$ and

$$M''' = 2\sigma^2 u M + (\sigma^2 + u^2) u M = (3\sigma^2 u + u^3) M$$

At $t = 0$ we have $u = \mu$ and $M = 1$:

$$E[X] = \mu, \qquad E[X^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2, \qquad E[X^3] = \mu^3 + 3\mu\sigma^2$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ with shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$, so $M(t) = (1 - t/\beta)^{-\alpha}$
for $t \lt \beta$. Use the binomial series $(1 - u)^{-\alpha} = \sum_n \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)}{n!} u^n$
to find $E[X^n]$, and check the mean and variance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Put $u = \frac{t}{\beta}$. The coefficient of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$ is the moment:

$$M(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)}{\beta^n} \cdot \frac{t^n}{n!} \quad \implies \quad E[X^n] = \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)}{\beta^n}$$

For $n = 1$: $E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}$. For $n = 2$: $E[X^2] = \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta^2}$, so

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta^2} - \frac{\alpha^2}{\beta^2} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}$$

These match the table in Appendix B of the Syllabus. With $\alpha = 1$ we get $n!/\beta^n$, the exponential moments.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check by simulation that $E[Z^4] = 3$ and $E[Z^6] = 15$ for a standard normal $Z$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>


```text

```

</details>

## Summary

- If the MGF exists, $E[X^n] = M_X^{(n)}(0)$, and $M_X(t) = \sum_n E[X^n] \frac{t^n}{n!}$.
- Mean: $M'(0)$. Variance: $M''(0) - M'(0)^2$.
- With $K(t) = \ln M(t)$: mean $= K'(0)$ and variance $= K''(0)$.
- Reading a series is often faster than differentiating: Exponential gives $n!/\lambda^n$, the standard
  normal gives $\frac{(2k)!}{2^k k!}$ for even moments and $0$ for odd ones.
- Always set $t = 0$ at the end, and remember the factor $n!$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X^n] = M_X^{(n)}(0)}
$$
