# 15.7 Cumulants

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 15: Generating Functions](README.md) / 15.7 Cumulants

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [15.6 Characteristic Function](15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)

> **Big idea:** The cumulants are the coefficients of $\ln M_X(t)$; the first two are the mean and the variance, the next two measure skewness and tail weight, and all of them simply add for independent variables, which is why the normal distribution is the one with no cumulants beyond the second.

---

## Why It Matters

Moments are not friendly for sums. The mean and the variance add when we add independent
variables, but the fourth moment does not: there is a messy cross term. **Cumulants** fix
this. They are a rearranged list of the same information as the moments, built so that
**every one of them adds** for independent sums. They also give the cleanest
statement of why the Central Limit Theorem works, and of how fast: the skewness of a
standardized sum shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. In machine learning, the log of an
MGF is the log-partition function of exponential families, and its derivatives are the
means and variances of the model.

## Intuition

Add two independent fair coins, $X_1, X_2 \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$. Each coin has mean $\frac{1}{2}$,
variance $\frac{1}{4}$, and central fourth moment $E[(X - \frac{1}{2})^4] = \frac{1}{16}$ (the distance is always $\frac{1}{2}$).
The sum $S = X_1 + X_2$ is $\text{Bin}(2, \frac{1}{2})$. Check what adds:

| Quantity | One coin | Sum of two | Two times one coin? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $1$ | yes |
| Variance | $\frac{1}{4}$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | yes |
| Central 4th moment | $\frac{1}{16}$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | **no** ($2 \times \frac{1}{16} = \frac{1}{8}$) |

For the sum, the values $0, 1, 2$ have probabilities $\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}$, and the distances from the mean $1$ are
$1, 0, 1$, so the fourth moment is $\frac{1}{4} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{2}$. The fourth moment does not add.
But the combination $\mu_4 - 3\sigma^4$ does: for one coin it is $\frac{1}{16} - \frac{3}{16} = -\frac{1}{8}$, and for
the sum it is $\frac{1}{2} - 3 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1}{4}$, which is exactly twice $-\frac{1}{8}$. That combination is the
**fourth cumulant**. Cumulants are the right quantities to track when we add independent pieces.

## Picture

```text
   K(t) = ln M(t) = k1 t + k2 t^2/2! + k3 t^3/3! + k4 t^4/4! + ...

   n   cumulant   meaning                    N(mu,s2)  Pois(L)  Exp(rate r)
   --  ---------  -------------------------  --------  -------  -----------
   1   k1         mean                       mu        L        1/r
   2   k2         variance                   s2        L        1/r^2
   3   k3         third central moment       0         L        2/r^3
   4   k4         mu4 - 3 (variance)^2       0         L        6/r^4
   n   kn         (higher shape)             0         L        (n-1)!/r^n

   independent X, Y:   kn(X + Y) = kn(X) + kn(Y)   for every n
```

The normal has zero cumulants from the third on; the Poisson has all cumulants equal to its mean $\lambda$.

## Definition

Let $M_X(t)$ be the MGF of $X$, which exists near $0$ and is positive there, so its logarithm is defined. The
**cumulant generating function** (CGF) is

$$
K_X(t) = \ln M_X(t)
$$

and the **cumulants** $\kappa_1, \kappa_2, \kappa_3, \dots$ are defined by the series

$$
K_X(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kappa_n \frac{t^n}{n!}, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \kappa_n = K_X^{(n)}(0)
$$

**In words:** the $n$-th cumulant is the $n$-th derivative of the log of the MGF at zero; moments come from $M$, cumulants come from $\ln M$.

In terms of the raw moments $m_n = E[X^n]$ and the central moments $\mu_n = E[(X - m_1)^n]$:

$$
\kappa_1 = m_1, \qquad \kappa_2 = \sigma^2, \qquad \kappa_3 = \mu_3, \qquad \kappa_4 = \mu_4 - 3\sigma^4
$$

So the shape numbers of [6.7](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md) are ratios of cumulants:

$$
\operatorname{Skew}(X) = \frac{\kappa_3}{\kappa_2^{3/2}}, \qquad \text{Excess kurtosis} = \operatorname{Kurt}(X) - 3 = \frac{\kappa_4}{\kappa_2^2}
$$

**Theorem (properties).**

1. **Additivity.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\kappa_n(X + Y) = \kappa_n(X) + \kappa_n(Y)$ for every $n$.
2. **Shift.** $\kappa_1(X + b) = \kappa_1(X) + b$ and $\kappa_n(X + b) = \kappa_n(X)$ for $n \ge 2$.
3. **Scale.** $\kappa_n(aX) = a^n \kappa_n(X)$.
4. **Normal.** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has $\kappa_1 = \mu$, $\kappa_2 = \sigma^2$ and $\kappa_n = 0$ for $n \ge 3$. Conversely, a variable with an MGF whose cumulants vanish beyond the second is normal.
5. **Poisson and exponential.** $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ has $\kappa_n = \lambda$ for all $n$. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ (rate $\lambda$) has $\kappa_n = \frac{(n-1)!}{\lambda^n}$.

**In words:** cumulants add, shifting changes only the mean, scaling multiplies the $n$-th cumulant by $a^n$.

## Why It Is True

**Properties 1 to 3 from the CGF.** For independent $X$ and $Y$ the MGFs multiply ([15.3](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)), and the logarithm turns a product into a sum:

$$
K_{X+Y}(t) = \ln\big( M_X(t) M_Y(t) \big) = K_X(t) + K_Y(t)
$$

Two series that are equal have equal coefficients, so $\kappa_n(X+Y) = \kappa_n(X) + \kappa_n(Y)$. For a shift, $M_{X+b}(t) = e^{bt}M_X(t)$, so $K_{X+b}(t) = bt + K_X(t)$: only the coefficient of $t$ changes. For a scale, $M_{aX}(t) = M_X(at)$, so $K_{aX}(t) = K_X(at)$, and the coefficient of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$ is multiplied by $a^n$.

**Cumulants from moments.** By the shift property we may assume $E[X] = 0$, so $m_1 = 0$ and $m_n = \mu_n$. Then, with $\mu_2 = \sigma^2$,

$$
M_X(t) = 1 + u, \qquad u = \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} + \frac{\mu_3 t^3}{6} + \frac{\mu_4 t^4}{24} + \cdots
$$

Use $\ln(1 + u) = u - \frac{u^2}{2} + \cdots$. The first term of $u^2$ is $\frac{\sigma^4 t^4}{4}$, and the other terms of $u^2$ and all of $u^3$ start at $t^5$ or later. Collect the powers of $t$:

$$
K_X(t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2} t^2 + \frac{\mu_3}{6} t^3 + \left( \frac{\mu_4}{24} - \frac{\sigma^4}{8} \right) t^4 + \cdots
$$

Compare with $\kappa_n \frac{t^n}{n!}$: $\kappa_2 = \sigma^2$, $\kappa_3 = \mu_3$ and $\kappa_4 = 24\left( \frac{\mu_4}{24} - \frac{\sigma^4}{8} \right) = \mu_4 - 3\sigma^4$. This is also what [15.2](15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md) gave: $K'(0)$ is the mean and $K''(0)$ is the variance.

**Property 4.** $K(t) = \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}$ is a polynomial of degree 2, so every coefficient beyond $t^2$ is $0$. For the converse, the MGF is then $e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2 / 2}$, and uniqueness ([15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)) says the variable is normal.

**Property 5.** For the Poisson, $K(t) = \lambda(e^t - 1) = \lambda \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{t^n}{n!}$, so $\kappa_n = \lambda$. For the exponential, $K(t) = -\ln(1 - \frac{t}{\lambda})$, and the series $-\ln(1 - u) = \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{u^n}{n}$ gives

$$
K(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n \lambda^n} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(n-1)!}{\lambda^n} \cdot \frac{t^n}{n!}
$$

**Why standardized sums look normal.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. with cumulants $\kappa_r$, and $Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\kappa_1}{\sqrt{n\kappa_2}}$. By additivity, shift and scale, for $r \ge 3$:

$$
\kappa_r(Z_n) = \frac{n \kappa_r}{(n\kappa_2)^{r/2}} = n^{1 - r/2} \frac{\kappa_r}{\kappa_2^{r/2}} \to 0
$$

since $1 - \frac{r}{2} \lt 0$. Meanwhile $\kappa_1(Z_n) = 0$ and $\kappa_2(Z_n) = 1$. So the cumulants of $Z_n$ approach those of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. In particular the skewness of $Z_n$ is $\frac{\text{Skew}(X)}{\sqrt{n}}$ and its excess kurtosis is $\frac{\text{Excess kurtosis}(X)}{n}$. This is a second view of the CLT, which also shows the **speed**: skewness fades like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Shape of a Poisson Count

**Problem.** For $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ (mean $\lambda$) find the skewness and the excess kurtosis, and evaluate them for $\lambda = 4$.

**Solution.**

1. All cumulants are $\lambda$: $\kappa_2 = \kappa_3 = \kappa_4 = \lambda$.
2. Skewness: $\dfrac{\kappa_3}{\kappa_2^{3/2}} = \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda^{3/2}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}$.
3. Excess kurtosis: $\dfrac{\kappa_4}{\kappa_2^2} = \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda^2} = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}$.
4. For $\lambda = 4$: skewness $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$ and excess kurtosis $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$.

**Answer.** Skewness $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}$ and excess kurtosis $\frac{1}{\lambda}$; for $\lambda = 4$ they are $0.5$ and $0.25$.

**Check.** As $\lambda$ grows both go to $0$: a Poisson with a large mean is nearly normal, which is the normal approximation of
[7.5](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.5-Poisson.md) and [17.6](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md).
A Poisson with mean $4$ is a sum of four $\text{Pois}(1)$ variables, so by additivity its cumulants are 4 times as large, as expected.

### Example 2: How Fast Does a Sum of Exponentials Become Bell-Shaped?

**Problem.** A job has $n$ stages, each taking an $\text{Exp}(1)$ time (rate 1), independently. Find the skewness and excess kurtosis of the total time $T_n$, for $n = 1, 10, 100$.

**Solution.**

1. One stage: $\kappa_r = (r-1)!$, so $\kappa_2 = 1$, $\kappa_3 = 2$, $\kappa_4 = 6$.
2. By additivity, the total has $\kappa_r(T_n) = n (r-1)!$: $\kappa_2 = n$, $\kappa_3 = 2n$, $\kappa_4 = 6n$.
3. Skewness: $\dfrac{2n}{n^{3/2}} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}$. Excess kurtosis: $\dfrac{6n}{n^2} = \dfrac{6}{n}$.
4. Evaluate: $n = 1$: $2$ and $6$. $n = 10$: $\frac{2}{3.162} = 0.632$ and $0.6$. $n = 100$: $0.2$ and $0.06$.

**Answer.** Skewness $\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}$ and excess kurtosis $\frac{6}{n}$: $(2, 6)$, $(0.632, 0.6)$, $(0.2, 0.06)$.

**Check.** $T_n \sim \text{Gamma}(n, 1)$ and scipy gives the same numbers: for $n = 100$, skewness $0.2$ and excess kurtosis $0.06$. The skewness drops by $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$ when $n$ goes up by 10, as the
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ rule says.

### Example 3: Cumulants of a Binomial

**Problem.** Find $\kappa_3$ and $\kappa_4$ of $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$, then the skewness and excess kurtosis of $\text{Bin}(100, 0.1)$.

**Solution.**

1. $K(t) = \ln(1 - p + pe^t)$. Let $m(t) = K'(t) = \dfrac{pe^t}{1 - p + pe^t}$. By the quotient rule, $m'(t) = \dfrac{pe^t(1 - p)}{(1 - p + pe^t)^2} = m(t)\big(1 - m(t)\big)$.
2. So $K'' = m(1 - m) = m - m^2$. Differentiate again, using $m' = m - m^2$: $K''' = m'(1 - 2m) = m(1 - m)(1 - 2m) = m - 3m^2 + 2m^3$. And once more: $K'''' = m'(1 - 6m + 6m^2) = m(1-m)(1 - 6m + 6m^2)$.
3. At $t = 0$ the value of $m$ is $p$:

$$
\kappa_3 = p(1-p)(1 - 2p), \qquad \kappa_4 = p(1-p)\big(1 - 6p(1-p)\big)
$$

4. A binomial is a sum of $n = 100$ independent Bernoulli variables, so every cumulant is multiplied by 100. With $p = 0.1$: $\kappa_2 = 100 \times 0.09 = 9$, $\kappa_3 = 100 \times 0.09 \times 0.8 = 7.2$, and $\kappa_4 = 100 \times 0.09 \times (1 - 0.54) = 4.14$.
5. Skewness $= \dfrac{7.2}{9^{3/2}} = \dfrac{7.2}{27} \approx 0.2667$. Excess kurtosis $= \dfrac{4.14}{81} \approx 0.0511$.

**Answer.** $\kappa_3 = p(1-p)(1-2p)$ and $\kappa_4 = p(1-p)(1 - 6p(1-p))$ per trial; for $\text{Bin}(100, 0.1)$ the skewness is $0.2667$ and the excess kurtosis is $0.0511$.

**Check.** For $p = \frac{1}{2}$ the formula gives $\kappa_3 = 0$ (symmetric) and $\kappa_4 = \frac{1}{4}\left(1 - \frac{3}{2}\right) = -\frac{1}{8}$, matching the coin in the Intuition. The simulation below agrees with $0.2667$ and $0.0511$.

## Simulate It

The code estimates the first four cumulants of one die and of the sum of two dice (they should double), and then the skewness and excess kurtosis of sums of $n$ exponentials. A sample cumulant uses $\kappa_3 = E[(X - \mu)^3]$ and $\kappa_4 = E[(X - \mu)^4] - 3\sigma^4$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 2_000_000

def cumulants(x):
    d = x - x.mean()
    return x.mean(), np.mean(d**2), np.mean(d**3), np.mean(d**4) - 3 * np.mean(d**2) ** 2

def show(label, k):
    print(f"{label}: " + "  ".join(f"{v:8.3f}" for v in k))

# Cumulants add for independent variables. Exact for one die: 3.5, 2.917, 0, -10.792.
one = rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials)
two = rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials) + rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials)
show("one die ", cumulants(one))
show("two dice", cumulants(two))
print("exact two dice:  7.000     5.833     0.000   -21.583")

# A sum of n Exp(1) has kappa_r = n (r-1)!, so skewness 2/sqrt(n) and excess kurtosis 6/n.
for n in [1, 10, 100]:
    s = rng.exponential(1.0, (200_000, n)).sum(axis=1)
    k1, k2, k3, k4 = cumulants(s)
    print(f"sum of {n:>3} Exp(1): skewness {k3 / k2**1.5:.3f} vs {2 / np.sqrt(n):.3f}, "
          f"excess kurtosis {k4 / k2**2:.3f} vs {6 / n:.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
one die :    3.499     2.915     0.003   -10.771
two dice:    7.001     5.834    -0.009   -21.571
exact two dice:  7.000     5.833     0.000   -21.583
sum of   1 Exp(1): skewness 2.000 vs 2.000, excess kurtosis 6.027 vs 6.000
sum of  10 Exp(1): skewness 0.632 vs 0.632, excess kurtosis 0.589 vs 0.600
sum of 100 Exp(1): skewness 0.204 vs 0.200, excess kurtosis 0.064 vs 0.060
```

The two-dice cumulants are twice the one-die values, up to simulation noise. In the last three lines each pair is "simulation vs exact". The skewness falls like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ and the excess kurtosis like $\frac{1}{n}$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that all central moments add.** Only the second and third do. The fourth central moment of a sum has the cross term $6\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2$ (Practice 5). The fourth **cumulant** adds.
- **Mixing up kurtosis and excess kurtosis.** $\operatorname{Kurt} = \mu_4 / \sigma^4$ (normal: 3). The excess kurtosis is $\kappa_4 / \kappa_2^2 = \operatorname{Kurt} - 3$ (normal: 0).
- **Using the sum rule without independence.** $\kappa_n(X + Y) = \kappa_n(X) + \kappa_n(Y)$ needs independence. For $X + X = 2X$ use the scale rule: $\kappa_n(2X) = 2^n \kappa_n(X)$.
- **Taking $\ln$ of a moment instead of the MGF.** The CGF is $\ln E[e^{tX}]$, the log of the whole MGF, not $E[\ln(\cdot)]$ of anything.
- **Expecting cumulants when the MGF does not exist.** For the Cauchy or the log-normal there is no $K_X$ near $0$. (The log of the characteristic function still works for the distributions that have the moments.)

## Where It Is Used

- **The CLT and its speed:** the skewness of a standardized sum is $\frac{\text{Skew}}{\sqrt{n}}$, which is the first correction in the Berry-Esseen and Edgeworth results beyond this book
  ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** the log-partition function $A(\theta) = \ln \int e^{\theta T(x)} h(x) \thinspace dx$ of an exponential family is a CGF: its first derivative is the mean of the sufficient statistic and its second derivative is the variance. Independent component analysis (ICA) uses the fourth cumulant (kurtosis) to find non-normal directions, because mixing independent sources makes the data more normal.
- **Concentration:** the Chernoff bound uses $e^{-ta}M_X(t) = e^{K_X(t) - ta}$, so the best bound is found by minimizing $K_X(t) - ta$
  ([16.6](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)).
- **Finance:** return distributions are described by mean, variance, skewness and excess kurtosis (the first four cumulants), and adjustments to the normal model use them.
- **Physics:** the log of a partition function is a free energy, and its derivatives are the mean energy and its fluctuations.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Find all cumulants of $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ from its CGF.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$M_X(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2 / 2}$, so $K_X(t) = \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} t^2$. Reading the coefficients of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$: $\kappa_1 = \mu$, $\kappa_2 = \sigma^2$ and $\kappa_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 3$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Find the skewness and the excess kurtosis of $X \sim \text{Pois}(9)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{Skewness} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333, \qquad \text{Excess kurtosis} = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Show that every $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ variable has skewness $2$ and excess kurtosis $6$, whatever the rate $\lambda$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\kappa_n = \frac{(n-1)!}{\lambda^n}$, so $\kappa_2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$, $\kappa_3 = \frac{2}{\lambda^3}$ and $\kappa_4 = \frac{6}{\lambda^4}$. Then

$$\text{Skewness} = \frac{2/\lambda^3}{(1/\lambda^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2/\lambda^3}{1/\lambda^3} = 2, \qquad \text{Excess kurtosis} = \frac{6/\lambda^4}{(1/\lambda^2)^2} = 6$$

The shape numbers do not depend on $\lambda$, because $\lambda$ only changes the units (the scale), and scaling cancels in the ratios.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Find $\kappa_3$ for $X \sim \text{Bin}(20, 0.3)$ and the skewness.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Per trial, $\kappa_3 = p(1-p)(1-2p) = 0.3 \times 0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.084$. For 20 trials multiply by 20: $\kappa_3 = 1.68$. Also $\kappa_2 = 20 \times 0.21 = 4.2$, so

$$\text{Skewness} = \frac{1.68}{4.2^{3/2}} = \frac{1.68}{8.607} \approx 0.195$$

Positive: the long tail is on the right, since $p \lt \frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X, Y$ be independent with mean $0$ and variances $a = \sigma_X^2$, $b = \sigma_Y^2$. Show that $\mu_4(X + Y) = \mu_4(X) + \mu_4(Y) + 6ab$, so the fourth central moment does not add, but $\kappa_4$ does.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand $(X + Y)^4 = X^4 + 4X^3Y + 6X^2Y^2 + 4XY^3 + Y^4$ and take expectations. For independent variables $E[X^3Y] = E[X^3]E[Y] = 0$, $E[XY^3] = 0$ and $E[X^2Y^2] = ab$:

$$\mu_4(X + Y) = \mu_4(X) + 6ab + \mu_4(Y)$$

Now use $\kappa_4 = \mu_4 - 3\sigma^4$ with $\sigma^2_{X+Y} = a + b$:

$$\kappa_4(X + Y) = \mu_4(X) + \mu_4(Y) + 6ab - 3(a + b)^2 = \big( \mu_4(X) - 3a^2 \big) + \big( \mu_4(Y) - 3b^2 \big) = \kappa_4(X) + \kappa_4(Y)$$

because $3(a + b)^2 = 3a^2 + 6ab + 3b^2$ and the $6ab$ terms cancel.

</details>

## Summary

- Cumulants $\kappa_n$ are the coefficients of $K(t) = \ln M(t)$: $\kappa_1$ is the mean, $\kappa_2$ the variance, $\kappa_3 = \mu_3$, and $\kappa_4 = \mu_4 - 3\sigma^4$.
- Skewness is $\kappa_3/\kappa_2^{3/2}$ and excess kurtosis is $\kappa_4/\kappa_2^2$.
- For independent variables **every** cumulant adds; shifts change only $\kappa_1$; scaling multiplies $\kappa_n$ by $a^n$.
- The normal has $\kappa_n = 0$ for $n \ge 3$; the Poisson has all $\kappa_n = \lambda$; the exponential has $\kappa_n = (n-1)!/\lambda^n$.
- For a standardized sum, $\kappa_r \to 0$ like $n^{1 - r/2}$: this is the CLT, and the skewness fades like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{K_X(t) = \ln M_X(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kappa_n \frac{t^n}{n!}}
$$
