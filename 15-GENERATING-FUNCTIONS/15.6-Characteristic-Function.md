# 15.6 Characteristic Function

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 15: Generating Functions](README.md) / 15.6 Characteristic Function

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [15.5 Probability Generating Function](15.5-Probability-Generating-Function.md)

> **Big idea:** The characteristic function $\varphi_X(t) = E[e^{itX}]$ is the MGF with an imaginary exponent; because $e^{itX}$ always has length 1, it exists for every random variable, it determines the distribution, and it still turns sums into products.

---

## Why It Matters

The MGF has a weak point: $e^{tX}$ can be huge, so $E[e^{tX}]$ can be infinite. The
Cauchy and the log-normal show this ([15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)). The fix is
small but powerful: put an **imaginary** number in the exponent, so $e^{tX}$ becomes
$e^{itX}$. This number never gets larger than $1$ in size, so its average always
exists. The **characteristic function** keeps every good property of the MGF: it is a
fingerprint, it multiplies for independent sums, and it gives moments. It is the tool
that proves the Central Limit Theorem for every distribution with a finite variance
([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)), and it explains why the average
of Cauchy variables does not settle down ([9.9](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md)).

## Intuition

**The minimum of complex numbers you need.** The letter $i$ stands for a number with
$i^2 = -1$. A **complex number** is $a + bi$ with real $a$ and $b$; think of it as the
point $(a, b)$ on a plane. Its **length** is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, and its **conjugate** is
$a - bi$. We need one fact, **Euler's formula**: for any real angle $\theta$ (in radians),

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i \sin\theta
$$

This is the point on the circle of radius 1, at angle $\theta$. Its length is
$\sqrt{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta} = 1$. (Why the formula holds: put $x = i\theta$ in the series
$e^x = \sum_n x^n / n!$ and use $i^2 = -1$; the even powers give the series of $\cos\theta$ and the odd powers give $i$ times the
series of $\sin\theta$.) The usual rule $e^{a+b} = e^a e^b$ still works, so $e^{i(a+b)} = e^{ia} e^{ib}$. That is all.

**The idea.** Take a number $x$ and a chosen $t$. Turn $x$ into the point $e^{itx}$ on the
unit circle, at angle $tx$. This is like wrapping the number line around a circle. The
characteristic function is the **average position** of the wrapped points: the centre
of mass of all the values $e^{itX}$.

Think about the two extremes. If $X$ is a constant $c$, all points sit at the same place
$e^{itc}$, and their average is that point, on the circle, with length 1. If $X$ is very spread
out, the points are scattered all around the circle, and they cancel: the average is near
the centre, with length near 0. So $\lvert \varphi(t) \rvert$ measures how concentrated the wrapped values are. An
average of points on a circle cannot lie outside the circle, so $\lvert \varphi(t) \rvert \le 1$ always.

## Definition

The **characteristic function** of a random variable $X$ is the function of a real number $t$

$$
\varphi_X(t) = E\big[e^{itX}\big] = E[\cos(tX)] + i \thinspace E[\sin(tX)]
$$

For a discrete $X$ it is $\sum_x e^{itx} p_X(x)$, and for a continuous $X$ it is $\int e^{itx} f_X(x) \thinspace dx$.
The average of a complex number is taken separately for the real part and the imaginary part.

**In words:** the characteristic function at $t$ is the average of the point $e^{itX}$ on the unit circle.

**Theorem (properties).** For every random variable $X$:

1. **It always exists**, and $\varphi_X(0) = 1$, $\lvert \varphi_X(t) \rvert \le 1$ and $\varphi_X(-t)$ is the conjugate of $\varphi_X(t)$.
2. **Shift and scale:** $\varphi_{aX+b}(t) = e^{ibt} \varphi_X(at)$.
3. **Independent sums:** $\varphi_{X+Y}(t) = \varphi_X(t) \varphi_Y(t)$.
4. **Uniqueness:** if $\varphi_X(t) = \varphi_Y(t)$ for all real $t$, then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution. No other condition is needed.
5. **Continuity (Lévy):** if $\varphi_{X_n}(t) \to \varphi_X(t)$ for every real $t$, then $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$.
6. **Moments:** if $E[\lvert X \rvert^n] \lt \infty$, then $\varphi_X$ can be differentiated $n$ times and $E[X^n] = \dfrac{\varphi_X^{(n)}(0)}{i^n}$.
7. **Inversion:** if $\int \lvert \varphi_X(t) \rvert \thinspace dt \lt \infty$, then $X$ has the density
   $f_X(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\pi} \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-itx} \varphi_X(t) \thinspace dt$.

Common characteristic functions ($\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ has rate $\lambda$; $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$ is the standard Cauchy):

| Distribution | $\varphi_X(t)$ |
|---|---|
| $\text{Bern}(p)$ | $1 - p + pe^{it}$ |
| $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ | $\exp\big( \lambda (e^{it} - 1) \big)$ |
| $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ | $\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda - it}$ |
| $\text{Unif}(-1, 1)$ | $\dfrac{\sin t}{t}$ |
| $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\exp\left( i\mu t - \dfrac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} \right)$ |
| $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$ | $e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$ |

**In words:** to get the characteristic function from an MGF formula, put $it$ where $t$ was.
This works when the MGF exists. For the Cauchy there is no MGF, yet the characteristic function exists.

## Why It Is True

**It always exists (Property 1).** The average of a complex number $W$ satisfies $\lvert E[W] \rvert \le E[\lvert W \rvert]$
(the length of an average is at most the average of the lengths). With $W = e^{itX}$ we have $\lvert W \rvert = 1$, so

$$
\lvert \varphi_X(t) \rvert \le E\big[\lvert e^{itX} \rvert\big] = E[1] = 1
$$

This is the whole reason the characteristic function always exists. The MGF has the integrand $e^{tx}$, which grows
without a bound; the characteristic function has $e^{itx}$, which stays on the unit circle. Also
$\varphi_X(0) = E[e^0] = 1$, and $e^{-i\theta}$ is the conjugate of $e^{i\theta}$, which gives the symmetry.

**Properties 2 and 3.** Exactly as for the MGF: $E[e^{it(aX+b)}] = e^{ibt} E[e^{i(at)X}]$, and for independent $X, Y$
the average of the product $e^{itX} e^{itY}$ is the product of the averages ([15.3](15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)).

**Property 6 (moments).** Differentiate $e^{itX}$ with respect to $t$: its derivative is $iX e^{itX}$. At $t = 0$ that is $iX$. So
$\varphi'(0) = i E[X]$, and $n$ derivatives give $\varphi^{(n)}(0) = i^n E[X^n]$. In series form
$\varphi_X(t) = \sum_n \frac{(it)^n}{n!} E[X^n]$ when this converges. Notice what this says: **a moment exists exactly when
$\varphi$ is smooth enough at $0$**. The Cauchy corner at $t = 0$ means no mean.

**Properties 4, 5, 7.** The uniqueness, continuity and inversion theorems are proved with Fourier analysis, and we do not prove
them here. They are the full versions of the theorems in [15.4](15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md), without the
condition that an MGF exists.

<details>
<summary>Full proof that the standard normal has φ(t) = e^(-t^2/2) (optional)</summary>

Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ with density $\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}$, which satisfies $\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)$. Differentiate $\varphi(t) = \int e^{itz}\phi(z) \thinspace dz$ under the integral:

$$\varphi'(t) = \int iz \thinspace e^{itz} \phi(z) \thinspace dz = -i \int e^{itz} \phi'(z) \thinspace dz$$

Integrate by parts. The boundary term $e^{itz}\phi(z)$ is $0$ at both ends, so $\int e^{itz}\phi'(z) \thinspace dz = -\int it \thinspace e^{itz}\phi(z) \thinspace dz = -it \thinspace \varphi(t)$. Hence

$$\varphi'(t) = -i \cdot (-it) \thinspace \varphi(t) = i^2 t \thinspace \varphi(t) = -t \thinspace \varphi(t)$$

Then $\frac{d}{dt}\big( \varphi(t) e^{t^2/2} \big) = \big( \varphi'(t) + t \varphi(t) \big) e^{t^2/2} = 0$, so $\varphi(t) e^{t^2/2}$ is a constant, and it equals $\varphi(0) = 1$ at $t = 0$. So $\varphi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}$. For $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ use Property 2 with $X = \mu + \sigma Z$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Full proof that the standard Cauchy has φ(t) = e^(-|t|) (optional)</summary>

Start with the Laplace density $g(x) = \frac{1}{2}e^{-\lvert x \rvert}$. Its characteristic function is, using symmetry (the sine part cancels):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-\lvert x \rvert} e^{itx} \thinspace dx = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-x}\cos(tx) \thinspace dx = \operatorname{Re} \frac{1}{1 - it} = \frac{1}{1 + t^2}$$

This is integrable, so the inversion formula (Property 7) gives $\frac{1}{2}e^{-\lvert x \rvert} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-itx} \frac{1}{1 + t^2} \thinspace dt$. Multiply by 2:

$$e^{-\lvert x \rvert} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-itx} \frac{1}{\pi(1 + t^2)} \thinspace dt = E\big[e^{-ixC}\big]$$

where $C$ has the standard Cauchy density. This is $\varphi_C(-x)$, and since the Cauchy density is symmetric, $\varphi_C(-x) = \varphi_C(x)$. So $\varphi_C(x) = e^{-\lvert x \rvert}$.

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Random Sign and a Random Walk

**Problem.** A step $X$ is $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Find $\varphi_X$. Then find the characteristic function of $S_2 = X_1 + X_2$ for two independent steps, and read off the distribution of $S_2$.

**Solution.**

1. Use the definition and Euler's formula. $e^{it} + e^{-it} = 2\cos t$, so

$$
\varphi_X(t) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{it} + \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-it} = \cos t
$$

2. The steps are independent, so multiply: $\varphi_{S_2}(t) = \cos^2 t$.
3. Use $\cos^2 t = \frac{1 + \cos 2t}{2}$ and $\cos 2t = \frac{e^{2it} + e^{-2it}}{2}$:

$$
\varphi_{S_2}(t) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} e^{2it} + \tfrac{1}{4} e^{-2it}
$$

4. Each term $p\thinspace e^{itx}$ stands for the value $x$ with probability $p$. By uniqueness, $S_2$ is $0$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and $+2$ or $-2$ with probability $\frac{1}{4}$ each.

**Answer.** $\varphi_X(t) = \cos t$, and $S_2 \in \lbrace -2, 0, 2 \rbrace$ with probabilities $\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}$.

**Check.** Direct count: two steps give $(+,+), (-,-)$ for $\pm 2$ (one pair each, $\frac{1}{4}$) and $(+,-), (-,+)$ for $0$ ($\frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$). Also $\varphi_{S_2}(0) = 1$.

### Example 2: Moments of a Uniform Variable

**Problem.** For $X \sim \text{Unif}(-1, 1)$ find $\varphi_X(t)$ and use it to find $E[X^2]$ and $E[X^4]$.

**Solution.**

1. The density is $\frac{1}{2}$ on $[-1, 1]$:

$$
\varphi_X(t) = \int_{-1}^{1} \tfrac{1}{2} e^{itx} \thinspace dx = \frac{e^{it} - e^{-it}}{2it} = \frac{2i \sin t}{2it} = \frac{\sin t}{t}
$$

   (using $e^{it} - e^{-it} = 2i\sin t$).
2. Expand with $\sin t = t - \frac{t^3}{6} + \frac{t^5}{120} - \cdots$:

$$
\varphi_X(t) = 1 - \frac{t^2}{6} + \frac{t^4}{120} - \cdots
$$

3. Compare with $\varphi_X(t) = \sum_n \frac{(it)^n}{n!} E[X^n] = 1 - \frac{t^2}{2} E[X^2] + \frac{t^4}{24} E[X^4] - \cdots$ (since $i^2 = -1$, $i^4 = 1$).
4. Match the $t^2$ terms: $\frac{E[X^2]}{2} = \frac{1}{6}$, so $E[X^2] = \frac{1}{3}$. Match the $t^4$ terms: $\frac{E[X^4]}{24} = \frac{1}{120}$, so $E[X^4] = \frac{1}{5}$.

**Answer.** $\varphi_X(t) = \frac{\sin t}{t}$, $E[X^2] = \frac{1}{3}$ and $E[X^4] = \frac{1}{5}$.

**Check.** Directly, $E[X^2] = \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x^2}{2} dx = \frac{1}{3}$, which is also $\frac{(b-a)^2}{12} = \frac{4}{12}$. And $E[X^4] = \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x^4}{2} dx = \frac{1}{5}$. The odd moments are $0$, because only even powers of $t$ appear.

### Example 3: Averaging Cauchy Variables Does Not Help

**Problem.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. standard Cauchy and $\bar{X}_n$ their mean. Find the distribution of $\bar{X}_n$, and $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \lt 1)$.

**Solution.**

1. One term has $\varphi(t) = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$.
2. For the sum, multiply $n$ copies: $\varphi_{S_n}(t) = e^{-n\lvert t \rvert}$. For the mean, $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n} S_n$, so replace $t$ by $\frac{t}{n}$:

$$
\varphi_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = \left( e^{-\lvert t \rvert / n} \right)^n = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}
$$

3. This is again the standard Cauchy characteristic function. By uniqueness, $\bar{X}_n$ is standard Cauchy for **every** $n$.
4. For a standard Cauchy, $P(\lvert C \rvert \lt 1) = \frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(1) = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Answer.** $\bar{X}_n$ has the same distribution as a single $X_1$, and $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \lt 1) = \frac{1}{2}$ for all $n$.

**Check.** For a variable with a finite variance, the mean of $n$ values would be squeezed toward the true mean as $n$ grows. Here nothing improves: one thousand
Cauchy values give an average that is just as wild as one value. The Law of Large Numbers and the CLT fail
([17.9](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)). The simulation agrees for $n = 10$.

## Simulate It

The code estimates $E[\cos(tX)]$, the real part of $\varphi_X(t)$, for the normal and the Cauchy. It
checks that the mean of 10 Cauchy values has the same $\varphi$ as one value, and it shows that the log-normal, which has no MGF,
has a perfectly good characteristic function: the simulation and a numerical integral agree.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# (a) Standard normal: phi(t) = exp(-t^2/2). Estimate E[cos(tX)]; the sine part averages to 0.
z = rng.standard_normal(n_trials)
for t in [0.5, 1, 2]:
    print(f"normal, t = {t}: simulation {np.mean(np.cos(t * z)):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-t**2 / 2):.4f}")

# (b) Cauchy: phi(t) = exp(-|t|). The mean of 10 Cauchy values is again standard Cauchy.
c = rng.standard_cauchy((n_trials // 10, 10))
avg = c.mean(axis=1)
print(f"Cauchy, one value:   phi(1) simulation {np.mean(np.cos(c[:, 0])):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-1):.4f}")
print(f"Cauchy, mean of 10:  phi(1) simulation {np.mean(np.cos(avg)):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-1):.4f}")
print(f"P(|mean of 10| < 1) = {np.mean(np.abs(avg) < 1):.4f}   (one value: 0.5000)")

# (c) Log-normal has no MGF, but phi(1) = E[cos X] + i E[sin X] exists. Compare with an integral.
x = np.exp(rng.standard_normal(n_trials))
y = np.linspace(-10, 6, 1_600_001)
w = np.exp(-y**2 / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) * (y[1] - y[0])
print(f"log-normal phi(1): simulation {np.mean(np.cos(x)):.4f} + {np.mean(np.sin(x)):.4f}i")
print(f"                   integral   {np.sum(np.cos(np.exp(y)) * w):.4f} + {np.sum(np.sin(np.exp(y)) * w):.4f}i")
```

Output:

```text
normal, t = 0.5: simulation 0.8824, exact 0.8825
normal, t = 1: simulation 0.6065, exact 0.6065
normal, t = 2: simulation 0.1360, exact 0.1353
Cauchy, one value:   phi(1) simulation 0.3658, exact 0.3679
Cauchy, mean of 10:  phi(1) simulation 0.3668, exact 0.3679
P(|mean of 10| < 1) = 0.4983   (one value: 0.5000)
log-normal phi(1): simulation 0.3395 + 0.5071i
                   integral   0.3403 + 0.5072i
```

Every estimate is stable, even for the Cauchy and the log-normal, because $\cos$ and $\sin$ are bounded between $-1$ and $1$.
The estimate of $E[e^{tX}]$ for the same variables (the MGF) was not stable in [15.1](15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating $\varphi$ as a real function.** In general $\varphi_X(t)$ is a complex number. It is real exactly when $X$ is symmetric about $0$ (Practice 5). Do not drop the imaginary part unless you have a reason.
- **Forgetting the absolute value in the Cauchy.** $\varphi(t) = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$, not $e^{-t}$. The $\lvert t \rvert$ is what makes the corner at $0$.
- **Getting moments without the factor $i^n$.** $\varphi'(0) = iE[X]$, not $E[X]$. Divide $\varphi^{(n)}(0)$ by $i^n$.
- **Replacing $t$ by $it$ blindly.** The rule of putting $it$ in place of $t$ works only when the MGF exists. The Cauchy has no MGF at all, so there is nothing to substitute into; its $\varphi$ is $e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$.
- **Thinking that equal products imply independence.** If $\varphi_{X+Y} = \varphi_X \varphi_Y$ the variables need not be independent. Example: take $Y = X$ standard Cauchy; then $\varphi_{2X}(t) = e^{-2\lvert t \rvert} = \varphi_X(t)^2$, but $X$ and $X$ are certainly dependent.

## Where It Is Used

- **Limit theorems:** the CLT for every distribution with a finite variance
  ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)), using Lévy's continuity theorem (Property 5), and the failure of the LLN for the Cauchy.
- **Heavy tails and stable laws:** distributions such as the Cauchy are defined by their characteristic function, because
  their densities and MGFs are awkward or missing.
- **Signal processing:** $\varphi_X$ is the Fourier transform of the density, the same tool as the frequency view of a sound or an image. Adding independent noise multiplies the transforms.
- **AI and machine learning:** random Fourier features approximate a kernel by writing it as a characteristic function, $k(x - y) = E[e^{i\omega(x-y)}]$ for a random frequency $\omega$. Some generative models compare distributions through their characteristic functions.
- **Finance:** option prices in models with jumps are computed by inverting the characteristic function of the log-price.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** For $X \sim \text{Bern}(0.3)$, find $\varphi_X(t)$ and evaluate it at $t = \pi$. (Use $e^{i\pi} = -1$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\varphi_X(t) = 0.7 + 0.3e^{it}$$

At $t = \pi$: $\varphi_X(\pi) = 0.7 + 0.3 \times (-1) = 0.4$. It is real and its length $0.4$ is at most $1$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Show that $\lvert e^{i\theta} \rvert = 1$ for $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$, and explain why $\lvert \varphi_X(t) \rvert \le 1$ for every random variable.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$e^{i\pi/3} = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, with length $\sqrt{\frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4}} = 1$. In general $\lvert e^{itX} \rvert = 1$, and the length of an average is at most the average of the lengths, so $\lvert \varphi_X(t) \rvert \le 1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = 3Z + 1$. Find $\varphi_Y(t)$ and identify the distribution of $Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Property 2, with $a = 3$ and $b = 1$:

$$\varphi_Y(t) = e^{it} \varphi_Z(3t) = e^{it} e^{-9t^2/2} = \exp\left( i \cdot 1 \cdot t - \frac{9 t^2}{2} \right)$$

This is the normal characteristic function with $\mu = 1$ and $\sigma^2 = 9$. By uniqueness, $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(1, 9)$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ show that $\varphi_X(t) = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - it}$, then find $E[X]$ from $\varphi'(0)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\varphi_X(t) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{itx} \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx = \lambda \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(\lambda - it)x} \thinspace dx = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - it}$$

The last step holds because $\lvert e^{-(\lambda - it)x} \rvert = e^{-\lambda x}$ goes to $0$. Differentiate: $\varphi'(t) = \frac{i\lambda}{(\lambda - it)^2}$, so $\varphi'(0) = \frac{i}{\lambda}$. Then

$$E[X] = \frac{\varphi'(0)}{i} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $X$ is symmetric about $0$ (that is, $X$ and $-X$ have the same distribution) if and only if $\varphi_X(t)$ is real for every $t$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Property 2 with $a = -1$, $\varphi_{-X}(t) = \varphi_X(-t)$, and by Property 1 this equals the conjugate $\overline{\varphi_X(t)}$.

If $X$ and $-X$ have the same distribution, they have the same characteristic function, so $\varphi_X(t) = \overline{\varphi_X(t)}$. A complex number equal to its conjugate is real.

Conversely, if $\varphi_X(t)$ is real, then $\overline{\varphi_X(t)} = \varphi_X(t)$, so $\varphi_{-X} = \varphi_X$. By uniqueness (Property 4), $-X$ and $X$ have the same distribution.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate $\varphi(1)$ for $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and compare with the exact value $\frac{1}{1 - i} = \frac{1 + i}{2}$.

## Summary

- The characteristic function $\varphi_X(t) = E[e^{itX}]$ uses $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, a point on the unit circle.
- It exists for **every** random variable, since $\lvert e^{itX} \rvert = 1$ and so $\lvert \varphi_X(t) \rvert \le 1$.
- It multiplies for independent sums, $\varphi_{aX+b}(t) = e^{ibt}\varphi_X(at)$, and gives moments by $E[X^n] = \varphi^{(n)}(0)/i^n$ when they exist.
- It determines the distribution with no conditions, and convergence of $\varphi$ gives convergence in distribution.
- Cauchy: $\varphi(t) = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$, so the mean of $n$ Cauchy values is still Cauchy; the corner at $t = 0$ means no mean.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\varphi_X(t) = E\big[e^{itX}\big] = E[\cos(tX)] + i \thinspace E[\sin(tX)]}
$$
