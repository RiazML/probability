# 17.9 When the CLT Fails

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.9 When the CLT Fails

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [17.8 Slutsky's Theorem](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md)

> **Big idea:** The Law of Large Numbers needs a finite mean and the Central Limit Theorem needs a finite variance; for heavy-tailed data such as the Cauchy, averages never settle, and for the Pareto with infinite variance they settle slowly and are not normal.

---

## Why It Matters

The LLN and the CLT are so successful that it is easy to think they work for all data. They do not. Each one has an assumption, and some of the most important real data break it: financial returns, insurance claims, file sizes, network traffic, city populations and the revenue per customer. In these data one huge value can be larger than all the others together. If you average such data and then report "mean plus or minus 1.96 standard errors", the report can be wrong by orders of magnitude. This topic shows exactly how and why the theorems fail, and what takes their place.

## Intuition

Spin a laser pointer in a random direction toward a long wall ([9.9](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md)). The dot lands at position $X$. Most of the time $X$ is small. But when the beam is almost parallel to the wall, the dot lands 100 meters, 1 kilometer or 1000 kilometers away. These rare spins are not rare enough. They happen often enough that **one** of them is bigger than the sum of all the other spins.

Now average 1,000 spins. If the biggest spin was 1000 kilometers, it contributes 1 kilometer to the average, however many ordinary spins you have. So the average of 1,000 spins is not tame. In fact it has exactly the same distribution as one spin. Averaging does not help at all.

Compare this with heights of people. The tallest person is 2.3 meters. Averaging 1,000 people, the tallest adds 2.3 millimeters. Every value matters a little, and none matters a lot. That is the situation where the LLN and the CLT work.

## Picture

```text
  100      # 0.0674                      #################### 1
  1,000    . 0.0213                      #################### 1

```

For normal data, the average gets 10 times more precise with 100 times more data. For Cauchy data, the average stays exactly as wild as a single value for ever. (The numbers are exact. The simulation below confirms them.)

## Definition

Which assumption does each theorem need? The table also shows which classic distributions fail.

| Theorem | Needs | Fails for |
|---|---|---|
| LLN (weak and strong) | finite mean: $E\lvert X \rvert \lt \infty$ | Cauchy, Pareto with $\alpha \le 1$, St. Petersburg game |
| CLT | finite variance: $E[X^2] \lt \infty$ | Cauchy, Pareto with $1 \lt \alpha \lt 2$, Student $t$ with 1.5 degrees of freedom |
| Both | independence (or weak dependence) | $X_1 = X_2 = \cdots$ (all copies of one variable) |

A **heavy tail** is a tail that falls like a power $x^{-\alpha}$ rather than exponentially. The **Pareto distribution** with parameter $\alpha \gt 0$ and minimum value 1 has

$$
P(X \gt x) = x^{-\alpha} \quad \text{for } x \ge 1
$$

Its mean is finite only if $\alpha \gt 1$ (and then equals $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha - 1}$). Its variance is finite only if $\alpha \gt 2$. (At exactly $\alpha = 2$ the variance is infinite, and the sum is still asymptotically normal, but only with the slower scaling $\sqrt{n \ln n}$.)

**What replaces the CLT.** Let $X_i$ be i.i.d. with tail $P(\lvert X \rvert \gt x) \approx C x^{-\alpha}$ for some $0 \lt \alpha \lt 2$. Then the sum, scaled by $n^{1/\alpha}$ instead of $\sqrt{n}$, converges to a non-normal limit called an **$\alpha$-stable distribution**. This is the **generalized CLT**. We state it without proof. The normal is the stable law with $\alpha = 2$, the Cauchy is the stable law with $\alpha = 1$.

**In words:** with a power tail, sums grow like $n^{1/\alpha}$, which is faster than $\sqrt{n}$ when $\alpha \lt 2$, and the limit is a heavy-tailed shape, not a bell.

## Why It Is True

### The Mean of Cauchy Values Is Cauchy

A standard Cauchy variable has density $\frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$ and characteristic function $\varphi(t) = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$ ([15.6](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)). For the average of $n$ independent copies:

$$
\varphi_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = \left[\varphi\left(\tfrac{t}{n}\right)\right]^n = \left(e^{-\lvert t \rvert / n}\right)^n = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}
$$

This is the characteristic function of a single standard Cauchy. By uniqueness ([15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md), [15.6](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)), $\bar{X}_n$ is a standard Cauchy for every $n$. So $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \gt 1) = \frac{1}{2}$ for all $n$, and $\bar{X}_n$ does not converge to anything: the Law of Large Numbers fails.

The reason is that the mean does not exist. The mean would be $\int \frac{\lvert x \rvert}{\pi(1 + x^2)} dx$, and for large $x$ the integrand is about $\frac{1}{\pi x}$, whose integral grows like $\ln x$ and diverges. The distribution is symmetric about 0, which tempts us to say "the mean is 0", but a mean exists only if the positive part and the negative part are both finite. Here each is infinite.

### Where the CLT Proof Breaks

The proof of the CLT ([17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md), [15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)) needs the expansion $\varphi_Y(s) = 1 - \frac{s^2}{2} + o(s^2)$ near $s = 0$ for the standardized piece $Y$. This holds only if $E[Y^2] = 1$ is finite. For the Cauchy, $\varphi(s) = e^{-\lvert s \rvert} = 1 - \lvert s \rvert + \cdots$. The leading term is $\lvert s \rvert$, which is much bigger than $s^2$ near 0 (the curve has a sharp corner at 0, so it is not even twice differentiable). If we insist on dividing by $\sqrt{n}$, we get

$$
\left[\varphi\left(\tfrac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n = e^{-\sqrt{n}\lvert t \rvert} \to 0 \quad \text{for every } t \ne 0
$$

which is not the characteristic function of any distribution: the standardized sum escapes to infinity. The right scaling for the Cauchy is $n$, not $\sqrt{n}$, which is the $n^{1/\alpha}$ rule with $\alpha = 1$.

### Finite Mean, Infinite Variance: Slow and Not Normal

Take a Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$. By direct integration (density $1.5\thinspace x^{-2.5}$ on $[1, \infty)$):

$$
E[X] = \int_1^{\infty} x \cdot 1.5\thinspace x^{-2.5} dx = 1.5 \int_1^{\infty} x^{-1.5} dx = 1.5 \times 2 = 3, \qquad E[X^2] = 1.5 \int_1^{\infty} x^{-0.5} dx = \infty
$$

The LLN holds: the average goes to 3 (the strong law needs only a finite mean). But the CLT does not: the error of the average is of order $n^{1/\alpha - 1} = n^{-1/3}$ instead of $n^{-1/2}$, and its shape is a stable law. So 10 times more data reduces the error by only $10^{1/3} = 2.15$, not by $\sqrt{10} = 3.16$. The sample variance $S_n^2$ does not converge either (it keeps growing), so a standard error computed from it is meaningless.

### Infinite Mean: the St. Petersburg Game

Flip a fair coin until the first head, which comes at flip $K$ with probability $2^{-K}$. You win $2^K$ dollars. Then $E[\text{win}] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 2^k \cdot 2^{-k} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 1 = \infty$. There is no mean for the averages to converge to. The average of $n$ games grows like $\log_2 n$: the more games, the bigger the average.

### Other Ways the Theorems Fail

- **Dependence.** If $X_1 = X_2 = \cdots = X$, then $\bar{X}_n = X$ and nothing averages out ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md), Practice 4). Weak dependence is fine, but strong dependence is not.
- **One term dominates.** If the pieces are independent but not identical, and one piece has most of the variance, the sum looks like that one piece. The CLT needs a condition (the Lindeberg condition) that says that no single piece matters much.
- **Slow convergence, not failure.** For a very skewed distribution with finite variance, the CLT is true but needs a huge $n$. The log-normal ([9.8](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.8-Log-Normal.md)) with $\sigma = 1$ has skewness $(e + 2)\sqrt{e - 1} = 6.18$. The rule of thumb from [17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md) ($\text{error} \approx \frac{0.0665\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}$) says that about 1,700 terms are needed to reduce the error of the normal approximation to 0.01.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Averaging Cauchy Values Versus Normal Values

**Problem.** Let $X_i$ be i.i.d. standard Cauchy. Find $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \lt 1)$ for $n = 1, 10, 100$ and compare with i.i.d. standard normal data.

**Solution.**

1. For the Cauchy, $\bar{X}_n$ is standard Cauchy for every $n$. The CDF of a standard Cauchy is $F(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(x)$, so

$$
P(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \lt 1) = F(1) - F(-1) = \frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(1) = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

2. For standard normal data, $\bar{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{1}{n})$, so $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \lt 1) = 2\Phi(\sqrt{n}) - 1$.
3. For $n = 1$: $2\Phi(1) - 1 = 0.6827$. For $n = 10$: $2\Phi(3.162) - 1 = 0.9984$. For $n = 100$: $2\Phi(10) - 1 = 1 - 1.5 \times 10^{-23}$.

| $n$ | 1 | 10 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal data | $0.6827$ | $0.9984$ | $1 - 10^{-23}$ |
| Cauchy data | $0.5$ | $0.5$ | $0.5$ |

**Answer.** For the Cauchy the probability stays at $\frac{1}{2}$. For the normal it goes to 1.

**Check.** The simulation below gives the half-width of the middle 50% of the average as $0.98$, $1.00$, $1.02$, $1.00$ and $1.04$ for $n = 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10000$ (close to the exact value 1 for every $n$), against $0.66$, $0.21$, $0.065$, $0.022$ and $0.0068$ for normal data.

### Example 2: Pareto With Infinite Variance

**Problem.** A web server returns files whose sizes (in kilobytes) are Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$ and minimum size 1. (a) Find the mean file size. (b) How likely is a single file bigger than 100 kilobytes? (c) Is the average of $n$ file sizes approximately normal?

**Solution.**

1. (a) From the calculation above, the mean is $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha - 1} = \frac{1.5}{0.5} = 3$ kilobytes.
2. (b) $P(X \gt 100) = 100^{-1.5} = \frac{1}{1000} = 0.001$. So 1 file in 1000 is more than 100 kilobytes, which is 33 times the mean.
3. (c) The variance is infinite ($E[X^2] = \infty$), so the CLT does not apply. The average still converges to 3 (the Strong Law), but the error shrinks like $n^{-1/3}$, and the shape of the error is a skewed stable law.
4. A check from the simulation below: the half-width of the middle 50% of the average is $0.358$ for $n = 100$ and $0.0398$ for $n = 100{,}000$. A thousand times more files reduces the spread by a factor $9.0$, not by $\sqrt{1000} = 31.6$ as for finite-variance data. The prediction for the power law is $1000^{1/3} = 10$.

**Answer.** (a) 3 kilobytes. (b) $0.001$. (c) No: the error is not normal and shrinks more slowly.

**Check.** Planning with a normal approximation would give confidence intervals that are too narrow. A heavy-tailed average needs much more data for the same precision, and the interval should not be computed from the sample variance.

### Example 3: The St. Petersburg Game

**Problem.** In the St. Petersburg game you win $2^K$ dollars, where $K$ is the number of flips until the first head. (a) Show that the expected win is infinite. (b) What is the expected win if the bank can pay at most $2^{20}$ dollars (about 1.05 million dollars)? (c) How does the average of $n$ games behave?

**Solution.**

1. (a) $P(K = k) = 2^{-k}$ and the win is $2^k$, so each term of the expectation is $2^k \cdot 2^{-k} = 1$. The sum of infinitely many ones is infinite.
2. (b) With the cap, a win is $\min(2^K, 2^{20})$. For $k \le 20$ each term still equals 1, which gives 20. For $k \gt 20$ the win is $2^{20}$ and the total probability of $K \gt 20$ is $2^{-20}$. So the expected win is $20 + 2^{20} \cdot 2^{-20} = 21$ dollars.
3. (c) With a bank limit of $2^m$ dollars the fair price is $m + 1$. In $n$ games the biggest win is typically around $n$ dollars (a win of $2^k$ has probability $2^{-k}$, so about one game in $n$ pays $n$ or more). So the game behaves like one with a bank limit near $n = 2^m$, where $m = \log_2 n$. The average win of $n$ games is therefore of the order of $\log_2 n$: it grows by about $\log_2 100 = 6.6$ dollars each time we play 100 times more games.

**Answer.** (a) Infinite. (b) 21 dollars. (c) The average grows without bound, like $\log_2 n$.

**Check.** The code in Practice 6 gives a median average of $9.3$, $16.0$ and $22.5$ dollars for $n = 100$, $10^4$ and $10^6$. The steps between them are $6.7$ and $6.5$, as predicted by $\log_2 100 = 6.6$. The Law of Large Numbers fails: there is no limit.

## Simulate It

Part 1 compares the spread of the average of $n$ normal values and of $n$ Cauchy values. Part 2 shows how the average of Pareto ($\alpha = 1.5$) values has a spread that shrinks only like $n^{-1/3}$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def iqr_half(x):
    """Half the distance between the 25% and 75% quantiles: how far the middle half spreads."""
    q1, q3 = np.quantile(x, [0.25, 0.75])
    return (q3 - q1) / 2

# Part 1: spread of the average of n values. Normal: 0.6745/sqrt(n). Cauchy: always 1.
print("      n   normal data   Cauchy data")
for n in [1, 10, 100, 1000, 10_000]:
    n_runs = 10_000
    normal_mean = rng.normal(size=(n_runs, n)).mean(axis=1)
    cauchy_mean = rng.standard_cauchy(size=(n_runs, n)).mean(axis=1)
    print(f"{n:>7}   {iqr_half(normal_mean):>11.4f}   {iqr_half(cauchy_mean):>11.4f}")

# Part 2: Pareto with alpha = 1.5 (mean 3, infinite variance). The mean still settles,
# but its spread shrinks only like n^(-1/3), not n^(-1/2).
print("\n      n   spread of Pareto average   ratio to previous   (10^(1/3) = 2.15)")
previous = None
for n in [100, 1000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    n_runs = 3000
    means = np.array([(1 + rng.pareto(1.5, size=(100, n))).mean(axis=1) for _ in range(n_runs // 100)]).ravel()
    spread = iqr_half(means)
    ratio = f"{previous / spread:>8.2f}" if previous else "       -"
    print(f"{n:>7}   {spread:>22.4f}   {ratio}")
    previous = spread
```

Output:

```text
      n   normal data   Cauchy data
      1        0.6636        0.9778
     10        0.2126        1.0001
    100        0.0650        1.0192
   1000        0.0218        0.9986
  10000        0.0068        1.0358

      n   spread of Pareto average   ratio to previous   (10^(1/3) = 2.15)
    100                   0.3582          -
   1000                   0.1827       1.96
  10000                   0.0867       2.11
 100000                   0.0398       2.18
```

For normal data the spread follows $\frac{0.6745}{\sqrt{n}}$. For Cauchy data it stays near 1 for every $n$ (the small differences from 1 are simulation noise, since each row uses 10,000 runs). For Pareto data each factor of 10 in $n$ reduces the spread by about 2 (close to $10^{1/3} = 2.15$), not by 3.16.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the CLT because $n$ is large.** A large $n$ does not repair an infinite variance. The theorem needs finite variance, not just many values.
- **Saying the Cauchy has mean 0 because it is symmetric.** Symmetry is not enough: the mean exists only if $E\lvert X \rvert \lt \infty$. For the Cauchy it is infinite.
- **Trusting a sample mean and a standard error from heavy-tailed data.** The sample variance does not converge, so "mean $\pm$ 1.96 standard errors" is meaningless. Check the tail (is $P(X \gt x)$ a power law?) before averaging.
- **Believing that more data always fixes it.** For Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$ you gain only a factor $n^{-1/3}$, and for the Cauchy you gain nothing. Use a different estimator, such as the median or a trimmed mean, or the "median of means" trick.
- **Confusing slow with false.** For a log-normal the CLT is true but needs thousands of terms. Failing means no limit or a wrong limit; slow means the limit is right but far away.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance and risk:** daily returns have heavy tails. Averages of returns, and variances estimated from a few years of data, can be dominated by a single crash. Risk models use stable or Student $t$ distributions instead of the normal.
- **Networks and computer science:** file sizes, web page popularity and job runtimes follow power laws ([9.13](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)). Average load is dominated by rare huge items, so capacity planning must look at the tail, not only at the mean.
- **Machine learning:** the noise in stochastic gradients can be heavy-tailed. This is one reason for gradient clipping and for robust losses. Median-of-means estimators keep guarantees when only a finite variance is known.
- **Insurance and catastrophes:** claims from earthquakes and floods have infinite or huge variance, so the premium cannot be set from a normal approximation of the average claim.
- **Everyday life:** the "average" income of a room changes completely when one billionaire enters. The median is the stable summary for heavy-tailed data.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_{100}$ be i.i.d. standard Cauchy. What is $P(\lvert \bar{X}_{100} \rvert \lt 1)$? What would it be for a single value $X_1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The average of Cauchy values is Cauchy with the same scale, so both probabilities are the same:

$$P(\lvert \bar{X}_{100} \rvert \lt 1) = P(\lvert X_1 \rvert \lt 1) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

Averaging 100 values gave no gain at all.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For each distribution, say whether the Law of Large Numbers and the CLT hold: $\text{Exp}(1)$; Pareto with $\alpha = 3$; Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$; the standard Cauchy.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\text{Exp}(1)$: both hold (finite mean and variance). Pareto with $\alpha = 3$: both hold ($\alpha \gt 2$, so the variance is finite). Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$: the LLN holds (mean $3$), the CLT fails (infinite variance). Standard Cauchy: neither holds.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$ and minimum 1, show that $E[X] = 3$ and $E[X^2] = \infty$. What is the probability that a value exceeds 10,000?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The density is $1.5x^{-2.5}$ for $x \ge 1$. Then

$$E[X] = 1.5\int_1^{\infty} x^{-1.5}dx = 1.5 \cdot \Big[ -2x^{-0.5} \Big]_1^{\infty} = 1.5 \cdot 2 = 3$$

$$E[X^2] = 1.5\int_1^{\infty} x^{-0.5}dx = 1.5 \cdot \Big[ 2x^{0.5} \Big]_1^{\infty} = \infty$$

The tail is $P(X \gt 10000) = 10000^{-1.5} = 10^{-6}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In the St. Petersburg game, what is the expected win if the bank can pay at most $2^{30}$ dollars? What is the general formula for a cap of $2^m$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The terms for $k = 1, \dots, m$ each equal $2^k \cdot 2^{-k} = 1$, which gives $m$. The remaining probability $P(K \gt m) = 2^{-m}$ pays $2^m$, which adds $2^m \cdot 2^{-m} = 1$. So the expected win is $m + 1$ dollars: $31$ dollars for $m = 30$. A cap of one billion dollars makes the game worth only about 31 dollars.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. standard Cauchy with characteristic function $e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$. Prove that $\bar{X}_n$ is standard Cauchy, and deduce that $\bar{X}_n$ does not converge in probability to any constant $c$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By independence the characteristic function of the sum is $e^{-n\lvert t \rvert}$. The average is $\frac{1}{n}$ times the sum, so replace $t$ by $\frac{t}{n}$:

$$\varphi_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = e^{-n\lvert t/n \rvert} = e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$$

By uniqueness $\bar{X}_n$ is standard Cauchy. Suppose $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} c$. Then $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{d} c$ ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)), so its CDF would converge to the step function at $c$. But the CDF of $\bar{X}_n$ is the same continuous Cauchy CDF for every $n$, which is not a step function. This is a contradiction, so there is no constant limit.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the average of $n$ St. Petersburg games for $n = 100$, $10^4$ and $10^6$, using 1,000 runs for each. Print the median average next to $\log_2 n$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs = 1000
print("        n   median average   log2(n)   largest average / smallest average")
for n in [100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    averages = np.empty(n_runs)
    for i in range(n_runs):
        k = rng.geometric(0.5, size=n)           # flips until the first head: P(K = k) = 2^-k
        averages[i] = (2.0 ** k).mean()          # payoff 2^K, averaged over n games
    print(f"{n:>9}   {np.median(averages):>14.1f}   {np.log2(n):>7.1f}   "
          f"{averages.max() / averages.min():>20.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
        n   median average   log2(n)   largest average / smallest average
      100              9.3       6.6                  618.8
    10000             16.0      13.3                  168.5
  1000000             22.5      19.9                  132.1
```

The median average grows by about 6.6 for every factor of 100 in $n$, like $\log_2 n$. The last column shows that the averages of different runs differ by a factor of more than 100 even for $n = 10^6$: the average is also extremely unstable.

</details>

## Summary

- The LLN needs a finite mean, and the CLT needs a finite variance. Heavy tails ($P(X \gt x)$ falling like a power $x^{-\alpha}$) can break either one.
- For the Cauchy, the average of $n$ values is again Cauchy, so averaging does nothing and the LLN fails. The CLT proof fails because $E[X^2] = \infty$: the characteristic function has a corner $1 - \lvert s \rvert$, not a bend $1 - \frac{s^2}{2}$.
- For $1 \lt \alpha \lt 2$ the average converges but the error shrinks like $n^{1/\alpha - 1}$ and is not normal (the generalized CLT, with stable limits). For an infinite mean, as in the St. Petersburg game, the average grows without limit.
- Dependence and one dominating term also break the CLT. Slow convergence (log-normal) is different from failure.
- For heavy-tailed data use the median, trimmed means or median-of-means, and look at the tail before you average.

The one idea to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{LLN needs } E\lvert X \rvert \lt \infty \qquad \text{CLT needs } \operatorname{Var}(X) \lt \infty}
$$
