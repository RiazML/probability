# 17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [17.2 Relationships Between Modes](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)

> **Big idea:** The average of many independent measurements is very likely to be close to the true mean, and Chebyshev's inequality shows exactly how likely: the chance of a miss of size $\varepsilon$ is at most $\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$.

---

## Why It Matters

Why does a casino make a steady profit when each single bet is a gamble? Why can a poll of 1,000 people tell us about 50 million voters? Why does a Monte Carlo simulation work? The answer to all three is the **Weak Law of Large Numbers** (WLLN): when you average many independent random values, the average settles near the mean. It is the link between the theory of probability and the real world of repeated experiments. In [3.1](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.1-Interpretations-of-Probability.md) we said "probability is the long-run frequency". This topic proves it.

## Intuition

Roll a fair die once. The result is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, and could be far from the mean 3.5. Roll it twice and average: 1 and 6 give 3.5, but 6 and 6 give 6. The average of 100 rolls is almost never below 3.0 or above 4.0. The average of 10,000 rolls is almost always between 3.45 and 3.55.

Why? To get a large average you need many large rolls at the same time, and independent rolls do not cooperate. A few high rolls are cancelled by a few low ones. The average is a **compromise**, and the more rolls you have, the less any single roll counts: each of $n$ rolls has weight $\frac{1}{n}$.

Notice the exact rate. One roll has spread (standard deviation) $\sigma \approx 1.71$. The average of $n$ rolls has spread $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$. To make the average 10 times more precise, you need 100 times more rolls. The WLLN says the spread goes to zero. Chebyshev's inequality ([16.2](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)) turns the spread into a probability.

## Picture

```text
  Spread of the fraction of heads after n fair flips:  sd = 0.5 / sqrt(n)

  n          sd
  10         ################################   0.158
  100        ##########                         0.050
  1,000      ###                                0.016
  10,000     #                                  0.005

  (one # = 0.005.  100 times more flips  ->  10 times smaller wobble)
```

The average wobbles less and less as $n$ grows. The wobble is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ times the wobble of a single value.

## Definition

**Weak Law of Large Numbers.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with mean $\mu = E[X_i]$ and finite variance $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_i)$. Let $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n)$ be the **sample mean**: the average of the first $n$ values. Then for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$:

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty
$$

That is, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$: the sample mean converges **in probability** to the true mean ([17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)).

where:

- $\varepsilon$ is how far off we allow the average to be (the tolerance).
- $\sigma^2$ is the variance of one value.
- $n$ is the number of values averaged.

**In words:** for any tolerance, however small, the chance that the average misses the true mean by more than the tolerance goes to zero as we collect more data.

The bound also tells us how much data is enough. If we want the failure probability to be at most $\delta$, it is enough that

$$
n \ge \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2 \delta}
$$

**Frequency form (Bernoulli's law).** Let $A$ be an event, and in each of $n$ independent repetitions let $X_i = I_{A_i}$ be 1 if $A$ happens and 0 if not. Then $\mu = P(A)$, $\sigma^2 = P(A)(1 - P(A))$, and $\bar{X}_n$ is the **relative frequency** of $A$. So the relative frequency of any event converges in probability to its probability.

## Why It Is True

The proof is two facts about the sample mean and one inequality.

**Fact 1: the mean of the average is $\mu$.** By linearity of expectation ([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)):

$$
E[\bar{X}_n] = \frac{1}{n}\big(E[X_1] + \cdots + E[X_n]\big) = \frac{n\mu}{n} = \mu
$$

**Fact 2: the variance of the average is $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$.** The variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of the variances, and a constant factor $\frac{1}{n}$ comes out squared ([6.6](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)):

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\big(\operatorname{Var}(X_1) + \cdots + \operatorname{Var}(X_n)\big) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}
$$

**Fact 3: Chebyshev.** Apply Chebyshev's inequality ([16.2](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)) to the random variable $\bar{X}_n$, which has mean $\mu$ and variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) &\le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n)}{\varepsilon^2} && \text{(Chebyshev)} \\
&= \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} && \text{(Fact 2)} \\
&\to 0 && \text{(} \sigma^2 \text{ and } \varepsilon \text{ are fixed numbers)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the whole proof. It also shows convergence in mean square: $E[(\bar{X}_n - \mu)^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \to 0$.

**What was really used.** Only Fact 2 needed independence, and only to say that the variances add. So the WLLN also holds if the $X_i$ are only **uncorrelated** ([11.2](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md)) and have a common mean, or if they are independent with different distributions but variances all at most some number $M$. Then $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) \le \frac{M}{n}$.

<details>
<summary>Khinchin's version: only a finite mean is needed (optional)</summary>

The variance was used only for convenience. The WLLN stays true when $E\lvert X_i \rvert \lt \infty$ and the variance is infinite. The proof uses the characteristic function $\varphi(t) = E[e^{itX}]$ ([15.6](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)). A finite mean means $\varphi$ is differentiable at 0 with $\varphi'(0) = i\mu$, so $\varphi(s) = 1 + i\mu s + o(s)$ for small $s$. For the sample mean of independent values:

$$\varphi_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = \left[\varphi\left(\tfrac{t}{n}\right)\right]^n = \left[1 + \frac{i\mu t}{n} + o\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right]^n \to e^{i\mu t}$$

The limit $e^{i\mu t}$ is the characteristic function of the constant $\mu$. By Lévy's continuity theorem, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mu$, and by [17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md) (convergence in distribution to a constant is convergence in probability), $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$. The rate is then slower and not given by a formula, but the law still holds. A finite mean is really needed: the Cauchy distribution has none, and its averages do not settle ([17.9](17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Averaging Die Rolls

**Problem.** You roll a fair die $n = 1000$ times. Use the WLLN to bound the chance that the average roll is off from $3.5$ by more than $0.1$. How many rolls make this chance at most $0.05$?

**Solution.**

1. One roll has $\mu = 3.5$ and $E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = \frac{91}{6}$. So

$$
\sigma^2 = \frac{91}{6} - 3.5^2 = 15.1667 - 12.25 = 2.9167 = \frac{35}{12}
$$

2. The variance of the average of 1000 rolls is $\frac{2.9167}{1000} = 0.002917$.
3. Chebyshev with $\varepsilon = 0.1$:

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_{1000} - 3.5 \rvert \gt 0.1\big) \le \frac{2.9167}{1000 \times 0.1^2} = \frac{2.9167}{10} = 0.2917
$$

4. For the second question use $n \ge \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2 \delta}$ with $\delta = 0.05$:

$$
n \ge \frac{2.9167}{0.01 \times 0.05} = 5833.3
$$

**Answer.** The chance is at most $0.2917$. At least $n = 5834$ rolls give a guarantee of $0.05$.

**Check.** The simulation below finds that the real chance is only about $0.063$ (the exact value from the distribution of the sum is $0.0627$), so the bound is about 4.7 times too large. This is normal: Chebyshev must protect against every distribution with this variance.

### Example 2: Estimating Pi with Random Points

**Problem.** Throw $n$ random points into the unit square $[0, 1] \times [0, 1]$. A point lands in the quarter circle $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$ with probability $p = \frac{\pi}{4}$. Let $\hat{\pi}_n = 4 \times (\text{fraction of points inside})$. How large must $n$ be so that $\hat{\pi}_n$ is within $0.01$ of $\pi$ with probability at least $0.95$?

**Solution.**

1. Let $X_i = 1$ if point $i$ is inside, else 0. Then $\hat{\pi}_n = 4\bar{X}_n$ and $E[\hat{\pi}_n] = 4p = \pi$. By the WLLN, $\hat{\pi}_n \xrightarrow{p} \pi$.
2. The variance of one indicator is $p(1 - p) = 0.7854 \times 0.2146 = 0.1685$. So

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\pi}_n) = 16 \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \frac{2.697}{n}
$$

3. Chebyshev with $\varepsilon = 0.01$ and $\delta = 0.05$:

$$
\frac{2.697}{n \times 0.01^2} \le 0.05 \implies n \ge \frac{2.697}{0.0001 \times 0.05} = 539{,}354
$$

4. If $p$ were unknown, we could use $p(1-p) \le \frac{1}{4}$, which gives $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\pi}_n) \le \frac{4}{n}$ and $n \ge 800{,}000$.

**Answer.** About $539{,}354$ points are enough for the guarantee (800,000 if we do not use the value of $p$).

**Check.** The spread of $\hat{\pi}_n$ is $\sqrt{\frac{2.697}{n}}$: $0.0519$ for $n = 1000$, $0.0052$ for $n = 100{,}000$ and $0.0016$ for $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ (the code in Practice 6 shows these numbers). To get one more digit of $\pi$ you need 100 times more points, which is why this method is a poor way to get digits but a good example of Monte Carlo ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)).

### Example 3: Why the Casino Always Wins

**Problem.** In European roulette there are 37 slots (0 to 36). A player bets 1 dollar on red, which wins with probability $\frac{18}{37}$ and pays 1 dollar, and otherwise loses the 1 dollar. Let $X$ be the player's net gain from one bet. Show that after 1,000,000 bets the player's average gain is negative with probability at least $0.9986$.

**Solution.**

1. $X = +1$ with probability $\frac{18}{37}$ and $X = -1$ with probability $\frac{19}{37}$. The mean is

$$
\mu = \frac{18}{37} - \frac{19}{37} = -\frac{1}{37} \approx -0.02703 \text{ dollars}
$$

2. Since $X^2 = 1$ always, $\sigma^2 = 1 - \mu^2 = 1 - 0.00073 = 0.99927$.
3. The player's average gain is $\bar{X}_n$ with $n = 1{,}000{,}000$. It is non-negative only if it misses its mean by at least $\frac{1}{37} = 0.02703$. By Chebyshev:

$$
P(\bar{X}_n \ge 0) \le P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge 0.02703\big) \le \frac{0.99927}{10^6 \times 0.02703^2} = \frac{0.99927}{730.5} = 0.00137
$$

4. So the player's average is negative with probability at least $1 - 0.00137 = 0.99863$.

**Answer.** At least $0.9986$. The casino expects to earn $\frac{1{,}000{,}000}{37} \approx 27{,}027$ dollars from these bets.

**Check.** Chebyshev is very cautious here. The true probability is far smaller: the total gain has standard deviation about $\sqrt{10^6} \approx 1000$ dollars, but the casino's expected win is 27,027 dollars, which is 27 standard deviations. The casino does not need to win each bet. It only needs a mean in its favor and many bets.

## Simulate It

Part 1 shows that the fraction of heads settles, while the number of heads minus $\frac{n}{2}$ does not. Part 2 averages 1000 die rolls many times and compares with Chebyshev.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Part 1: one long run of fair coin flips. The fraction settles, the raw count does not.
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=1_000_000)
heads = np.cumsum(flips)
print("       n   fraction of heads   heads - n/2")
for n in [10, 100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    print(f"{n:>8}   {heads[n - 1] / n:>17.4f}   {heads[n - 1] - n / 2:>11.1f}")

# Part 2: average of n = 1000 die rolls, many times. Compare with Chebyshev.
n, n_trials = 1000, 20_000
total = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(n_trials, n)).sum(axis=1)   # sum of 1000 rolls
miss = np.abs(total - 3500) > 100          # average off from 3.5 by more than 0.1
sigma2 = 35 / 12                                    # variance of one roll
print(f"\nP(|average - 3.5| > 0.1): simulation {np.mean(miss):.4f}, "
      f"Chebyshev bound {sigma2 / (n * 0.1**2):.4f}")
print(f"Variance of the average: simulation {(total / n).var():.5f}, exact {sigma2 / n:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
       n   fraction of heads   heads - n/2
      10              0.4000          -1.0
     100              0.5200           2.0
   10000              0.4939         -61.0
 1000000              0.4998        -164.0

P(|average - 3.5| > 0.1): simulation 0.0624, Chebyshev bound 0.2917
Variance of the average: simulation 0.00291, exact 0.00292
```

The fraction of heads gets closer to $0.5$ ($0.5200$, then $0.4939$, then $0.4998$), but the gap in the raw count grows from 2 to 164. The gap in the count behaves like $\sqrt{n}$, which is small compared to $n$. Part 2 agrees with Example 1: the true chance of a miss is $0.063$, below the bound $0.2917$.

## Common Mistakes

- **The gambler's fallacy.** "I got 5 tails in a row, so heads is due." The next flip is independent of the past, and the coin has no memory. The law of large numbers does not **correct** old results. It **dilutes** them: 5 extra tails matter less and less as the number of flips grows.
- **Confusing the average with the sum.** The law says $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$, not $S_n - n\mu \to 0$. The gap of the sum grows like $\sqrt{n}$ (the simulation shows 2, then 61, then 164).
- **Applying it to a small sample.** The WLLN is a statement for large $n$. The bound $\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$ is above 1 (no information) until $n \gt \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$.
- **Forgetting independence.** If $X_1 = X_2 = \cdots = X$, then $\bar{X}_n = X$ for every $n$, and nothing settles. The values must be independent (or at least uncorrelated).
- **Using it when no mean exists.** For the Cauchy distribution the averages never settle ([17.9](17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **Machine learning:** the average loss on a test set converges to the true expected loss, so test accuracy is a valid estimate. Training on a minibatch estimates the full gradient.
- **Monte Carlo simulation:** any expectation $E[g(X)]$ can be estimated by averaging $g$ over many samples ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)).
- **Insurance and casinos:** a company that has many independent customers or bets has an almost fixed average payout, so it can set a price with a small safety margin.
- **Polls and A/B tests:** the fraction of a sample who say "yes" estimates the fraction in the whole population.
- **Everyday life:** a batting average, a restaurant's rating, and the long-run frequency meaning of a weather forecast are all averages that settle with enough data.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You flip a fair coin 10,000 times. Use the WLLN bound to bound the chance that the fraction of heads differs from $0.5$ by $0.01$ or more.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A flip has $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{4}$. With $\varepsilon = 0.01$ and $n = 10{,}000$:

$$P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - 0.5 \rvert \ge 0.01\big) \le \frac{0.25}{10000 \times 0.0001} = 0.25$$

(The true value is about $0.047$, so the bound is weak but valid.)

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Measurements have standard deviation $\sigma = 3$. How many independent measurements guarantee that the average is within $0.5$ of the true mean with probability at least $0.9$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use $n \ge \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2 \delta}$ with $\sigma^2 = 9$, $\varepsilon = 0.5$ and $\delta = 0.1$:

$$n \ge \frac{9}{0.25 \times 0.1} = 360$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The values $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are independent with the same mean $\mu$ but different variances, all at most $M$. Show that $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean of $\bar{X}_n$ is $\mu$, and by independence

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) \le \frac{nM}{n^2} = \frac{M}{n}$$

Chebyshev gives $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \le \frac{M}{n\varepsilon^2} \to 0$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that the WLLN can fail without independence. Take $X$ to be $0$ or $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, and $X_i = X$ for all $i$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every $X_i$ has mean $\mu = \frac{1}{2}$, but $\bar{X}_n = X$ for every $n$. So for $\varepsilon = \frac{1}{4}$:

$$P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \tfrac{1}{2} \rvert \gt \tfrac{1}{4}\big) = P\big(\lvert X - \tfrac{1}{2} \rvert \gt \tfrac{1}{4}\big) = 1$$

for all $n$, which does not go to 0. The variance of the average is $\frac{1}{4}$, not $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, because the variables are perfectly correlated.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ have the same mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \sigma^2 \rho^{\lvert i - j \rvert}$ with $0 \lt \rho \lt 1$ (neighbors are correlated). Show that $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The variance of a sum includes all covariances. There are $n - k$ pairs $(i, j)$ with $j - i = k$, and each pair appears twice (as $(i, j)$ and $(j, i)$):

$$\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \sigma^2\left[n + 2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (n - k)\rho^k\right] \le \sigma^2 n\left[1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho^k\right] = \sigma^2 n \cdot \frac{1 + \rho}{1 - \rho}$$

So $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(S_n)}{n^2} \le \frac{\sigma^2(1 + \rho)}{(1 - \rho)n} \to 0$, and Chebyshev finishes the proof. The correlation costs only a constant factor $\frac{1 + \rho}{1 - \rho}$ in the variance. (If all pairs had correlation $\rho$, the variance of the average would stay near $\rho\sigma^2$ and the law would fail.)

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the numbers in Example 2: for $n = 1000, 10^4, 10^5, 10^6$ points, simulate many estimates of $\pi$ (use `rng.binomial` for the number of points inside) and compare their standard deviation with $\sqrt{2.697 / n}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p = np.pi / 4                                   # chance a random point lies in the quarter circle
n_runs = 100_000
print("      n   sd of estimate (sim)   sqrt(2.697/n)   P(|est - pi| > 0.01)")
for n in [1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    hits = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_runs)       # points inside the quarter circle, per run
    est = 4 * hits / n                           # estimate of pi
    print(f"{n:>7}   {est.std():>20.5f}   {np.sqrt(2.697 / n):>13.5f}   {np.mean(np.abs(est - np.pi) > 0.01):>10.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
      n   sd of estimate (sim)   sqrt(2.697/n)   P(|est - pi| > 0.01)
   1000                0.05186         0.05193       0.8464
  10000                0.01642         0.01642       0.5429
 100000                0.00519         0.00519       0.0543
1000000                0.00164         0.00164       0.0000
```

At $n = 100{,}000$ the miss probability is still $0.054$, and at $n = 539{,}354$ Chebyshev promises at most $0.05$. The true miss rate is far below $0.05$ there (the standard deviation is $0.0022$, so a miss of $0.01$ is 4.5 standard deviations).

</details>

## Summary

- The sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ of i.i.d. values with a finite variance converges in probability to the mean $\mu$.
- The proof: $E[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, then Chebyshev.
- The standard deviation of the average is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$: 100 times more data gives 10 times less spread.
- Independence (or being uncorrelated) is needed, and so is a mean. The sum does not settle, only the average does.
- Applied to indicators, it says that relative frequencies converge to probabilities.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}}
$$
