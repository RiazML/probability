# 17.2 Relationships Between Modes

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.2 Relationships Between Modes

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [17.1 Modes of Convergence](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)

> **Big idea:** Almost sure and mean square convergence each imply convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution; no arrow can be reversed in general, except that convergence in distribution to a constant is convergence in probability.

---

## Why It Matters

[17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md) gave four meanings of the phrase $X_n$ gets close to $X$. Now we ask which meaning is stronger. This tells us what we can conclude from a theorem. For example, the Strong Law of Large Numbers ([17.4](17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) implies the Weak Law ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) because almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability. It also tells us what we cannot conclude: from the Central Limit Theorem you cannot say that two averages are close to each other, only that their distributions are.

## Intuition

Think of $X_n$ as a runner's position at time $n$, and $X$ as the finish line. Each outcome $\omega$ is one race.

- **Almost sure:** in nearly every race, the runner reaches the line and stays there. Then, at any fixed late time $n$, the runner is surely near the line in most races. So almost sure implies in probability.
- **Mean square:** the average squared distance to the line is small. Then a big distance can happen only rarely, because big distances cost a lot in the average. So mean square implies in probability.
- **In probability:** at a late time $n$, most races have the runner near the line. But it may be a different set of races at each time. A runner can be near the line at time $n$, far away at time $n+1$, and so on forever, with the "far" races becoming rarer. Then no single race settles down, so in probability does **not** imply almost sure.
- **In distribution:** the crowd of runners looks right as a group, but we never matched runner to race. It is the weakest.

## Picture

```text
                +-----------------+
                |  almost surely  |
                +--------+--------+
                         |
                         v
 +-----------+   +----------------+   +-----------------+
 | in mean   |-->| in probability |-->| in distribution |
 | square    |   +----------------+   +-----------------+
 +-----------+           ^                     |
                         |                     |
                         +- - - - - - - - - - -+
                           only if the limit is a constant
```

The solid arrows are always true. Every other arrow is false in general. The dashed arrow back from "in distribution" works only when the limit is a constant.

| Missing arrow | Counterexample | Where |
|---|---|---|
| in probability does not give almost sure | typewriter sequence | Example 2 |
| in probability does not give mean square | spike $X_n = n \cdot I(U \lt 1/n)$ | Example 1 |
| almost sure does not give mean square | the same spike | Example 1 |
| mean square does not give almost sure | typewriter sequence | Example 2 |
| in distribution does not give in probability | $X_n = -X$ | [17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md), Example 2 |

## Definition

The facts below are the theorem of this topic. Let $X_n$ and $X$ be random variables on the same sample space.

**Theorem (the implications).**

1. If $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X$, then $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$.
2. If $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} X$, then $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$.
3. If $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$, then $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$.
4. If $X_n \xrightarrow{d} c$ where $c$ is a constant, then $X_n \xrightarrow{p} c$.

**In words:** the chain "almost sure or mean square, then probability, then distribution" always holds. The chain runs back only for a constant limit.

Two more facts are used all the time:

- **Mean square keeps the mean.** If $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} X$ then $E[X_n] \to E[X]$. The other modes do not promise this (Example 1).
- **Continuous functions keep convergence.** If $g$ is continuous and $X_n \to X$ in probability (or almost surely, or in distribution), then $g(X_n) \to g(X)$ in the same mode. This is the **continuous mapping theorem**. We use it without proof, and it is behind [17.7](17.7-Delta-Method.md) and [17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md).

## Why It Is True

**Proof of 2 (mean square implies probability).** The event $\lbrace \lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon \rbrace$ is the event $\lbrace (X_n - X)^2 \gt \varepsilon^2 \rbrace$. Markov's inequality ([16.1](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)) for the non-negative variable $(X_n - X)^2$ gives:

$$
P\big(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{E\big[(X_n - X)^2\big]}{\varepsilon^2} \to 0
$$

This is Chebyshev's inequality ([16.2](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)) with $X_n - X$ in place of $X - \mu$.

**Proof of 1 (almost sure implies probability).** Fix $\varepsilon \gt 0$. For each $N$ let $B_N$ be the event that from time $N$ on, $X_m$ stays within $\varepsilon$ of $X$:

$$
B_N = \lbrace \omega : \lvert X_m(\omega) - X(\omega) \rvert \le \varepsilon \text{ for all } m \ge N \rbrace
$$

The events grow: $B_1 \subseteq B_2 \subseteq \cdots$. If $X_n(\omega) \to X(\omega)$, then $\omega$ is in some $B_N$. Convergence happens with probability 1, so the union of all $B_N$ has probability 1. By continuity of probability from below ([3.9](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.9-Continuity-of-Probability.md)), $P(B_N) \to 1$. Since $B_N \subseteq \lbrace \lvert X_N - X \rvert \le \varepsilon \rbrace$:

$$
P\big(\lvert X_N - X \rvert \le \varepsilon\big) \ge P(B_N) \to 1
$$

So $P(\lvert X_N - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \to 0$.

**Proof of 3 (probability implies distribution).** Let $x$ be a point where $F_X$ is continuous, and let $\varepsilon \gt 0$. Write $F_n$ for the CDF of $X_n$ and $a_n = P(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon)$. Two inclusions of events hold:

- If $X_n \le x$ and $\lvert X_n - X \rvert \le \varepsilon$, then $X \le x + \varepsilon$. So $\lbrace X_n \le x \rbrace \subseteq \lbrace X \le x + \varepsilon \rbrace \cup \lbrace \lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon \rbrace$.
- If $X \le x - \varepsilon$ and $\lvert X_n - X \rvert \le \varepsilon$, then $X_n \le x$. So $\lbrace X \le x - \varepsilon \rbrace \subseteq \lbrace X_n \le x \rbrace \cup \lbrace \lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon \rbrace$.

Taking probabilities and using the union bound:

$$
F_X(x - \varepsilon) - a_n \le F_n(x) \le F_X(x + \varepsilon) + a_n
$$

Let $n \to \infty$. Then $a_n \to 0$, so $F_X(x - \varepsilon) \le \liminf F_n(x) \le \limsup F_n(x) \le F_X(x + \varepsilon)$. Now let $\varepsilon \to 0$. Because $F_X$ is continuous at $x$, both ends tend to $F_X(x)$. So $F_n(x) \to F_X(x)$.

**Proof of 4 (a constant limit).** The CDF of the constant $c$ is $F(x) = 0$ for $x \lt c$ and $1$ for $x \ge c$. The points $c - \varepsilon$ and $c + \varepsilon$ are continuity points of $F$. So $F_n(c - \varepsilon) \to 0$ and $F_n(c + \varepsilon) \to 1$. Then:

$$
P\big(\lvert X_n - c \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le F_n(c - \varepsilon) + \big(1 - F_n(c + \varepsilon)\big) \to 0 + (1 - 1) = 0
$$

**Proof of the mean fact.** By Jensen's inequality ([16.3](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)), $\big(E\lvert Y \rvert\big)^2 \le E[Y^2]$. With $Y = X_n - X$:

$$
\big\lvert E[X_n] - E[X] \big\rvert \le E\lvert X_n - X \rvert \le \sqrt{E\big[(X_n - X)^2\big]} \to 0
$$

> **Note:** Convergence in probability does give almost sure convergence **along a subsequence**: some $n_1 \lt n_2 \lt \cdots$ have $X_{n_k} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X$. The proof uses the Borel-Cantelli lemma ([20.6](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)). It is a handy trick, but it does not mean the full sequence converges almost surely.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Spike (Almost Sure, but Not Mean Square)

**Problem.** Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $X_n = n$ if $U \lt \frac{1}{n}$, and $X_n = 0$ otherwise. Which modes hold for $X_n \to 0$? What happens to $E[X_n]$?

**Solution.**

1. **In probability.** For $0 \lt \varepsilon \lt n$: $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = P(U \lt \frac{1}{n}) = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$. For $n = 100$ this is $0.01$.
2. **Almost surely.** Fix an outcome with $U = u \gt 0$ (this has probability 1). Then $X_n(u) \ne 0$ only if $n \lt \frac{1}{u}$. For all larger $n$ we have $X_n(u) = 0$. So the number sequence $X_n(u)$ is eventually 0, and it converges to 0. (Spikes can be huge, but there are only finitely many of them for each $u$.)
3. **In mean square.** $X_n^2 = n^2$ with probability $\frac{1}{n}$, so $E[X_n^2] = n^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n} = n \to \infty$. For $n = 100$ this is $100$. So mean square convergence **fails**.
4. **The mean.** $E[X_n] = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$ for every $n$. It does not go to $E[0] = 0$.
5. **In distribution.** By the Theorem, $X_n \xrightarrow{d} 0$ (also directly: $P(X_n \le x) \to 1$ for every $x \gt 0$).

**Answer.** $X_n \to 0$ almost surely, in probability and in distribution, but not in mean square. Also $E[X_n] = 1 \not\to 0$.

**Check.** The simulation below gives $E[X_n^2] \approx 100.6$ for $n = 100$, and $E[X_n] \approx 1.006$. The rare spike of size $n$ is too tall: it carries the whole mean and variance, but it is too rare to matter for "most runs".

### Example 2: The Typewriter (Probability and Mean Square, but Not Almost Sure)

**Problem.** Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$. Cut $[0, 1]$ into pieces in "blocks". Block $k$ has $k$ intervals of length $\frac{1}{k}$. The sequence of all intervals is

$$
[0, 1];\ [0, \tfrac{1}{2}], [\tfrac{1}{2}, 1];\ [0, \tfrac{1}{3}], [\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}], [\tfrac{2}{3}, 1];\ [0, \tfrac{1}{4}], \dots
$$

Let $I_n$ be the $n$-th interval and $X_n = 1$ if $U \in I_n$, else $0$. Which modes hold for $X_n \to 0$?

**Solution.**

1. **Mean square.** If $I_n$ belongs to block $k$, then $E[X_n^2] = P(U \in I_n) = \frac{1}{k}$. As $n \to \infty$, the block number $k \to \infty$. So $E[X_n^2] \to 0$.
2. **In probability.** By the Theorem (mean square implies probability), $X_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$. Directly: $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = \frac{1}{k} \to 0$ for $\varepsilon \lt 1$.
3. **Not almost surely.** Fix any outcome $u$. Each block covers all of $[0, 1]$, so every block has at least one interval that contains $u$. So $X_n(u) = 1$ for at least one $n$ in each block, for infinitely many blocks. Then $X_n(u) = 1$ infinitely often, and also $X_n(u) = 0$ infinitely often. The number sequence $X_n(u)$ never converges, for **every** $u$. The set where it converges is empty, with probability 0, not 1.

**Answer.** $X_n \to 0$ in mean square, in probability and in distribution, but **not** almost surely.

**Check.** For $u = 0.4$ the sequence starts $1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, \dots$. The 1s become rarer (one per block, but the blocks get longer), yet they never stop. At any fixed late time $n$ the chance of a 1 is tiny ($\frac{1}{k}$). This is exactly "close at each time, but never settling". The subsequence of first intervals $[0, \frac{1}{k}]$ does converge almost surely, as the Note above promises.

### Example 3: Averaging a Noisy Sensor

**Problem.** A sensor reads a true value $\theta$ plus independent noise with mean 0 and standard deviation 2 (so variance 4). You average $n$ readings, $\bar{X}_n$. (a) What is $E[(\bar{X}_n - \theta)^2]$? (b) Use it to bound $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \theta \rvert \gt 0.5)$ for $n = 64$ and compare with the exact value when the noise is normal. (c) How many readings guarantee a bound of $0.05$?

**Solution.**

1. The mean of $\bar{X}_n$ is $\theta$, so the mean square error is its variance: $E[(\bar{X}_n - \theta)^2] = \frac{4}{n}$. It goes to 0, so $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} \theta$.
2. By the proof of "mean square implies probability", for $n = 64$:

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_{64} - \theta \rvert \gt 0.5\big) \le \frac{4/64}{0.5^2} = \frac{0.0625}{0.25} = 0.25
$$

3. If the noise is normal, $\bar{X}_{64} - \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.0625)$ has standard deviation $0.25$. The distance $0.5$ is 2 standard deviations, so the exact probability is $2(1 - \Phi(2)) \approx 0.0455$.
4. For part (c) solve $\frac{4/n}{0.25} = \frac{16}{n} \le 0.05$, which gives $n \ge 320$.

**Answer.** (a) $\frac{4}{n}$. (b) The bound is $0.25$, and the exact value is about $0.0455$. (c) $n = 320$ readings are enough for the bound.

**Check.** The bound holds, but it is 5 times too large: it must work for every noise distribution. The simulation in Practice 6 agrees with $0.0455$.

## Simulate It

We simulate the two counterexamples. For the spike, the first columns show $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt 0.5) \to 0$ but $E[X_n^2]$ growing. For the typewriter, $P(X_n = 1)$ goes to 0, yet almost every outcome still has a 1 after time 1000.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
u = rng.random(1_000_000)                     # one outcome U per trial

# Spike: X_n = n if U < 1/n, else 0. Exact: P = 1/n, E[X_n^2] = n, E[X_n] = 1.
print("Spike      n  P(|X_n|>0.5)  E[X_n^2]  E[X_n]  P(some X_m != 0, m >= n)")
for n in [10, 100, 1000]:
    x = np.where(u < 1 / n, n, 0)
    print(f"{n:>11}  {np.mean(x > 0.5):>12.4f}  {np.mean(x**2):>8.1f}  {np.mean(x):>6.3f}"
          f"  {np.mean(u < 1 / n):>10.4f}")

# Typewriter: block k has k intervals of length 1/k; X_n = 1 if U is in the n-th interval.
k_idx = np.concatenate([np.full(k, k) for k in range(1, 101)])        # block of each n
j_idx = np.concatenate([np.arange(1, k + 1) for k in range(1, 101)])  # interval number
lo, hi = (j_idx - 1) / k_idx, j_idx / k_idx
v = u[:20_000, None]
x = (v >= lo) & (v <= hi)                     # 20,000 outcomes, 5050 steps each
print("\nTypewriter   n  P(X_n = 1)  1/k")
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 5000]:
    print(f"{n:>12}  {x[:, n - 1].mean():>10.4f}  {1 / k_idx[n - 1]:.4f}")
print("Share of outcomes with some X_n = 1 for n >= 1000:", x[:, 999:].any(axis=1).mean())
```

Output:

```text
Spike      n  P(|X_n|>0.5)  E[X_n^2]  E[X_n]  P(some X_m != 0, m >= n)
         10        0.0997      10.0   0.997      0.0997
        100        0.0101     100.6   1.006      0.0101
       1000        0.0010    1002.0   1.002      0.0010

Typewriter   n  P(X_n = 1)  1/k
          10      0.2483  0.2500
         100      0.0727  0.0714
        1000      0.0222  0.0222
        5000      0.0097  0.0100
Share of outcomes with some X_n = 1 for n >= 1000: 1.0
```

The spike has $E[X_n^2] \approx n$, so it fails mean square convergence. The last column is $P(U \lt \frac{1}{n})$: the chance that any spike remains after time $n$ goes to 0, which is almost sure convergence. For the typewriter, the same last test gives $1.0$ at every time: a 1 always comes later, so there is no almost sure convergence.

## Common Mistakes

- **Believing that almost sure implies mean square.** The spike has all runs end at 0, yet $E[X_n^2] \to \infty$. Convergence of every run says nothing about rare huge values.
- **Believing that in probability implies almost sure.** The typewriter converges in probability and in mean square, but no run settles down.
- **Assuming $E[X_n] \to E[X]$.** This is true for mean square convergence, but false for the other modes. In the spike, $X_n \to 0$ almost surely but $E[X_n] = 1$.
- **Going backward along the chain.** Convergence in distribution is the weakest mode. It does not give convergence in probability, except when the limit is a constant.
- **Forgetting that the statements need the variables on the same sample space.** Implications 1 to 3 compare $X_n(\omega)$ with $X(\omega)$, so $X_n$ and $X$ must be defined for the same outcomes.

## Where It Is Used

- **Law of Large Numbers:** the weak law is convergence in probability, and it is proved through mean square convergence (the variance of $\bar{X}_n$ is $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$). The strong law then gives the stronger mode ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md), [17.4](17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).
- **Slutsky's theorem:** it needs "in distribution to a constant implies in probability" ([17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md)).
- **Machine learning:** a model that converges in mean square also converges in probability. Statements about the mean (such as an unbiased gradient) are safe only under mean square convergence.
- **Reinforcement learning and optimization:** the proofs of almost sure convergence of Q-learning and SGD are used because they promise that every single training run settles, not only most runs.
- **Everyday life:** "most people are close to the average at any time" (probability) is weaker than "each person's long-run average settles" (almost sure).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $X_n = \frac{Z}{n}$. Show that $X_n \to 0$ in mean square. What follows for the other modes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[X_n^2] = \frac{E[Z^2]}{n^2} = \frac{1}{n^2} \to 0$, so $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} 0$. By the Theorem, $X_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$ and $X_n \xrightarrow{d} 0$. It also converges almost surely: for each outcome, $\frac{Z(\omega)}{n} \to 0$ as a number sequence.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $X_n = n$ if $U \lt \frac{1}{n^2}$, else $0$. Is $X_n \to 0$ in probability? In mean square? What is $E[X_n]$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In probability: $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = \frac{1}{n^2} \to 0$. Mean square: $E[X_n^2] = n^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n^2} = 1$, which does not go to 0, so mean square fails. Here $E[X_n] = n \cdot \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$, so the mean does go to 0 even though mean square fails.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In the typewriter sequence, find a subsequence $X_{n_k}$ that converges to 0 almost surely.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $n_k$ to be the position of the first interval of block $k$. It is the interval $[0, \frac{1}{k}]$, and $n_k = \frac{k(k-1)}{2} + 1$. Then $X_{n_k} = 1$ exactly when $U \le \frac{1}{k}$. For any outcome with $U \gt 0$, this is false once $k \gt \frac{1}{U}$, so $X_{n_k} = 0$ eventually. This happens with probability 1.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A sensor has noise with standard deviation 2. How many readings $n$ guarantee, by the mean-square bound, that $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \theta \rvert \gt 0.5) \le 0.05$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean square error is $\frac{4}{n}$, so the bound is $\frac{4/n}{0.25} = \frac{16}{n}$. We need $\frac{16}{n} \le 0.05$, so $n \ge 320$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $\lvert X_n \rvert \le C$ for all $n$ (a bound that does not depend on $n$), and let $X_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$. Prove that $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fix $\varepsilon \gt 0$ and split the expectation according to whether $\lvert X_n \rvert \le \varepsilon$ or not. On the first part $X_n^2 \le \varepsilon^2$. On the second part $X_n^2 \le C^2$:

$$E[X_n^2] \le \varepsilon^2 \cdot P(\lvert X_n \rvert \le \varepsilon) + C^2 \cdot P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \le \varepsilon^2 + C^2 \thinspace P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon)$$

The last probability goes to 0, so $\limsup E[X_n^2] \le \varepsilon^2$. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $E[X_n^2] \to 0$. The spike fails the assumption because $X_n = n$ is not bounded.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 3: simulate the mean of 64 readings with normal noise (standard deviation 2) and estimate the mean square error and $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \theta \rvert \gt 0.5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_readings, n_trials = 64, 200_000
# Each reading = true value + noise with standard deviation 2. Take the true value as 0.
readings = rng.normal(0, 2, size=(n_trials, n_readings))
error = readings.mean(axis=1)                      # error of the average of 64 readings
print(f"Mean square error: simulation {np.mean(error**2):.4f}, exact {4 / 64:.4f}")
print(f"P(|error| > 0.5):  simulation {np.mean(np.abs(error) > 0.5):.4f}, exact 0.0455, bound {0.0625 / 0.25:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Mean square error: simulation 0.0623, exact 0.0625
P(|error| > 0.5):  simulation 0.0453, exact 0.0455, bound 0.2500
```

</details>

## Summary

- Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, and mean square convergence implies convergence in probability.
- Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution. Convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability.
- No other arrow holds. The spike ($n$ with probability $\frac{1}{n}$) is almost sure but not mean square. The typewriter is mean square but not almost sure. The mirror image $-X$ is in distribution but not in probability.
- Mean square convergence keeps the mean, and continuous functions keep every mode.

The one picture to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{a.s.} \implies \text{in probability} \implies \text{in distribution}, \qquad \text{m.s.} \implies \text{in probability}}
$$
