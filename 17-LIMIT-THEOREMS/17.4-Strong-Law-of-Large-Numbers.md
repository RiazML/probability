# 17.4 Strong Law of Large Numbers

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.4 Strong Law of Large Numbers

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)

> **Big idea:** With probability 1, the running average of i.i.d. values with a finite mean settles down to the mean and stays there; so the long-run frequency of an event really is its probability, for almost every infinite sequence of trials.

---

## Why It Matters

The Weak Law ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) says: at any fixed late time, the average is probably close to $\mu$. It leaves one worry. Could a single long experiment still jump away from $\mu$ again and again, at times that are rarer and rarer? The **Strong Law of Large Numbers** (SLLN) says no: with probability 1, from some time on, the average never leaves a small band around $\mu$. This is the version that justifies the claim that the long-run frequency of heads in one infinite sequence of flips is $\frac{1}{2}$, and it is the version used to prove that Monte Carlo methods and learning algorithms converge.

## Intuition

Alice flips a fair coin forever. After every flip she writes down the fraction of heads so far. This gives one infinite list of numbers: her **sample path**.

The Weak Law is a statement about a fixed time. Take time 10,000. Among many Alices, only a few have a fraction far from $0.5$ at that time. But it may be a different few at time 10,001. So the Weak Law alone does not exclude the following: every Alice leaves the band $0.5 \pm 0.01$ infinitely often, at rarer and rarer times.

The Strong Law is a statement about the whole list. It says: for almost every Alice, there is a time $N$ (different for each Alice) after which her fraction stays within $0.5 \pm 0.01$ for ever. The word "almost" is needed. Some infinite sequences, such as "heads forever", never settle at $0.5$. But the set of such sequences has total probability 0.

## Definition

**Strong Law of Large Numbers (Kolmogorov).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. random variables with a finite mean $\mu = E[X_1]$, which means $E\lvert X_1 \rvert \lt \infty$. Then

$$
P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1
$$

that is, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$ ([17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)).

An equivalent way to say it: for every tolerance $\varepsilon \gt 0$,

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon \text{ for infinitely many } n\big) = 0
$$

**In words:** with probability 1, the sample average converges to the true mean. For each tolerance there is a time (random, and different for each run) after which the average stays within the tolerance for ever.

The finite mean is not just convenient. It is needed: if $E\lvert X_1 \rvert = \infty$ then, with probability 1, $\bar{X}_n$ does **not** converge to any finite number (Practice 5). No variance is needed.

| | Weak law | Strong law |
|---|---|---|
| Mode ([17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)) | in probability | almost surely |
| Says | at each late time $n$, a miss is unlikely | with probability 1, misses stop for good |
| Needs | finite variance (Chebyshev), or just a finite mean (Khinchin) | finite mean |
| Proof | Chebyshev, one line | harder; Borel-Cantelli or truncation |

Because almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)), the strong law **contains** the weak law.

## Why It Is True

**Why Chebyshev is not enough.** The Weak Law gives $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$. We would like to say that, with probability 1, only finitely many of the events $A_n = \lbrace \lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon \rbrace$ happen. A simple rule helps: if the probabilities $P(A_n)$ have a **finite sum**, then with probability 1 only finitely many $A_n$ happen. (Proof: the chance that some $A_n$ with $n \ge N$ happens is at most $\sum_{n \ge N} P(A_n)$ by the union bound, and this tail of a convergent sum goes to 0 as $N \to \infty$.) This rule is the first Borel-Cantelli lemma ([20.6](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)).

But $\sum_n \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$ is the harmonic series, which **diverges**. So the Chebyshev bound is too weak for the rule. We need a bound that shrinks faster than $\frac{1}{n}$.

**A faster bound from the fourth moment.** If the values also have a finite fourth moment, Markov's inequality on $\bar{X}_n^4$ gives a bound of order $\frac{1}{n^2}$. And $\sum_n \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$ is finite. So the rule applies, and we get the Strong Law. The full argument is below.

The proof for a finite mean only is harder: one cuts each $X_i$ at the level $i$ (truncation) and shows that the cut part and the rest are both controlled. We do not give it here. The idea is the same: sum probabilities that are small enough.

<details>
<summary>Full proof when $E[X^4] \lt \infty$ (optional)</summary>

Replace $X_i$ by $X_i - \mu$. Then we can assume $\mu = 0$, and the fourth moment is still finite. Let $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$.

**Step 1: the fourth moment of the sum.** Expand $S_n^4 = \sum_{i,j,k,l} X_i X_j X_k X_l$. If some index appears exactly once in a term, say $i$, then by independence $E[X_i X_j X_k X_l] = E[X_i] \cdot E[X_j X_k X_l] = 0$. The terms that survive have all four indices equal ($n$ terms) or two different pairs of equal indices ($3n(n-1)$ terms: choose the pairing in 3 ways, then 2 different values $i \ne j$ in $n(n-1)$ ways). So

$$E[S_n^4] = n\thinspace E[X^4] + 3n(n-1)\big(E[X^2]\big)^2 \le n\thinspace E[X^4] + 3n(n-1)\thinspace E[X^4] \le 3n^2 \thinspace E[X^4]$$

where we used $\big(E[X^2]\big)^2 \le E[X^4]$ (the variance of $X^2$ is not negative).

**Step 2: Markov.** For $\varepsilon \gt 0$, Markov's inequality for the non-negative variable $S_n^4$ gives:

$$P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) = P\big(S_n^4 \gt n^4 \varepsilon^4\big) \le \frac{E[S_n^4]}{n^4 \varepsilon^4} \le \frac{3\thinspace E[X^4]}{\varepsilon^4} \cdot \frac{1}{n^2}$$

**Step 3: sum and conclude.** The right side has a finite sum over $n$ (because $\sum \frac{1}{n^2} \lt \infty$). By the Borel-Cantelli rule above, with probability 1 only finitely many $n$ have $\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon$. Do this for $\varepsilon = 1, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \dots$. A countable union of events of probability 0 has probability 0 ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)). So with probability 1, for every $m$, we have $\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \le \frac{1}{m}$ for all large $n$. That is exactly $\bar{X}_n \to 0$, which proves the theorem under the fourth-moment assumption.

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Sequences That Fail Have Probability Zero

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin forever. (a) What is the probability that the first $n$ flips are all heads, for $n = 10, 30, 100$? (b) What does the Strong Law say about the sequence "heads forever"? (c) What is the probability that the fraction of heads converges to $0.7$?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The flips are independent, so $P(\text{first } n \text{ all heads}) = 2^{-n}$. For $n = 10$ this is $\frac{1}{1024} \approx 0.00098$. For $n = 30$ it is $2^{-30} \approx 9.3 \times 10^{-10}$. For $n = 100$ it is $2^{-100} \approx 7.9 \times 10^{-31}$.
2. (b) "Heads forever" is a possible outcome, because it is one of the infinite sequences. Its probability is below $2^{-n}$ for every $n$, so it is 0. Its running fraction is always 1, which does not converge to $\frac{1}{2}$. The Strong Law allows this: the bad sequences have probability 0.
3. (c) Let $X_i$ be 1 for heads and 0 for tails, so $\mu = \frac{1}{2}$. By the Strong Law, $\bar{X}_n \to \frac{1}{2}$ with probability 1. A sequence whose fraction converges to $0.7$ has a different limit, so it belongs to the exceptional set. Hence

$$
P\left(\bar{X}_n \to 0.7\right) = 0
$$

**Answer.** (a) $0.00098$, $9.3 \times 10^{-10}$, $7.9 \times 10^{-31}$. (b) It has probability 0, so it is allowed to exist but is ignored. (c) The probability is 0.

**Check.** Probability 0 does not mean impossible. The set of sequences with a limiting fraction of $0.7$ is not empty (for example, repeat "HHTHHTHHTH..." in a pattern with 7 heads in every 10 flips). But it is so thin that a random sequence never lands in it.

### Example 2: How Long Until the Average Stays Put?

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin. Use the fourth-moment bound to find a time $N$ such that, with probability at least $0.97$, the fraction of heads **never again** leaves $0.5 \pm 0.05$ after time $N$.

**Solution.**

1. Let $X_i = I_{\text{heads}} - \frac{1}{2}$ be the centered flip, equal to $\pm\frac{1}{2}$. Then $E[X^2] = \frac{1}{4}$ and $E[X^4] = \frac{1}{16}$.
2. From Step 1 of the proof, $E[S_n^4] = \frac{n}{16} + 3n(n-1)\cdot\frac{1}{16} = \frac{3n^2 - 2n}{16} \le \frac{3n^2}{16}$.
3. Markov for each time $n$, with $\varepsilon = 0.05$ (so $\varepsilon^4 = 6.25 \times 10^{-6}$):

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n \rvert \gt 0.05\big) \le \frac{3n^2/16}{n^4 \cdot 6.25 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{30000}{n^2}
$$

4. By the union bound, the chance of leaving the band at **some** time $n \ge N$ is at most $\sum_{n \ge N} \frac{30000}{n^2}$. Since $\sum_{n \ge N} \frac{1}{n^2} \le \frac{1}{N - 1}$, this is at most $\frac{30000}{N - 1}$.
5. Make it at most $0.03$: $N - 1 \ge \frac{30000}{0.03} = 10^6$. So $N = 1{,}000{,}001$ works.

**Answer.** After about one million flips, with probability at least $0.97$ the fraction of heads never again leaves $0.5 \pm 0.05$.

**Check.** The bound is very safe. The simulation below uses the band $\pm 0.02$ and finds that already by $n = 10{,}000$ only 0.03% of the runs ever leave the band afterwards. A guarantee that works for every distribution with a finite fourth moment is much weaker than the truth for a fair coin.

### Example 3: Monte Carlo Integration

**Problem.** We want $\mu = \int_0^1 e^{-x^2} dx = 0.746824$. Let $U_i$ be i.i.d. uniform on $[0, 1]$, and average $g(U_i) = e^{-U_i^2}$. By the Strong Law, $\frac{1}{n}\sum g(U_i) \to E[g(U)] = \mu$ almost surely. How close is one run?

**Solution.**

1. The mean of $g(U)$ is $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\thinspace dx = \mu$ (this is the definition of the expected value of a function of a uniform variable, [6.3](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.3-LOTUS.md)). The values $g(U_i)$ are in $[e^{-1}, 1]$, so they are bounded and the mean exists.
2. The variance of one value is $\int_0^1 e^{-2x^2}dx - \mu^2 = 0.59814 - 0.55775 = 0.04040$, so the standard deviation is $0.201$.
3. The typical error of the average after $n$ terms is $\frac{0.201}{\sqrt{n}}$: $0.0636$ for $n = 10$, $0.0064$ for $n = 1000$, and $0.0002$ for $n = 10^6$.
4. One run of the code in Practice 6 gives the running averages $0.6744$ ($n = 10$), $0.7480$ ($n = 1000$), $0.7468$ ($n = 10^6$).

**Answer.** The run is within about $0.0001$ of $0.746824$ after a million samples.

**Check.** The errors of the run ($-0.072$, $+0.0012$, $-0.0000004$) match the typical size $\frac{0.201}{\sqrt{n}}$. The Strong Law promises that this happens for almost every run, not only on average over many runs.

## Simulate It

We simulate 8,000 paths of 50,000 fair coin flips. For each checkpoint $N$ we compare two probabilities: being more than $0.02$ off at the single time $N$ (weak law), and being more than $0.02$ off at some time $n \ge N$ (the strong law says this also goes to 0).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_max, n_batches = 50_000, 16
n = np.arange(1, n_max + 1)
checkpoints = [100, 1000, 3000, 10_000]
single = np.zeros(len(checkpoints))     # off by more than 0.02 at the one time N
ever = np.zeros(len(checkpoints))       # off by more than 0.02 at SOME time n >= N
for _ in range(n_batches):              # 500 coin-flip paths per batch
    flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(500, n_max), dtype=np.int8)
    heads = np.cumsum(flips, axis=1, dtype=np.int32)
    off = 25 * np.abs(2 * heads - n) > n     # exactly: |heads/n - 1/2| > 0.02
    for i, N in enumerate(checkpoints):
        single[i] += off[:, N - 1].sum()
        ever[i] += off[:, N - 1:].any(axis=1).sum()
n_paths = 500 * n_batches

print("    N   at time N: sim  exact   at some time n >= N: sim")
for i, N in enumerate(checkpoints):
    low, high = int(0.48 * N + 0.5) - 1, int(0.52 * N + 0.5)   # off if heads <= low or heads > high
    exact = binom.cdf(low, N, 0.5) + binom.sf(high, N, 0.5)
    print(f"{N:>6}   {single[i] / n_paths:>16.4f} {exact:.4f}   {ever[i] / n_paths:>16.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
    N   at time N: sim  exact   at some time n >= N: sim
   100             0.6160 0.6173             0.9986
  1000             0.1989 0.1948             0.4026
  3000             0.0250 0.0271             0.0531
 10000             0.0001 0.0001             0.0003
```

Both columns go to 0, as the Weak and the Strong Law say. The last column is always larger than the exact single-time value: being off at some time from $N$ on is more likely than being off at one given time. At $N = 1000$ it is twice as likely ($0.40$ against $0.19$).

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up the two laws.** The Weak Law says each late time is probably fine. The Strong Law says, with probability 1, a run is fine from some time on. Name the mode ([17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)).
- **Thinking that every sequence converges.** Only almost every one does. "Heads forever" is a legal outcome of probability 0.
- **Peeking.** If you check a running average many times and stop as soon as it looks good, you are using the "at some time" event, which is more likely than the "at one time" event. In the table, at $N = 1000$ the chance of being off at some later time is $0.40$, but at one fixed time $0.19$. This is a classic error in A/B tests.
- **Expecting a rate.** The Strong Law says convergence happens, but not how fast. For the speed you need the CLT ([17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)), which says the error is about $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$.
- **Forgetting the mean must exist.** For a Cauchy variable, or any variable with $E\lvert X \rvert = \infty$, the averages do not converge ([17.9](17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **Monte Carlo and MCMC:** the Strong Law is the reason why a single long simulation run converges to the right answer ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)).
- **Reinforcement learning and stochastic optimization:** proofs that Q-learning and SGD converge for every training run use almost sure convergence, which has the same flavor as the Strong Law.
- **Information theory:** the compression limit (the asymptotic equipartition property) says $-\frac{1}{n}\log P(X_1, \dots, X_n) \to$ entropy, which is the Strong Law applied to $-\log P(X_i)$.
- **Statistics:** the empirical CDF $\hat{F}_n(x)$ (the fraction of data at most $x$) converges to $F(x)$ almost surely for each $x$ (Practice 3). Uniform versions are the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem.
- **Philosophy of probability:** it makes the frequency interpretation precise ([3.1](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.1-Interpretations-of-Probability.md)): in a single infinite series of trials, the relative frequency of an event converges to its probability, with probability 1.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(0.3)$. What does the Strong Law say about the fraction of ones? Is the sequence $0, 0, 0, \dots$ possible, and what is its probability?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With probability 1, the fraction of ones converges to $0.3$. The all-zero sequence is possible (it is one of the outcomes), but $P(\text{first } n \text{ are zero}) = 0.7^n \to 0$, so its probability is 0. It is one of the exceptional sequences.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** True or false: "The Strong Law implies the Weak Law." Give a reason.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

True. The Strong Law gives $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)), which is the Weak Law. (The reverse is false in general.)

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with CDF $F$. For a fixed number $x$, let $\hat{F}_n(x)$ be the fraction of the first $n$ values that are $\le x$. Show that $\hat{F}_n(x) \to F(x)$ almost surely.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $Y_i = I_{\lbrace X_i \le x \rbrace}$, which is 1 if $X_i \le x$ and 0 otherwise. The $Y_i$ are i.i.d. (functions of independent variables), with mean $E[Y_i] = P(X_i \le x) = F(x)$, which is finite. Then $\hat{F}_n(x) = \bar{Y}_n$, and the Strong Law gives $\bar{Y}_n \to F(x)$ almost surely.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X_i = \pm 1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Check the formula $E[S_n^4] = n\thinspace E[X^4] + 3n(n-1)(E[X^2])^2$ for $n = 3$ by listing the possible values of $S_3$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $X^2 = X^4 = 1$, so the formula gives $3 + 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 21$. Directly, $S_3$ equals $\pm 3$ with probability $\frac{1}{8}$ each (all three flips agree) and $\pm 1$ with probability $\frac{3}{8}$ each. So

$$E[S_3^4] = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 81 + 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 1 = 20.25 + 0.75 = 21$$

The two numbers agree.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with $E\lvert X_1 \rvert = \infty$. Show that $\bar{X}_n$ does not converge almost surely to a finite number. (You may use the second Borel-Cantelli lemma, [20.6](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md): for **independent** events with $\sum P(A_n) = \infty$, infinitely many $A_n$ happen with probability 1.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Suppose $\bar{X}_n \to L$ with positive probability, where $L$ is finite. On that event, $\frac{X_n}{n} = \bar{X}_n - \frac{n-1}{n}\bar{X}_{n-1} \to L - L = 0$. So $\lvert X_n \rvert \gt n$ happens only finitely often on that event.

Now, $E\lvert X_1 \rvert \le 1 + \sum_{n \ge 1} P(\lvert X_1 \rvert \gt n)$ (slice the integral of the tail into unit pieces). Since $E\lvert X_1 \rvert = \infty$, we get $\sum_n P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt n) = \sum_n P(\lvert X_1 \rvert \gt n) = \infty$. The events $\lbrace \lvert X_n \rvert \gt n \rbrace$ are independent, so by the second Borel-Cantelli lemma they happen infinitely often with probability 1. This contradicts the previous paragraph, so $\bar{X}_n$ converges to a finite limit with probability 0.

</details>

## Summary

- The Strong Law: if $E\lvert X_1 \rvert \lt \infty$, then $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ with probability 1 (almost sure convergence). No variance is needed.
- It is stronger than the Weak Law: it says a run stays near $\mu$ from some time on, not only that it is near $\mu$ at each fixed late time.
- The idea of the proof: show that $\sum_n P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon)$ is finite. Chebyshev's $\frac{1}{n}$ is too weak, but a fourth-moment bound of order $\frac{1}{n^2}$ works.
- "Almost surely" allows exceptional sequences (like heads forever), but they have probability 0.
- It says nothing about speed (use the CLT) and it fails when the mean does not exist.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1 \quad \text{if } E\lvert X_1 \rvert \lt \infty}
$$
