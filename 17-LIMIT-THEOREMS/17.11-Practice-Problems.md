# 17.11 Practice Problems

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.11 Practice Problems

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 3 hours · **Before this:** [17.10 Poisson Limit and Law of Rare Events](17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md)

> **Big idea:** Limit theorems answer one question in different ways: as the number of terms grows, what does the random total do; so the skill is to pick the right theorem for the question and to check its assumptions.

---

## How to Use This Set

- Each problem says which topic it uses. If you get stuck, reread that topic first.
- Try every problem on paper before opening the answer. Write each step, as in the worked examples.
- **Warm-Up** problems check the basic formulas. **Core** problems mix two or three ideas. **Challenge** problems include proofs. **Simulation Projects** ask you to write code.
- A good habit: ask first "what do I want to know, and what do I know?" The average settles at $\mu$: Law of Large Numbers. The shape of the error of an average or a sum: CLT. A count of whole numbers: continuity correction. A count of rare events: Poisson. A function of an average: delta method. An unknown $\sigma$: Slutsky. Heavy tails: check the assumptions first.
- After solving a problem by hand, check the number with a short simulation.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy)** *Uses [17.1 Modes of Convergence](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md).*
Let $X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{1}{n})$. Show that $X_n \to 0$ in mean square and in probability. Compute $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt 0.1)$ for $n = 100, 400, 10000$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Mean square: $E[X_n^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X_n) = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$. Mean square convergence implies convergence in probability ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)). Directly, $\sqrt{n}X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, so

$$P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt 0.1) = P\big(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 0.1\sqrt{n}\big) = 2\big(1 - \Phi(0.1\sqrt{n})\big)$$

For $n = 100$: $2(1 - \Phi(1)) = 0.3173$. For $n = 400$: $2(1 - \Phi(2)) = 0.0455$. For $n = 10000$: $2(1 - \Phi(10)) = 1.5 \times 10^{-23}$.

</details>

**W2. (Easy)** *Uses [17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md).*
You estimate the probability $p$ of a click by the fraction of clicks in $n$ independent page views. Use Chebyshev to find how many page views guarantee that the estimate is within $0.03$ of $p$ with probability at least $0.9$, whatever $p$ is.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A click is Bernoulli, so $\sigma^2 = p(1-p) \le \frac{1}{4}$. Use $n \ge \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2\delta}$ with $\varepsilon = 0.03$ and $\delta = 0.1$:

$$n \ge \frac{0.25}{0.0009 \times 0.1} = 2777.8$$

So $n = 2778$ page views are enough.

</details>

**W3. (Easy)** *Uses [17.5 Central Limit Theorem](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md).*
Heights in a population have mean $170$ cm and standard deviation $14$ cm. A random sample of 49 people is taken. Estimate the probability that the sample mean is below $167$ cm.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard error is $\frac{14}{\sqrt{49}} = 2$ cm, and $z = \frac{167 - 170}{2} = -1.5$:

$$P(\bar{X}_{49} \lt 167) \approx \Phi(-1.5) = 0.0668$$

</details>

**W4. (Easy)** *Uses [17.6 Normal Approximation and Continuity Correction](17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md).*
A fair coin is flipped 64 times. Estimate the probability of at least 40 heads.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\mu = 32$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{64 \times 0.25} = 4$. At least 40 means $X \ge 40$, so the boundary is $39.5$:

$$z = \frac{39.5 - 32}{4} = 1.875, \qquad P(X \ge 40) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.875) = 0.0304$$

The exact binomial value is $0.0300$.

</details>

**W5. (Easy)** *Uses [17.10 Poisson Limit and Law of Rare Events](17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md).*
3,000 independent items are tested, and each is defective with probability $0.001$. Use the Poisson approximation for the probability of at most 1 defective item.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\lambda = 3000 \times 0.001 = 3$:

$$P(Y \le 1) = e^{-3}(1 + 3) = 4e^{-3} = 0.1991$$

The exact binomial value is $0.1990$.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium)** *Uses [17.2 Relationships Between Modes](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md).*
Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $X_n = \sqrt{n}$ if $U \lt \frac{1}{n}$, and $0$ otherwise. Which of these hold for $X_n \to 0$: in probability, almost surely, in mean square? What is $E[X_n]$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In probability: for $0 \lt \varepsilon \lt \sqrt{n}$, $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$. Almost surely: for an outcome with $U = u \gt 0$, $X_n(u) \ne 0$ only for $n \lt \frac{1}{u}$, so the sequence is eventually 0. Mean square: $E[X_n^2] = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$, which does not go to 0, so it **fails**. Finally $E[X_n] = \sqrt{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0$. So this sequence converges almost surely and in probability, and its mean goes to 0, yet it does not converge in mean square.

</details>

**C2. (Medium)** *Uses [17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md) and [17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md).*
Measurements have standard deviation $\sigma = 2$. How many measurements make the average be within $0.1$ of the true mean with probability at least $0.95$, (a) by Chebyshev, (b) by the CLT? Why do the answers differ?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Chebyshev: $n \ge \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2\delta} = \frac{4}{0.01 \times 0.05} = 8000$.

(b) CLT: the average is within $1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ of $\mu$ with probability 95%, so we need $\frac{1.96 \times 2}{\sqrt{n}} \le 0.1$, which gives $\sqrt{n} \ge 39.2$ and $n \ge 1536.6$. So $n = 1537$.

Chebyshev must work for every distribution with variance 4, including the worst one, and it uses only the variance. The CLT uses the (approximately) normal shape of the average. It is about 5 times smaller, but it is an approximation, while Chebyshev is a guarantee.

</details>

**C3. (Medium)** *Uses [17.5 Central Limit Theorem](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md) and [17.8 Slutsky's Theorem](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md).*
A sample of $n = 100$ exam scores has mean $68$ and sample standard deviation $S = 12$. Give a 95% confidence interval for the true mean. Which theorem lets you use $S$ in place of the unknown $\sigma$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The estimated standard error is $\frac{12}{\sqrt{100}} = 1.2$, so the interval is $68 \pm 1.96 \times 1.2 = 68 \pm 2.35$, that is $(65.65, 70.35)$. Slutsky's theorem: $S_n \xrightarrow{p} \sigma$ by the Weak Law, so $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, the same limit as with $\sigma$.

</details>

**C4. (Medium)** *Uses [17.6 Normal Approximation and Continuity Correction](17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md).*
A cache serves each query from memory with probability $0.8$, independently. Among 500 queries, estimate the probability of at least 410 cache hits.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\mu = 500 \times 0.8 = 400$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{500 \times 0.8 \times 0.2} = \sqrt{80} = 8.944$. At least 410 means $X \ge 410$, so the boundary is $409.5$:

$$z = \frac{409.5 - 400}{8.944} = 1.062, \qquad P(X \ge 410) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.062) = 0.1441$$

The exact binomial value is $0.1437$.

</details>

**C5. (Medium)** *Uses [17.7 Delta Method](17.7-Delta-Method.md).*
Response times have mean $5$ seconds and standard deviation $2$ seconds. You average $n = 400$ of them and estimate the **throughput** $\frac{1}{\mu}$ (requests per second) by $\frac{1}{\bar{X}_n}$. Find the estimate's standard error and an approximate 95% interval around $0.2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard error of $\bar{X}_n$ is $\frac{2}{\sqrt{400}} = 0.1$. For $g(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, $g'(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2}$ and $\lvert g'(5) \rvert = \frac{1}{25} = 0.04$. The standard error of $\frac{1}{\bar{X}_n}$ is

$$0.04 \times 0.1 = 0.004$$

The interval around $0.2$ is $0.2 \pm 1.96 \times 0.004 = 0.2 \pm 0.0078$, that is $(0.192, 0.208)$.

</details>

**C6. (Medium)** *Uses [17.10](17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md) and [17.6](17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md).*
In a given minute, each of 10,000 customers phones a call center with probability $0.0005$, independently. Estimate $P(\text{more than 8 calls})$ with (a) the Poisson approximation and (b) the normal approximation with continuity correction. Which is better?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The number of calls is $\text{Bin}(10000, 0.0005)$ with mean $\lambda = 5$. More than 8 means $X \ge 9$.

(a) Poisson: $P(Y \ge 9) = 1 - P(Y \le 8) = 0.0681$.

(b) Normal: $\sigma = \sqrt{10000 \times 0.0005 \times 0.9995} = 2.2355$ and $z = \frac{8.5 - 5}{2.2355} = 1.566$, so $P \approx 1 - \Phi(1.566) = 0.0587$.

The exact binomial value is $0.0681$. The Poisson answer is almost exact. The normal approximation is 14% too low, because with a mean of only 5 and $p$ tiny, the distribution is skewed to the right.

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md) and [16.2 Chebyshev Inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md).*
Prove the Weak Law of Large Numbers using Chebyshev's inequality: if $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, then $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$. Then explain what changes if the $X_i$ are only uncorrelated.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Mean of the average.** By linearity of expectation:

$$E[\bar{X}_n] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} E[X_i] = \frac{n\mu}{n} = \mu$$

**Variance of the average.** The variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of the variances, and the constant $\frac{1}{n}$ comes out squared:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

**Chebyshev.** For any $\varepsilon \gt 0$:

$$P\big(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n)}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$$

The right side goes to 0 as $n \to \infty$ because $\sigma^2$ and $\varepsilon$ are fixed numbers. This is the definition of $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$.

**Uncorrelated variables.** Independence was used only to say that the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances. This also holds when $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0$ for $i \ne j$ ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)). So the proof works unchanged. If the variances differ but are all at most $M$, the bound becomes $\frac{M}{n\varepsilon^2}$, which still goes to 0.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [17.9 When the CLT Fails](17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md) and [17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md).*
Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. standard Cauchy with $n = 2m + 1$ odd, and let $M_n$ be their **median** (the $(m+1)$-th smallest value). Prove that $M_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$, although the sample mean does not converge. Use the Weak Law for indicator variables.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fix $\varepsilon \gt 0$. The median is above $\varepsilon$ exactly when at least $m + 1$ of the $n$ values are above $\varepsilon$, which means that the **fraction** of values above $\varepsilon$ is at least $\frac{m+1}{n} \gt \frac{1}{2}$.

Let $I_i = 1$ if $X_i \gt \varepsilon$. The $I_i$ are i.i.d. Bernoulli with

$$q = P(X \gt \varepsilon) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(\varepsilon) \lt \frac{1}{2}$$

By the Weak Law, the fraction $\bar{I}_n$ converges in probability to $q$. Let $\eta = \frac{1}{2} - q \gt 0$. If the fraction is at least $\frac{1}{2}$, it is at least $\eta$ away from $q$, so

$$P(M_n \gt \varepsilon) \le P\big(\bar{I}_n \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\big) \le P\big(\lvert \bar{I}_n - q \rvert \ge \eta\big) \le \frac{q(1-q)}{n\eta^2} \to 0$$

The same argument with $X_i \lt -\varepsilon$ gives $P(M_n \lt -\varepsilon) \to 0$. Hence $P(\lvert M_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \to 0$, and $M_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$. The median is stable because it depends only on how many values are above or below a level, not on how large the extreme values are. This is why robust estimators survive heavy tails.

</details>

**H3. (Hard)** *Uses [17.5 Central Limit Theorem](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md).*
For averages of $\text{Exp}(1)$ values, the largest error of the CLT in the CDF is about $\frac{0.133}{\sqrt{n}}$. (a) How large must $n$ be for an error of at most $0.01$? (b) What does the Berry-Esseen bound with $C = 0.4748$ and $\rho = 2.4146$ require? (c) Check (a) with the exact Gamma distribution.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $\frac{0.133}{\sqrt{n}} \le 0.01$ gives $\sqrt{n} \ge 13.3$, so $n \ge 176.9$ and $n = 177$.

(b) With $\sigma = 1$, the bound is $\frac{0.4748 \times 2.4146}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1.1465}{\sqrt{n}} \le 0.01$, so $\sqrt{n} \ge 114.65$ and $n \ge 13{,}144$. The bound is safe but asks for 74 times more data.

(c) The sum of $n$ exponentials is $\text{Gamma}(n, 1)$, so the exact CDF of the standardized sum is `gamma.cdf(n + z * sqrt(n), n)`.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import gamma, norm

z = np.linspace(-6, 6, 1_200_001)
print("      n   largest error   0.133/sqrt(n)   Berry-Esseen bound")
for n in [30, 100, 176, 177, 1000]:
    cdf = gamma.cdf(n + z * np.sqrt(n), n)          # exact CDF of the standardized sum of n Exp(1)
    err = np.max(np.abs(cdf - norm.cdf(z)))
    print(f"{n:>7}   {err:>13.5f}   {0.133 / np.sqrt(n):>13.5f}   {0.4748 * 2.4146 / np.sqrt(n):>18.4f}")
print("n needed by Berry-Esseen for error 0.01:", int(np.ceil((0.4748 * 2.4146 / 0.01) ** 2)))
```

Output:

```text
      n   largest error   0.133/sqrt(n)   Berry-Esseen bound
     30         0.02429         0.02428               0.2093
    100         0.01330         0.01330               0.1146
    176         0.01002         0.01003               0.0864
    177         0.01000         0.01000               0.0862
   1000         0.00421         0.00421               0.0363
n needed by Berry-Esseen for error 0.01: 13144
```

The error drops below $0.01$ at $n = 177$ (at $n = 176$ it is $0.01002$), exactly as the rule predicts.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Project)** *Uses [17.5 Central Limit Theorem](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md).*
**Watch the CLT happen.** Average $n = 1, 2, 5, 30$ draws from $\text{Exp}(1)$ (200,000 times each), standardize, and print a text histogram next to the standard normal bins. At which $n$ does the skew become hard to see? What remains different at $n = 30$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000
edges = np.arange(-3.0, 3.01, 0.5)              # 12 bins of width 0.5 on the standardized scale
columns = {}
for n in [1, 2, 5, 30]:
    xbar = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_trials, n)).mean(axis=1)
    z = (xbar - 1.0) * np.sqrt(n)               # standardized average (mean 0, sd 1)
    columns[f"n={n}"] = np.histogram(z, bins=edges)[0] / n_trials
columns["normal"] = np.diff(norm.cdf(edges))

def bar(p):
    """One # per 0.04 of probability; a dot marks a small but non-zero bin."""
    return "#" * round(p / 0.04) or ("." if p > 0.002 else "")

print("  z from  " + "".join(f"{name:<11}" for name in columns))
for i, left in enumerate(edges[:-1]):
    print(f"  {left:>5.1f}   " + "".join(f"{bar(col[i]):<11}" for col in columns.values()))
print("  (one # = 0.04 of the probability, bins 0.5 wide, Exp(1) data)")
```

Output:

```text
  z from  n=1        n=2        n=5        n=30       normal     
   -3.0                                               .          
   -2.5                                    .          .          
   -2.0                         #          #          #          
   -1.5              ###        ###        ###        ##         
   -1.0   ########## ######     #####      ####       ####       
   -0.5   ######     ######     #####      #####      #####      
    0.0   ####       ####       ####       #####      #####      
    0.5   ##         ###        ###        ###        ####       
    1.0   #          ##         ##         ##         ##         
    1.5   #          #          #          #          #          
    2.0   .          #          #          .          .          
    2.5   .          .          .          .          .          
  (one # = 0.04 of the probability, bins 0.5 wide, Exp(1) data)
```

For $n = 1$ the histogram is a skewed exponential with nothing below $-1$. By $n = 5$ the left tail exists and the pile-up near $-1$ is smaller (4 marks instead of 10). At $n = 30$ the rows agree with the normal column except in the bin from $0.5$ to $1.0$ ($3$ marks against $4$) and in the thin left tail. The remaining difference is the skewness $\frac{2}{\sqrt{30}} = 0.37$, which a coarse text histogram can hardly show.

</details>

**S2. (Project)** *Uses [17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md), [17.4](17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md) and [17.9](17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md).*
**Running averages of four distributions.** For one long run of $10^6$ values from each of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, $\text{Exp}(1)$, Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$ (mean 3, infinite variance) and the standard Cauchy, print the running average at $n = 10, 10^2, \dots, 10^6$. Which ones settle, which settle slowly, which never?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_max = 1_000_000
samples = {
    "Normal(0,1)": rng.normal(size=n_max),              # mean 0
    "Exp(1)": rng.exponential(1.0, size=n_max),         # mean 1
    "Pareto(1.5)": 1 + rng.pareto(1.5, size=n_max),     # mean 3, infinite variance
    "Cauchy": rng.standard_cauchy(size=n_max),          # no mean
}
running = {name: np.cumsum(x) / np.arange(1, n_max + 1) for name, x in samples.items()}

print(f"{'n':>9}" + "".join(f"{name:>14}" for name in running))
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    print(f"{n:>9}" + "".join(f"{avg[n - 1]:>14.4f}" for avg in running.values()))
```

Output:

```text
        n   Normal(0,1)        Exp(1)   Pareto(1.5)        Cauchy
       10       -0.3356        0.8356        3.0485       -6.3878
      100       -0.0503        0.8627        2.5663       -2.2516
     1000       -0.0289        0.9902        3.1000       -1.0156
    10000       -0.0102        1.0144        3.4677       10.3235
   100000       -0.0042        0.9985        3.0583        0.0119
  1000000        0.0001        0.9991        2.9938        0.6822
```

The normal and exponential averages settle quickly at their means $0$ and $1$ (error about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$). The Pareto average wanders (it is $3.47$ at $n = 10^4$ and $2.99$ at $n = 10^6$), because an occasional huge value moves it, but it converges to $3$ slowly (the Strong Law holds since the mean is finite). The Cauchy average never settles: it jumps from $-1.0$ to $10.3$ to $0.01$ to $0.68$, with no tendency to shrink, since the average of $n$ Cauchy values is again Cauchy.

</details>
