# 17.1 Modes of Convergence

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.1 Modes of Convergence

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 16: Inequalities and Concentration](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/README.md)

> **Big idea:** A sequence of random variables can "get close" to a target in four different ways (in distribution, in probability, in mean square, almost surely), and every limit theorem says which one it proves.

---

## Why It Matters

A sequence of ordinary numbers either converges or it does not. A sequence of **random** variables is harder: each $X_n$ is a whole bundle of possible values, so the sentence $X_n$ is close to $X$ can mean several different things. The Law of Large Numbers, the Central Limit Theorem and the Poisson limit each use a different meaning. If you mix them up, you will misread what a theorem promises. This topic gives the four definitions. [17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md) shows how they fit together.

## Intuition

Imagine 1,000 friends. Each friend flips a fair coin again and again, and after every flip reports the fraction of heads so far. Each friend's reports form a sequence $X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots$ that should settle near $0.5$. But what does settle mean? Here are four different promises.

1. **Each friend's own list eventually stays close to 0.5 forever.** Look at one friend's whole list. After some flip, every report is within 0.01 of 0.5. This is the strongest promise. It is called **almost sure** convergence.
2. **At a fixed late time, almost every friend is close.** Pick flip number 10,000. Only a tiny fraction of the 1,000 friends report a number far from 0.5. A different friend may be the bad one at flip 10,001. This is convergence **in probability**.
3. **The squared error is small on average.** Average $(X_n - 0.5)^2$ over friends. This average goes to 0. This is convergence **in mean square**.
4. **The crowd of reports has a stable shape.** We do not compare friend with friend. We only ask whether the histogram of the 1,000 reports at time $n$ approaches a fixed histogram. This is convergence **in distribution**. It is the weakest promise, because it says nothing about any single friend.

The Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md) and [17.4](17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) is promise 2 and promise 1. The Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) is promise 4.

## Picture

```text
  strongest                                                       weakest

  +-----------------+     +-----------------+     +-----------------+
  | almost surely   |     | in probability  |     | in distribution |
  | every run       |---->| most runs are   |---->| only the shape  |
  | settles down    |     | close at time n |     | of X_n is close |
  +-----------------+     +-----------------+     +-----------------+
                                   ^
  +-----------------+              |
  | in mean square  |--------------+
  | squared gap     |
  | shrinks to 0    |
  +-----------------+
```

An arrow means "implies". Almost sure and mean square both imply in probability, and in probability implies in distribution. The reverse arrows are false. [17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md) proves the arrows and gives a counterexample for each missing one.

## Definition

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ and $X$ be random variables on the same sample space $\Omega$. A random variable is a function: it gives each outcome $\omega$ a number $X_n(\omega)$. Here are the four modes, from the easiest to state to the hardest.

**1. Convergence in distribution.** $X_n$ **converges in distribution** to $X$, written $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$, if

$$
F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x) \quad \text{for every } x \text{ where } F_X \text{ is continuous}
$$

**In words:** the CDF of $X_n$ gets close to the CDF of $X$. Only the distributions are compared, never the values.

**2. Convergence in probability.** $X_n$ **converges in probability** to $X$, written $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$, if for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$

$$
P\big(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty
$$

**In words:** for any tolerance $\varepsilon$, the chance that $X_n$ is farther than $\varepsilon$ from $X$ goes to zero.

**3. Convergence in mean square.** $X_n$ **converges in mean square** to $X$, written $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} X$, if

$$
E\big[(X_n - X)^2\big] \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty
$$

**In words:** the average squared distance between $X_n$ and $X$ goes to zero. (This needs $E[X_n^2]$ and $E[X^2]$ to be finite.)

**4. Almost sure convergence.** $X_n$ **converges almost surely** to $X$, written $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X$, if

$$
P\big(\lbrace \omega : X_n(\omega) \to X(\omega) \rbrace\big) = 1
$$

**In words:** for each outcome $\omega$ we get an ordinary sequence of numbers $X_1(\omega), X_2(\omega), \dots$. With probability 1, that sequence converges to $X(\omega)$. "Almost surely" means "with probability 1", so outcomes that fail are allowed, but they have total probability 0.

Often the limit is not a random variable but a constant $c$. Then we write $X_n \xrightarrow{p} c$, and so on, and all definitions work with $X = c$. For modes 2 to 4 the limit is unique up to events of probability 0. For mode 1 only the distribution of the limit is unique.

## Why It Is True

There is nothing to prove in a definition, but three questions explain why the definitions look the way they do.

**Why do we need "almost" in the almost sure definition?** The set of bad outcomes can be non-empty. Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $X_n = U^n$. For every $\omega$ with $U(\omega) \lt 1$ we have $U^n \to 0$. For the single outcome $U = 1$ we get $1^n = 1$, which does not go to 0. That one outcome has probability 0, so $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0$ is still true.

**Why only at continuity points of $F_X$?** Take the constant $X_n = \frac{1}{n}$ and the constant $X = 0$. Surely $X_n$ should converge to $X$. But $F_{X_n}(0) = 0$ for every $n$, since $\frac{1}{n} \gt 0$, while $F_X(0) = 1$. So $F_{X_n}(0) \not\to F_X(0)$. The point $0$ is a jump of $F_X$ (not a continuity point), and the definition rightly ignores it. At every other point, for instance $x = 0.001$ or $x = -3$, the CDFs do converge.

**Why is convergence in distribution weaker?** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and define $X_n = -X$ for every $n$. By symmetry, $-X$ also has the $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ distribution, so $F_{X_n} = F_X$ and $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ is true. But $\lvert X_n - X \rvert = 2\lvert X \rvert$ never shrinks: $X_n$ is the mirror image of $X$, not close to it. Convergence in distribution forgets which outcome produced which value.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Largest of n Uniform Numbers

**Problem.** Let $U_1, \dots, U_n$ be independent and uniform on $[0, 1]$, and let $M_n = \max(U_1, \dots, U_n)$. Show that $M_n \to 1$ in probability and in mean square, and find the limit in distribution of $n(1 - M_n)$.

**Solution.**

1. Since $M_n \le 1$, we have $\lvert M_n - 1 \rvert \gt \varepsilon$ exactly when $M_n \lt 1 - \varepsilon$. The maximum is below $1 - \varepsilon$ only if all $n$ numbers are:

$$
P\big(\lvert M_n - 1 \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) = P(U_1 \lt 1 - \varepsilon, \dots, U_n \lt 1 - \varepsilon) = (1 - \varepsilon)^n
$$

2. For fixed $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$, $(1 - \varepsilon)^n \to 0$. For $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and $n = 10$ the value is $0.9^{10} \approx 0.3487$. For $n = 50$ it is about $0.00515$. So $M_n \xrightarrow{p} 1$.
3. Let $W_n = 1 - M_n$. Then $P(W_n \gt y) = (1 - y)^n$, so $W_n$ has density $n(1 - y)^{n-1}$ on $[0, 1]$. This is the $\text{Beta}(1, n)$ distribution ([9.5](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md)), whose second moment is $\frac{1 \cdot 2}{(n+1)(n+2)}$:

$$
E\big[(M_n - 1)^2\big] = E[W_n^2] = \frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)} \to 0
$$

   For $n = 10$ this is $\frac{2}{132} \approx 0.01515$. So $M_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} 1$.
4. Rescale to see the shape of the gap. For $x \ge 0$:

$$
P\big(n(1 - M_n) \gt x\big) = P\left(W_n \gt \tfrac{x}{n}\right) = \left(1 - \frac{x}{n}\right)^n \to e^{-x}
$$

   So $P\big(n(1 - M_n) \le x\big) \to 1 - e^{-x}$, which is the CDF of $\text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1). Hence $n(1 - M_n) \xrightarrow{d} \text{Exp}(1)$.

**Answer.** $M_n \xrightarrow{p} 1$ and $M_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} 1$. The gap $1 - M_n$ is about $\frac{1}{n}$ times an $\text{Exp}(1)$ variable.

**Check.** The simulation below gives $0.5903$ against the exact $0.5905$ for $n = 5$ in the first column, and the third column creeps toward $1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321$. This example shows one sequence that converges in three modes: the first two give a number (1), and the third gives a shape (Exp(1) after rescaling).

### Example 2: Same Distribution, Not Close

**Problem.** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $X_n = -X$ for all $n$. Does $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$? Does $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$?

**Solution.**

1. The CDF of $-X$ is $P(-X \le x) = P(X \ge -x) = 1 - \Phi(-x) = \Phi(x)$, because the normal curve is symmetric. So $F_{X_n} = F_X$ for every $n$, and $F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x)$ trivially. Yes, $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$.
2. For convergence in probability, take $\varepsilon = 1$. Then $X_n - X = -2X$, so

$$
P\big(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt 1\big) = P\left(\lvert X \rvert \gt \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 2\big(1 - \Phi(0.5)\big) = 2 \times 0.3085 = 0.6171
$$

3. This number is $0.6171$ for every $n$, so it does not go to 0. No, $X_n \not\xrightarrow{p} X$.

**Answer.** Convergence in distribution holds, convergence in probability fails.

**Check.** The mirror image of a bell curve is the same bell curve, but the values are on opposite sides. That is why the statement $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ never allows you to subtract $X_n$ and $X$.

### Example 3: A Poll Gets Closer to the Truth

**Problem.** Exactly half of the voters support party A. A poll asks $n$ random voters (independently), and $\bar{X}_n$ is the fraction who support A. Compute $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - 0.5 \rvert \gt 0.05)$ for $n = 10, 100, 400, 1000$.

**Solution.**

1. The number of supporters is $K \sim \text{Bin}(n, 0.5)$, and $\bar{X}_n = \frac{K}{n}$. The event that $\bar{X}_n$ is off by more than $0.05$ is $K \lt 0.45n$ or $K \gt 0.55n$.
2. For $n = 10$: the event is $K \ne 5$, so $P = 1 - \binom{10}{5} / 2^{10} = 1 - \frac{252}{1024} = 0.7539$.
3. For $n = 100$: the event is $K \le 44$ or $K \ge 56$. Adding the binomial probabilities (with `scipy.stats.binom`) gives $0.2713$.
4. In the same way, $n = 400$ gives $0.0402$ and $n = 1000$ gives $0.0014$.

| $n$ | 10 | 100 | 400 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - 0.5 \rvert \gt 0.05)$ | $0.7539$ | $0.2713$ | $0.0402$ | $0.0014$ |

**Answer.** The chance of a poll being off by more than 5 points falls from 75% to 0.14%.

**Check.** The numbers go to 0 as $n$ grows, which is the definition of $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} 0.5$. Notice that we proved nothing yet: we only computed four values. That the same thing happens for every distribution is the Weak Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).

## Simulate It

The code uses Example 1. It compares the simulated and exact values for the three kinds of convergence of $M_n$, the maximum of $n$ uniform numbers.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# M_n is the largest of n uniform(0, 1) numbers. It should creep up to 1.
print("   n | P(far): sim  exact | E[(M-1)^2]: sim   exact | CDF at 1: sim  exact")
for n in [5, 10, 50, 200]:
    m = rng.random((n_trials, n)).max(axis=1)
    far = np.mean(np.abs(m - 1) > 0.1)          # in probability: exact 0.9^n
    ms = np.mean((m - 1) ** 2)                  # mean square: exact 2/((n+1)(n+2))
    cdf = np.mean(n * (1 - m) <= 1)             # distribution: exact 1-(1-1/n)^n
    print(f"{n:>4} |   {far:.5f} {0.9**n:.5f} |        {ms:.6f} {2 / ((n + 1) * (n + 2)):.6f} |"
          f"        {cdf:.4f} {1 - (1 - 1 / n) ** n:.4f}")
print(f"Limit of the last column: 1 - exp(-1) = {1 - np.exp(-1):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
   n | P(far): sim  exact | E[(M-1)^2]: sim   exact | CDF at 1: sim  exact
   5 |   0.59026 0.59049 |        0.047635 0.047619 |        0.6721 0.6723
  10 |   0.34957 0.34868 |        0.015243 0.015152 |        0.6504 0.6513
  50 |   0.00520 0.00515 |        0.000752 0.000754 |        0.6386 0.6358
 200 |   0.00000 0.00000 |        0.000049 0.000049 |        0.6334 0.6330
Limit of the last column: 1 - exp(-1) = 0.6321
```

The first two column pairs go to 0 (convergence in probability and in mean square). The third pair goes to $0.6321$, the value of the $\text{Exp}(1)$ CDF at 1 (convergence in distribution after rescaling by $n$).

## Common Mistakes

- **Saying "converges" without a mode.** The sentence $\bar{X}_n$ converges to $\mu$ is incomplete. Say in probability (weak law) or almost surely (strong law). The two statements are different theorems.
- **Reading $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ as: $X_n$ is close to $X$.** Only the distributions are close. In Example 2, $X_n$ is the mirror image of $X$.
- **Demanding $F_n(x) \to F(x)$ at every point.** At a jump of $F$ it can fail, as the sequence $\frac{1}{n} \to 0$ shows. The definition asks for continuity points only.
- **Thinking $P(X_n = X) \to 1$ is needed.** For continuous variables, $P(X_n = X)$ can be 0 for every $n$ while $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$. For example $X_n = X + \frac{Z}{n}$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ independent of $X$: $X_n \ne X$ almost surely, yet the gap $\frac{Z}{n}$ shrinks.
- **Forgetting that the variables must live on the same sample space.** Modes 2 to 4 compare $X_n(\omega)$ with $X(\omega)$ for the same outcome. Convergence in distribution is the only mode that makes sense for variables defined on different spaces.

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics and machine learning:** an estimator is **consistent** if it converges in probability to the true parameter. This is the minimum we ask of any estimator, such as the average loss on a growing test set.
- **Optimization:** stochastic gradient descent theorems (Robbins and Monro) prove almost sure convergence of the parameters under a decreasing learning rate. Others prove convergence in mean square.
- **Deep learning theory:** the output of a very wide neural network at initialization converges in distribution to a Gaussian process. Only the distribution is claimed, never the individual values.
- **Simulation:** Monte Carlo estimates ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)) converge almost surely (strong law), and in practice we judge them by their mean square error.
- **Everyday life:** "polls are accurate if they are large" is convergence in probability. "The crowd's average guess is right" is a statement about the shape of the crowd, which is closer to convergence in distribution.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X_n = 3 + \frac{1}{n}$ (a constant, not random). Check that $X_n \to 3$ in probability and in mean square. Does $F_{X_n}(x) \to F_3(x)$ for every $x$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $n \gt \frac{1}{\varepsilon}$ we have $\lvert X_n - 3 \rvert = \frac{1}{n} \lt \varepsilon$, so $P(\lvert X_n - 3 \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = 0$ from then on. Mean square:

$$E\big[(X_n - 3)^2\big] = \frac{1}{n^2} \to 0$$

The CDF of the constant 3 jumps at 3. We have $F_{X_n}(x) = 0$ for $x \lt 3 + \frac{1}{n}$ and $1$ after. At $x \lt 3$ both are 0, and at $x \gt 3$ both are eventually 1, so the CDFs converge everywhere except possibly at the jump $x = 3$: there $F_{X_n}(3) = 0$ but $F(3) = 1$. This is allowed, since $3$ is not a continuity point.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $X_n$ be $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{n}$ and $0$ otherwise. Show that $X_n \to 0$ in probability and in mean square.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $0 \lt \varepsilon \lt 1$ we have $P(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = P(X_n = 1) = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$. Also $E[X_n^2] = 1^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n} + 0 = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$. (For $\varepsilon \ge 1$ the probability is already 0.)

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $U_1, \dots, U_n$ be independent uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $Y_n = \min(U_1, \dots, U_n)$. Show that $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$, and that $nY_n \xrightarrow{d} \text{Exp}(1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The minimum is above $y$ only if all numbers are, so $P(Y_n \gt y) = (1 - y)^n$. For $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$:

$$P(\lvert Y_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) = (1 - \varepsilon)^n \to 0$$

For the rescaled variable, with $x \ge 0$:

$$P(nY_n \gt x) = P\left(Y_n \gt \tfrac{x}{n}\right) = \left(1 - \tfrac{x}{n}\right)^n \to e^{-x}$$

This is the survival function of $\text{Exp}(1)$, so $nY_n \xrightarrow{d} \text{Exp}(1)$. (The minimum and the maximum are mirror images: $Y_n$ has the same distribution as $1 - M_n$ in Example 1.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X$ be $0$ or $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, and $X_n = 1 - X$ for all $n$. Show that $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ but $X_n \not\xrightarrow{p} X$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$1 - X$ is also $0$ or $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, so $X_n$ and $X$ have the same CDF and $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$. But $\lvert X_n - X \rvert = \lvert 1 - 2X \rvert = 1$ always, so for $\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2}$:

$$P\big(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \tfrac{1}{2}\big) = 1 \not\to 0$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove: if $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} Y$, then $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{p} X + Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the triangle inequality, $\lvert (X_n + Y_n) - (X + Y) \rvert \le \lvert X_n - X \rvert + \lvert Y_n - Y \rvert$. If the left side is larger than $\varepsilon$, then at least one of the two terms on the right is larger than $\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$. By the union bound ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)):

$$P\big(\lvert X_n + Y_n - X - Y \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le P\big(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big) + P\big(\lvert Y_n - Y \rvert \gt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)$$

Both terms on the right go to 0, so the left side does.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** For $Y_n = \min(U_1, \dots, U_n)$ with $n = 100$, estimate $P(nY_n \le 1)$ and compare with $1 - (1 - \frac{1}{n})^n$ and with the limit $1 - e^{-1}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, n_trials = 100, 200_000
y = rng.random((n_trials, n)).min(axis=1)       # minimum of n uniform numbers
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(n * y <= 1):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {1 - (1 - 1 / n) ** n:.4f}")
print(f"Limit:      {1 - np.exp(-1):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.6332
Exact:      0.6340
Limit:      0.6321
```

</details>

## Summary

- A sequence of random variables $X_n$ can converge to $X$ in four ways: in distribution, in probability, in mean square and almost surely.
- In distribution compares only CDFs (at continuity points). The other three compare $X_n(\omega)$ with $X(\omega)$ for the same outcome.
- In probability: $P(\lvert X_n - X \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \to 0$. In mean square: $E[(X_n - X)^2] \to 0$. Almost surely: the number sequence $X_n(\omega)$ converges for outcomes of total probability 1.
- Always name the mode. The weak law is about convergence in probability, the strong law about almost sure convergence, the CLT about convergence in distribution.

The one idea to remember:

$$
\boxed{X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X \text{ or } X_n \xrightarrow{\text{m.s.}} X \implies X_n \xrightarrow{p} X \implies X_n \xrightarrow{d} X}
$$
