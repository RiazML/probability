# 17.8 Slutsky's Theorem

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.8 Slutsky's Theorem

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [17.7 Delta Method](17.7-Delta-Method.md)

> **Big idea:** If one quantity settles to a fixed number (in probability), you may treat it as that number inside a limit in distribution; so you can replace an unknown $\sigma$ by its estimate and keep the normal limit.

---

## Why It Matters

The CLT ([17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) says $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. It has a practical problem: it contains $\sigma$, and in real life we do not know $\sigma$. We only have the sample standard deviation $S_n$. Is the result still true with $S_n$ in place of $\sigma$? **Slutsky's theorem** says yes. It is the quiet tool behind every confidence interval and z-test that uses an estimated standard error, and it appeared in the proof of the delta method ([17.7](17.7-Delta-Method.md)).

## Intuition

You have a quantity $X_n$ that wobbles like a bell curve, and a second quantity $Y_n$ that is a bit random but is settling down to the fixed number 5. After many observations, $Y_n$ is almost exactly 5. So inside any probability statement, $X_n + Y_n$ behaves like $X_n + 5$, which is just the bell curve shifted by 5. And $X_n \cdot Y_n$ behaves like $5X_n$, the bell curve stretched by 5.

The key is that $Y_n$ **freezes**. Compare this with a second quantity that does not freeze. Suppose $Y_n$ is another copy of the wobble, say the mirror image $-X_n$. Then $X_n + Y_n = 0$, which has nothing to do with "bell curve plus bell curve". Freezing (convergence in probability to a constant) is the condition that makes the rule work.

## Picture

```text
  Slutsky's rules:   X_n --d--> X   and   Y_n --p--> c  (a fixed number)

     X_n + Y_n   --d-->   X + c        shifted shape
     X_n * Y_n   --d-->   c * X        stretched shape
     X_n / Y_n   --d-->   X / c        stretched shape   (c not 0)

  The t-statistic as an example:

     sqrt(n) (Xbar - mu) / sigma   --d-->  N(0, 1)       (the CLT)
     sigma / S_n                   --p-->  1             (S_n near sigma)
     ------------------------------------------------------------------
     sqrt(n) (Xbar - mu) / S_n     --d-->  N(0, 1) x 1  =  N(0, 1)
```

The unknown $\sigma$ in the CLT can be replaced by the estimate $S_n$, because the ratio $\frac{\sigma}{S_n}$ goes to 1.

## Definition

**Slutsky's theorem.** Let $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$, where $c$ is a **constant** (not a random variable). Then

$$
X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c, \qquad X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} cX, \qquad \frac{X_n}{Y_n} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{X}{c} \ \ (c \ne 0)
$$

where:

- $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ means the distribution of $X_n$ approaches the distribution of $X$ ([17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md)).
- $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$ means $Y_n$ is within any tolerance of $c$ with probability going to 1. By [17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md), convergence in distribution to a constant is the same thing.

**In words:** when one part of an expression converges in distribution and the other converges to a fixed number, you can compute the limit by plugging in the fixed number.

**Special case, the studentized mean.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ with $0 \lt \sigma^2 \lt \infty$, and let $S_n^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2$ be the **sample variance**. Then

$$
\frac{\sqrt{n}\thinspace(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

## Why It Is True

### Proof of the Sum Rule

We use the same sandwich as in the proof that "in probability implies in distribution" ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)). Let $z$ be a point where the CDF of $X + c$ is continuous, which means $z - c$ is a continuity point of $F_X$. Pick a small $\varepsilon \gt 0$ such that $z - c - \varepsilon$ and $z - c + \varepsilon$ are also continuity points of $F_X$ (all but countably many $\varepsilon$ work). Let $b_n = P(\lvert Y_n - c \rvert \gt \varepsilon)$. Two inclusions of events give:

- If $X_n + Y_n \le z$ and $\lvert Y_n - c \rvert \le \varepsilon$, then $X_n \le z - c + \varepsilon$. So $\lbrace X_n + Y_n \le z \rbrace \subseteq \lbrace X_n \le z - c + \varepsilon \rbrace \cup \lbrace \lvert Y_n - c \rvert \gt \varepsilon \rbrace$.
- If $X_n \le z - c - \varepsilon$ and $\lvert Y_n - c \rvert \le \varepsilon$, then $X_n + Y_n \le z$. So $\lbrace X_n \le z - c - \varepsilon \rbrace \subseteq \lbrace X_n + Y_n \le z \rbrace \cup \lbrace \lvert Y_n - c \rvert \gt \varepsilon \rbrace$.

Taking probabilities:

$$
F_{X_n}(z - c - \varepsilon) - b_n \le P(X_n + Y_n \le z) \le F_{X_n}(z - c + \varepsilon) + b_n
$$

Let $n \to \infty$. Then $b_n \to 0$, and $F_{X_n}$ converges to $F_X$ at the two continuity points. So $F_X(z - c - \varepsilon) \le \liminf P(X_n + Y_n \le z) \le \limsup P(X_n + Y_n \le z) \le F_X(z - c + \varepsilon)$. Let $\varepsilon \to 0$ along good values. Because $F_X$ is continuous at $z - c$, both bounds tend to $F_X(z - c) = P(X + c \le z)$. So $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c$.

### The Product and the Ratio

Write $X_n Y_n = c X_n + (Y_n - c) X_n$. The first term converges in distribution to $cX$ (the map $x \mapsto cx$ is continuous). The second term goes to 0 in probability: $Y_n - c \xrightarrow{p} 0$ and $X_n$ cannot run away to infinity, because it converges in distribution (Practice 5 proves this lemma). So the product rule follows from the sum rule, with $0$ as the constant. For the ratio, write $\frac{X_n}{Y_n} = X_n \cdot \frac{1}{Y_n}$. If $c \ne 0$, then $\frac{1}{Y_n} \xrightarrow{p} \frac{1}{c}$ (continuous functions keep convergence in probability, [17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)), and the product rule gives the limit $\frac{X}{c}$.

### Why the Studentized Mean Works

First, $S_n \xrightarrow{p} \sigma$. Expand the sample variance:

$$
S_n^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i^2 - \bar{X}_n^2\right)
$$

By the Weak Law ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) applied to the i.i.d. values $X_i^2$ (their mean $E[X^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2$ is finite), $\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2 + \mu^2$. By the Weak Law again, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$, so $\bar{X}_n^2 \xrightarrow{p} \mu^2$. Also $\frac{n}{n-1} \to 1$. Sums and products of things that converge in probability converge in probability to the sum and product of the limits. So $S_n^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2 + \mu^2 - \mu^2 = \sigma^2$, and by continuity of the square root, $S_n \xrightarrow{p} \sigma$.

Then use the product rule of Slutsky:

$$
\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n} = \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma}}_{\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sigma}{S_n}}_{\xrightarrow{p} 1} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Poll With an Estimated Standard Error

**Problem.** In a poll of $n = 1000$ voters, 54% say they support a candidate, so $\hat{p} = 0.54$. Find a 95% confidence interval for the true support $p$.

**Solution.**

1. The CLT for a proportion says $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{p} - p)}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The denominator contains the unknown $p$.
2. By the Weak Law, $\hat{p} \xrightarrow{p} p$, so $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})} \xrightarrow{p} \sqrt{p(1-p)}$ (continuity). By Slutsky,

$$
\frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{p} - p)}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

3. So we may use the estimated standard error: $\sqrt{\frac{0.54 \times 0.46}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.0002484} = 0.01576$.
4. The 95% interval is $0.54 \pm 1.96 \times 0.01576 = 0.54 \pm 0.0309$.

**Answer.** $(0.509, \thinspace 0.571)$, so about 51% to 57%.

**Check.** Using the unknown true $p$ in the standard error is impossible, but using $\hat{p}$ costs almost nothing for $n = 1000$. The simulation in Practice 6 (with $p = 0.1$) shows a coverage of $0.949$ for $n = 400$ but only $0.877$ for $n = 20$, so Slutsky is a large-sample tool.

### Example 2: Latency With an Unknown Spread

**Problem.** You time $n = 64$ requests on a server. The sample mean is $205$ ms and the sample standard deviation is $S = 40$ ms. (a) Give a 95% confidence interval for the true mean. (b) Test whether the true mean could be $200$ ms.

**Solution.**

1. The estimated standard error is $\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{40}{8} = 5$ ms.
2. (a) By Slutsky (the studentized mean), the interval is $205 \pm 1.96 \times 5 = 205 \pm 9.8$, that is $(195.2, \thinspace 214.8)$.
3. (b) The statistic is $T = \frac{205 - 200}{5} = 1.0$. For a standard normal this is well inside $\pm 1.96$, so $200$ ms is a believable value (it is inside the interval).
4. Compare with the Student $t$ interval ([9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)), which uses $t_{63} = 1.998$ instead of $1.96$: $205 \pm 9.99$, that is $(195.0, 215.0)$.

**Answer.** The interval is $(195.2, 214.8)$ ms, and $200$ ms is not rejected.

**Check.** The two intervals differ by only 0.2 ms at the ends. Slutsky says the normal limit is right as $n \to \infty$, and for $n = 64$ the $t$ correction is already tiny. For small $n$ with non-normal data neither interval is exact (see the simulation).

### Example 3: When Slutsky Does Not Apply

**Problem.** Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Define $X_n = Z$ and $Y_n = -Z$ for every $n$. Both converge in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. What is the limit of $X_n + Y_n$? What would Slutsky (wrongly applied) say?

**Solution.**

1. $X_n + Y_n = Z - Z = 0$ for every $n$. The limit is the constant $0$.
2. If someone treated $Y_n$ as if it were an independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, the sum would be $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$, with variance 2.
3. The constant 0 and $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$ are completely different. Slutsky does not apply, because $Y_n$ does not converge to a **constant**: $Y_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ only.

**Answer.** $X_n + Y_n = 0$, not $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$.

**Check.** The sum of two things that converge in distribution depends on how they are tied together (their joint distribution). Convergence in distribution forgets this, as in [17.1](17.1-Modes-of-Convergence.md), Example 2. Only a constant limit has no joint structure left to forget.

## Simulate It

The code covers the true mean $1$ of $\text{Exp}(1)$ data with three 95% intervals: one that uses the true $\sigma = 1$, one that uses the estimate $S_n$ with $z = 1.96$ (Slutsky), and one with the Student $t$ value.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import t

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 100_000

# Cover the true mean 1 of Exp(1) data with three 95% intervals.
print("    n   known sigma   estimated S (z=1.96)   estimated S (t)")
for n in [5, 10, 30, 100, 500]:
    x = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_trials, n))
    xbar, s = x.mean(axis=1), x.std(axis=1, ddof=1)       # sample mean and sample sd S_n
    known = np.abs(xbar - 1) <= 1.96 * 1.0 / np.sqrt(n)
    z_est = np.abs(xbar - 1) <= 1.96 * s / np.sqrt(n)
    t_est = np.abs(xbar - 1) <= t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * s / np.sqrt(n)
    print(f"{n:>5}   {known.mean():>11.4f}   {z_est.mean():>20.4f}   {t_est.mean():>15.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
    n   known sigma   estimated S (z=1.96)   estimated S (t)
    5        0.9566                 0.8099            0.8816
   10        0.9560                 0.8707            0.9011
   30        0.9526                 0.9176            0.9265
  100        0.9509                 0.9391            0.9421
  500        0.9488                 0.9456            0.9462
```

All three coverages go to $0.95$, as the CLT and Slutsky promise. The intervals with an estimated $S_n$ are worse for small $n$, because $S_n$ is itself random and, for skewed data, it is correlated with $\bar{X}_n$ (a sample with a small mean also tends to have a small spread). The gap closes slowly: at $n = 100$ it is still about $0.01$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using it when $Y_n$ only converges in distribution.** The limit of $Y_n$ must be a constant. Example 3 shows what goes wrong otherwise.
- **Thinking that replacing $\sigma$ by $S_n$ is always exact.** It is exact only in the limit. For small $n$ and skewed data the true coverage is lower than 95% (see the table).
- **Forgetting that the ratio needs $c \ne 0$.** If the denominator tends to 0, the ratio may not converge at all.
- **Applying it to a sum of two random limits.** If both $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{d} Y$, the limit of $X_n + Y_n$ is not $X + Y$ unless the pairs converge jointly (for example if $X_n$ and $Y_n$ are independent).
- **Worrying about $n$ versus $n - 1$ in $S_n^2$.** For the limit it makes no difference: $\frac{n}{n-1} \to 1$. For small samples, $n - 1$ makes $S_n^2$ unbiased.

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics:** z-intervals and z-tests with estimated standard errors, and the large-sample normal approximation for the $t$-statistic, rely on Slutsky ([9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md) for the exact $t$).
- **Machine learning:** confidence intervals for model accuracy, A/B test results and average treatment effects replace unknown variances by sample variances.
- **Delta method:** the proof in [17.7](17.7-Delta-Method.md) uses Slutsky to show that the remainder term vanishes.
- **Estimation theory:** an estimator that is consistent (converges in probability) can be plugged into any asymptotic formula, for example in maximum likelihood theory.
- **Everyday life:** the "margin of error" printed under a poll uses the observed percentage in its formula, not the unknown true one.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $X_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} 3$. What are the limits in distribution of $X_n + Y_n$, $X_n Y_n$ and $\frac{X_n}{Y_n}$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Slutsky: $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) + 3 = \mathcal{N}(3, 1)$. $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} 3 \cdot \mathcal{N}(0, 1) = \mathcal{N}(0, 9)$. $\frac{X_n}{Y_n} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{1}{3}\mathcal{N}(0, 1) = \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{1}{9})$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $X_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y_n = 5 + \frac{W}{\sqrt{n}}$ where $W \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find the limit in distribution of $X_n Y_n$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Y_n \xrightarrow{p} 5$, because $\frac{W}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0$ for every outcome (so also in probability). By Slutsky, $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} 5 \cdot \mathcal{N}(0, 1) = \mathcal{N}(0, 25)$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In a poll of $n = 400$, $\hat{p} = 0.30$. Give a 95% confidence interval for $p$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The estimated standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.3 \times 0.7}{400}} = \sqrt{0.000525} = 0.02291$. The half-width is $1.96 \times 0.02291 = 0.0449$:

$$0.30 \pm 0.0449 = (0.255, \thinspace 0.345)$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that $S_n^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2$ when the $X_i$ are i.i.d. with finite variance. Say which facts you use.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $S_n^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 - \bar{X}_n^2\Big)$. The Weak Law applied to the i.i.d. variables $X_i^2$ (with finite mean $\sigma^2 + \mu^2$) gives $\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2 + \mu^2$. The Weak Law applied to $X_i$ gives $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$, so $\bar{X}_n^2 \xrightarrow{p} \mu^2$ (continuous mapping). Also $\frac{n}{n-1} \to 1$. Combining (limits of sums and products in probability), $S_n^2 \xrightarrow{p} (\sigma^2 + \mu^2) - \mu^2 = \sigma^2$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove: if $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$, then $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$. (This is the lemma used for the product rule.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fix $\varepsilon \gt 0$ and $\delta \gt 0$. Choose $M$ so large that $P(\lvert X \rvert \ge M) \lt \delta$ and that $\pm M$ are continuity points of $F_X$ (possible, since $P(\lvert X \rvert \ge M) \to 0$ as $M \to \infty$ and all but countably many $M$ are good). If $\lvert X_n Y_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon$, then either $\lvert X_n \rvert \gt M$ or $\lvert Y_n \rvert \gt \frac{\varepsilon}{M}$. So

$$P\big(\lvert X_n Y_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon\big) \le P\big(\lvert X_n \rvert \gt M\big) + P\big(\lvert Y_n \rvert \gt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{M}\big)$$

As $n \to \infty$, the first term tends to $P(\lvert X \rvert \gt M) \lt \delta$ (convergence in distribution at the continuity points $\pm M$), and the second term tends to 0. So $\limsup P(\lvert X_n Y_n \rvert \gt \varepsilon) \le \delta$ for every $\delta \gt 0$, which means the limit is 0.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** The Wald interval $\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ uses Slutsky. Estimate its coverage for $p = 0.1$ and $n = 20, 100, 400, 2000$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p, n_trials = 0.1, 200_000
print("    n   coverage of p_hat +- 1.96 sqrt(p_hat (1 - p_hat) / n)")
for n in [20, 100, 400, 2000]:
    p_hat = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_trials) / n
    half = 1.96 * np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)     # standard error uses p_hat, not p
    covered = np.abs(p_hat - p) <= half
    print(f"{n:>5}   {covered.mean():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
    n   coverage of p_hat +- 1.96 sqrt(p_hat (1 - p_hat) / n)
   20   0.8765
  100   0.9331
  400   0.9488
 2000   0.9465
```

The coverage approaches $0.95$ as $n$ grows. For $n = 20$ it is only $0.877$, because $\hat{p}$ is often $0$ or very small, which gives a tiny standard error.

</details>

## Summary

- Slutsky: if $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$ (a constant), then $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c$, $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} cX$ and $\frac{X_n}{Y_n} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{X}{c}$ (for $c \ne 0$).
- The condition that $c$ is a constant is essential. Convergence in distribution of both parts is not enough.
- Main use: replace an unknown $\sigma$ by $S_n$. Since $S_n \xrightarrow{p} \sigma$, the studentized mean $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n}$ still tends to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
- It is a large-sample result; for small samples and skewed data the coverage of the resulting intervals is below the nominal level.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\sqrt{n}\thinspace(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)}
$$
