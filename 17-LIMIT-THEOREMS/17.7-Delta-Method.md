# 17.7 Delta Method

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.7 Delta Method

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [17.6 Normal Approximation and Continuity Correction](17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md)

> **Big idea:** If an average is approximately normal, then a smooth function of the average is approximately normal too, and its standard deviation is the old one multiplied by the absolute value of the slope.

---

## Why It Matters

The CLT ([17.5](17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) tells us how an average $\bar{X}_n$ behaves. But in practice we rarely report the raw average. We report its square, its logarithm, a ratio of two averages, an odds, or a perplexity. How uncertain is $g(\bar{X}_n)$? The **delta method** answers this with one derivative. It gives the standard error of almost any estimate that is a smooth function of averages, which makes it one of the most used tools in statistics and in machine learning evaluation.

## Intuition

You measure the radius of a circular plate. Your average radius is 10 mm, with a standard error of 0.1 mm. You compute the area $\pi r^2$. How accurate is the area?

Near $r = 10$, the area function is almost a straight line. If the radius grows by a small amount $d$, the area grows by about $2\pi r \cdot d = 62.8\thinspace d$. So the slope of the function is 62.8 mm. A wobble of 0.1 mm in the radius becomes a wobble of about $62.8 \times 0.1 = 6.3$ square millimeters in the area. The shape of the wobble does not change: a bell curve scaled by a constant is still a bell curve.

That is the whole idea. **Zoom in far enough, and every smooth function is a line.** An average with standard error $s$ lives in a tiny interval around $\mu$ when $n$ is large, so inside that interval $g$ is a line with slope $g'(\mu)$. The standard error is multiplied by $\lvert g'(\mu) \rvert$.

## Picture

```text
  X-bar ~ N(mu, s^2)  ---- apply g ---->  g(X-bar) ~ N(g(mu), g'(mu)^2 s^2)

  Same input wobble s = 0.2, three functions g:

  g(x)       at mu    slope g'(mu)    output sd = |slope| x 0.2
  x^2        3        6               ########################   1.20
  x          any      1               ####                       0.20
  sqrt(x)    4        0.25            #                          0.05

  (one # = 0.05)
```

A steep function (large slope) magnifies the noise, a flat one (small slope) shrinks it. The sign of the slope does not matter, only its size.

## Definition

**Delta method.** Let $Y_n$ be random variables and $\theta$ a number such that

$$
\sqrt{n}\thinspace(Y_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
$$

Let $g$ be a function that has a derivative $g'(\theta)$ at $\theta$, with $g'(\theta) \ne 0$. Then

$$
\sqrt{n}\thinspace\big(g(Y_n) - g(\theta)\big) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\big(0, \thinspace g'(\theta)^2 \sigma^2\big)
$$

where:

- $Y_n$ is usually the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ and $\theta = \mu$, so that $\sigma^2$ is the variance of one value (this is the CLT).
- $g'(\theta)$ is the slope of $g$ at $\theta$.

**In words:** a smooth function of an asymptotically normal estimate is asymptotically normal, centered at $g(\theta)$, and its variance is multiplied by the square of the slope.

**Practical form.** For large $n$, for the sample mean:

$$
g(\bar{X}_n) \approx \mathcal{N}\left(g(\mu), \thinspace \frac{g'(\mu)^2 \sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad \text{standard error} = \frac{\lvert g'(\mu) \rvert \thinspace \sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

In practice $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are replaced by estimates; [17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md) explains why this is allowed.

> **Note:** If $g'(\theta) = 0$ the first-order result says the limit variance is 0, which is true but not informative. Then one needs the next term of the Taylor series: $n\big(g(Y_n) - g(\theta)\big) \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\sigma^2 g''(\theta)}{2} Z^2$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The rate is $n$ instead of $\sqrt{n}$ and the limit is not normal. See Practice 5.

## Why It Is True

The proof is a Taylor expansion, plus the two tools of [17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md) and [17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md).

Define the "slope error" function $h(y) = \dfrac{g(y) - g(\theta)}{y - \theta} - g'(\theta)$ for $y \ne \theta$, and $h(\theta) = 0$. Since $g$ is differentiable at $\theta$, $h(y) \to 0$ as $y \to \theta$, so $h$ is continuous at $\theta$. Solving for $g(y)$:

$$
g(y) - g(\theta) = \big(g'(\theta) + h(y)\big)(y - \theta)
$$

Put $y = Y_n$ and multiply by $\sqrt{n}$:

$$
\sqrt{n}\thinspace\big(g(Y_n) - g(\theta)\big) = g'(\theta) \cdot \sqrt{n}\thinspace(Y_n - \theta) + h(Y_n) \cdot \sqrt{n}\thinspace(Y_n - \theta)
$$

Now look at the two terms.

1. **$Y_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta$.** We have $Y_n - \theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \cdot \sqrt{n}(Y_n - \theta)$. The second factor converges in distribution and the first goes to 0, so $Y_n - \theta \xrightarrow{d} 0$ (this is Slutsky's theorem, [17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md)). A limit in distribution that is a constant is a limit in probability ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)).
2. **$h(Y_n) \xrightarrow{p} 0$.** The function $h$ is continuous at $\theta$, and continuous functions keep convergence in probability ([17.2](17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)). So $h(Y_n) \xrightarrow{p} h(\theta) = 0$.
3. **The second term vanishes.** A factor that goes to 0 in probability times a factor that converges in distribution gives 0 ([17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md), Slutsky): $h(Y_n)\sqrt{n}(Y_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{p} 0$.
4. **The first term is normal.** Multiplying the limit $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ by the constant $g'(\theta)$ gives $\mathcal{N}(0, g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2)$ ([9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)).
5. **Add them.** A sequence that converges in distribution plus a sequence that goes to 0 in probability converges to the same limit (Slutsky again). So the sum converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0, g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2)$.

> **Note:** The delta method is about the spread. It does not say that $E[g(\bar{X}_n)] = g(\mu)$. By Jensen ([16.3](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)) these two can differ. The difference is of order $\frac{1}{n}$, much smaller than the standard error $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, so it is ignored for large $n$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Area of a Circle

**Problem.** A plate has true radius $r = 10$ mm. You measure the radius $n = 25$ times. Each measurement has standard deviation $0.5$ mm. You compute the area from the average radius, $\hat{A} = \pi \bar{R}^2$. What is the standard error of $\hat{A}$?

**Solution.**

1. The standard error of the average radius is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{25}} = 0.1$ mm.
2. The function is $g(r) = \pi r^2$, with slope $g'(r) = 2\pi r$. At $r = 10$: $g'(10) = 2\pi \times 10 = 62.83$.
3. By the delta method the standard error of the area is

$$
\lvert g'(10) \rvert \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 62.83 \times 0.1 = 6.28 \text{ square millimeters}
$$

**Answer.** $\hat{A} \approx \mathcal{N}(314.16, \thinspace 6.28^2)$, since the true area is $\pi \times 100 = 314.16$.

**Check.** The relative error of the area is $\frac{6.28}{314.16} = 0.02$ (2%), exactly twice the relative error of the radius ($\frac{0.1}{10} = 0.01$, or 1%). This matches the rule: for $g(r) = r^2$, relative errors double.

### Example 2: Confidence Interval for a Log-Odds

**Problem.** In an A/B test, a page has true conversion rate $p$. You show it to $n = 2500$ visitors and 20% convert, so $\hat{p} = 0.2$. The **odds** are $\frac{p}{1-p}$ and the **log-odds** are $\ln\frac{p}{1-p}$. Find a 95% confidence interval for the log-odds, and then for $p$.

**Solution.**

1. By the CLT, $\hat{p}$ has standard error $\sqrt{\frac{0.2 \times 0.8}{2500}} = \sqrt{0.000064} = 0.008$.
2. The function is $g(p) = \ln\frac{p}{1-p}$. Its derivative is $g'(p) = \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{1-p} = \frac{1}{p(1-p)}$. At $p = 0.2$: $g'(0.2) = \frac{1}{0.16} = 6.25$.
3. Delta method: the standard error of the log-odds is $6.25 \times 0.008 = 0.05$. (This equals $\frac{1}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{400}} = 0.05$.)
4. The estimate is $g(0.2) = \ln(0.25) = -1.3863$. The 95% interval is $-1.3863 \pm 1.96 \times 0.05 = (-1.4843, \thinspace -1.2883)$.
5. Transform back: the odds are $e^{-1.4843} = 0.2267$ and $e^{-1.2883} = 0.2757$, and $p = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}}$ gives $(0.1848, \thinspace 0.2161)$.

**Answer.** The log-odds interval is $(-1.484, -1.288)$, which means $p$ is between $0.185$ and $0.216$.

**Check.** The plain interval $0.2 \pm 1.96 \times 0.008$ is $(0.1843, 0.2157)$. The two agree to about 0.001 because $n$ is large. The log-odds version never leaves $(0, 1)$, which matters when $p$ is near 0 or 1. The simulation below finds a standard deviation of $0.0501$ for the log-odds, against the delta value $0.05$.

### Example 3: Estimating the Rate of Exponential Waiting Times

**Problem.** Waiting times are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ with true rate $\lambda = 2$ (so the mean is $\frac{1}{\lambda} = 0.5$ and the standard deviation is also $0.5$). You estimate the rate by $\hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\bar{X}_n}$ from $n = 100$ waiting times. How accurate is $\hat{\lambda}$?

**Solution.**

1. $\bar{X}_{100}$ has mean $0.5$ and standard error $\frac{0.5}{\sqrt{100}} = 0.05$.
2. The function is $g(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ with $g'(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2}$. At $x = 0.5$: $g'(0.5) = -4$.
3. The standard error of $\hat{\lambda}$ is $\lvert -4 \rvert \times 0.05 = 0.2$.
4. In general, $g'(\mu) = -\lambda^2$ and $\sigma = \frac{1}{\lambda}$, so the standard error is $\lambda^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}$. For $\lambda = 2$ and $n = 100$ it is $0.2$ again.

**Answer.** $\hat{\lambda} \approx \mathcal{N}(2, 0.2^2)$. The standard error is $0.2$, which is 10% of the true rate, and the estimate is almost always within $2 \times 0.2 = 0.4$ of it.

**Check.** The relative error of $\hat{\lambda}$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} = 0.1$ (10%) in standard deviation, the same as the relative error of the mean $\bar{X}_n$ (since $\sigma = \mu$ for the exponential). The simulation finds $0.2040$, slightly larger than $0.2$, because $\frac{1}{\bar{X}_n}$ is skewed at $n = 100$ (the exact standard deviation is $\frac{n\lambda}{(n-1)\sqrt{n-2}} = 0.2041$).

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks Example 3, Part 2 checks Example 2, and Part 3 shows the square-root transform of a Poisson count, where $g(y) = \sqrt{y}$ and $g'(\lambda) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}$ give a variance of $\frac{\lambda}{4\lambda} = \frac{1}{4}$ whatever $\lambda$ is.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs = 200_000

# 1. Estimate the rate of an Exp(2) variable by 1/mean of n = 100 draws.
lam, n = 2.0, 100
xbar = rng.exponential(1 / lam, size=(n_runs, n)).mean(axis=1)
rate_hat = 1 / xbar
print(f"Rate 1/xbar:  sd simulated {rate_hat.std():.4f}, delta method {lam / np.sqrt(n):.4f}")

# 2. Log-odds of a conversion rate p = 0.2 from n = 2500 visitors.
p, n = 0.2, 2500
p_hat = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_runs) / n
log_odds = np.log(p_hat / (1 - p_hat))
print(f"Log-odds:     sd simulated {log_odds.std():.4f}, delta method {1 / np.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p)):.4f}")

# 3. Square root of a Poisson count has variance close to 1/4, whatever lambda is.
for lam in [4, 25, 100, 400]:
    y = rng.poisson(lam, size=n_runs)
    print(f"Poisson({lam:>3}): variance of sqrt(Y) = {np.sqrt(y).var():.4f}   (delta method 0.2500)")
```

Output:

```text
Rate 1/xbar:  sd simulated 0.2040, delta method 0.2000
Log-odds:     sd simulated 0.0501, delta method 0.0500
Poisson(  4): variance of sqrt(Y) = 0.3059   (delta method 0.2500)
Poisson( 25): variance of sqrt(Y) = 0.2542   (delta method 0.2500)
Poisson(100): variance of sqrt(Y) = 0.2515   (delta method 0.2500)
Poisson(400): variance of sqrt(Y) = 0.2496   (delta method 0.2500)
```

The delta method is accurate for large $n$ and large $\lambda$. For $\lambda = 4$ the Poisson count is too small and skewed, and the variance of $\sqrt{Y}$ is $0.306$ instead of $0.25$. The delta method is a large-sample tool.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to square the slope in the variance.** The variance is multiplied by $g'(\mu)^2$, the standard deviation by $\lvert g'(\mu) \rvert$. Mixing them gives wrong numbers.
- **Forgetting the $\sqrt{n}$.** The standard error of $g(\bar{X}_n)$ is $\frac{\lvert g'(\mu) \rvert \sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, not $\lvert g'(\mu) \rvert \sigma$.
- **Using it when the slope is zero.** If $g'(\mu) = 0$ (for example $g(x) = x^2$ at $\mu = 0$) the result is a squared normal, not a normal (Practice 5).
- **Using it for a small sample or a very curved function.** The line is a good model of $g$ only if the interval where $\bar{X}_n$ lives is short. The Poisson case with $\lambda = 4$ above shows what happens when it is not.
- **Thinking that $E[g(\bar{X}_n)] = g(\mu)$.** The delta method describes the spread. The mean of $g(\bar{X}_n)$ is slightly shifted (Jensen), by an amount of order $\frac{1}{n}$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Machine learning evaluation:** perplexity is $e^{\text{mean negative log-likelihood}}$. A standard error for the perplexity is the perplexity times the standard error of the mean log-loss (Practice 3). The same works for any metric that is a smooth function of averages, such as an F-score.
- **Statistics:** standard errors of ratios, log risk ratios, log-odds and odds ratios in medicine and A/B tests (Example 2). Fisher's $z$-transform of a correlation is a variance-stabilizing application.
- **Variance-stabilizing transforms:** for Poisson counts $\sqrt{y}$ has constant variance $\frac{1}{4}$. For proportions, $2\operatorname{arcsin}\sqrt{\hat{p}}$ has variance $\frac{1}{n}$ (Practice 4). These make data with changing spread easier to model.
- **Engineering and physics:** error propagation. If a quantity is computed from measured values (area from radius, speed from distance and time), the delta method gives its uncertainty (Example 1).
- **Estimation theory:** maximum likelihood estimates are asymptotically normal, and any smooth function of the parameters inherits this by the delta method.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** The sample mean of $n = 100$ values has $\mu = 4$ and $\sigma = 2$. Find the approximate standard error of $\sqrt{\bar{X}_n}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard error of $\bar{X}_n$ is $\frac{2}{10} = 0.2$. For $g(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $g'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, and $g'(4) = \frac{1}{4}$:

$$\text{SE}\big(\sqrt{\bar{X}_n}\big) \approx \tfrac{1}{4} \times 0.2 = 0.05$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** The sample mean of $n = 25$ values has $\mu = 3$ and $\sigma = 1$. Find the approximate standard error of $\bar{X}_n^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard error of $\bar{X}_n$ is $\frac{1}{5} = 0.2$. For $g(x) = x^2$, $g'(3) = 6$:

$$\text{SE}\big(\bar{X}_n^2\big) \approx 6 \times 0.2 = 1.2$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A language model has an average negative log-likelihood of $2.30$ nats per token on $n = 10{,}000$ test tokens, and the per-token standard deviation is $1.5$. Its perplexity is $e^{2.30}$. Find the perplexity and its 95% confidence interval by the delta method.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The perplexity is $e^{2.30} = 9.974$. The standard error of the mean is $\frac{1.5}{\sqrt{10000}} = 0.015$. For $g(m) = e^m$, $g'(m) = e^m$, so

$$\text{SE}(\text{perplexity}) \approx 9.974 \times 0.015 = 0.150$$

The 95% interval is $9.974 \pm 1.96 \times 0.150 = (9.68, \thinspace 10.27)$. (Applying $e^{\cdot}$ to the interval $2.30 \pm 1.96 \times 0.015$ gives $(9.685, 10.272)$, nearly the same.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $\hat{p}$ be a sample proportion from $n$ trials. Show that $g(p) = 2\operatorname{arcsin}\sqrt{p}$ stabilizes the variance: the standard error of $g(\hat{p})$ is about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ for every $p$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the chain rule, $g'(p) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - p}} \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{p}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}$. The variance of $\hat{p}$ is $\frac{p(1-p)}{n}$, so

$$\operatorname{Var}\big(g(\hat{p})\big) \approx g'(p)^2 \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \frac{1}{p(1-p)} \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \frac{1}{n}$$

It does not depend on $p$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $\hat{p}$ be the fraction of heads in $n$ flips of a fair coin and $g(p) = p(1-p)$. Here $g'(\frac{1}{2}) = 0$. Show that $n\big(g(\hat{p}) - \frac{1}{4}\big) \xrightarrow{d} -\frac{1}{4}Z^2$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand: $\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) - \frac{1}{4} = -\big(\hat{p}^2 - \hat{p} + \frac{1}{4}\big) = -\big(\hat{p} - \frac{1}{2}\big)^2$. So

$$n\big(g(\hat{p}) - \tfrac{1}{4}\big) = -\Big(\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{p} - \tfrac{1}{2}\big)\Big)^2$$

By the CLT, $\sqrt{n}(\hat{p} - \frac{1}{2}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{1}{4})$, which is the distribution of $\frac{1}{2}Z$. Squaring is a continuous function, so the square converges in distribution to $\frac{1}{4}Z^2$, and with the minus sign we get $-\frac{1}{4}Z^2$. This matches the note in the Definition: $\frac{\sigma^2 g''(\theta)}{2}Z^2 = \frac{(1/4)(-2)}{2}Z^2 = -\frac{1}{4}Z^2$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 with $n = 400$ and $p = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.9$: simulate $n \cdot \operatorname{Var}(\hat{p})$ and $n \cdot \operatorname{Var}\big(2\operatorname{arcsin}\sqrt{\hat{p}}\big)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, n_runs = 400, 200_000
print("   p   n*Var(p_hat)   n*Var(2 arcsin sqrt(p_hat))")
for p in [0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.9]:
    p_hat = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_runs) / n
    stabilized = 2 * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(p_hat))
    print(f"{p:>4}   {n * p_hat.var():>12.4f}   {n * stabilized.var():>26.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
   p   n*Var(p_hat)   n*Var(2 arcsin sqrt(p_hat))
 0.1         0.0900                       1.0088
 0.3         0.2103                       1.0047
 0.5         0.2492                       0.9993
 0.9         0.0895                       1.0041
```

The raw variance changes with $p$ ($p(1-p)$ is $0.09$, $0.21$, $0.25$, $0.09$), while the stabilized variance is close to 1 for all four.

</details>

## Summary

- If $\sqrt{n}(Y_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ and $g'(\theta) \ne 0$, then $\sqrt{n}(g(Y_n) - g(\theta)) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2)$.
- In practice, $g(\bar{X}_n)$ has standard error $\frac{\lvert g'(\mu) \rvert \sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$: the standard error is multiplied by the size of the slope.
- The proof is a first-order Taylor expansion, with the remainder going to 0 in probability.
- It fails or changes when $g'(\theta) = 0$ (the limit is then a squared normal), and it is a large-sample approximation.
- Typical uses: log-odds, ratios, perplexity, error propagation and variance-stabilizing transforms.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{SE}\big(g(\bar{X}_n)\big) \approx \lvert g'(\mu) \rvert \thinspace \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}
$$
