# 17.5 Central Limit Theorem

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 17: Limit Theorems](README.md) / 17.5 Central Limit Theorem

**Level:** 4 (Big Theorems) · **Time:** about 70 minutes · **Before this:** [17.4 Strong Law of Large Numbers](17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)

> **Big idea:** Add up many independent random pieces, and the total looks like a bell curve, whatever the shape of one piece; so a sum or an average of $n$ values is approximately normal with mean $n\mu$ (or $\mu$) and spread $\sigma\sqrt{n}$ (or $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$).

---

## Why It Matters

The Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md), [17.4](17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) says the average settles at $\mu$. It does not say how far off it is at a given $n$, or what the error looks like. The **Central Limit Theorem** (CLT) answers both: the error is of size $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ and has a **normal** shape. This is why the normal distribution ([9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)) is everywhere: heights, measurement errors and exam scores are all sums of many small effects. It is also the reason that polls have a "margin of error", that A/B tests work, and that error bars on Monte Carlo results can be trusted.

## Intuition

Roll one die. All six faces are equally likely: a flat shape. Roll two dice and add. Now 7 is the most likely sum, because 6 pairs give it, while 2 and 12 need one pair each. The shape is a triangle. Roll five dice. To get a total near the minimum (5) all five dice must show low numbers. To get a total near 17 or 18, a huge number of mixtures work. So the middle wins by a large margin. With 30 dice, the shape is a smooth bell.

The reason is always the same. Extreme totals need **every** piece to push in the same direction, which is rare. Middle totals can be made in very many ways, because the pushes cancel. This does not depend on how one die looks. Replace the die by an exponential waiting time, a coin flip or a skewed price change: the sum still becomes bell-shaped.

Here is a second way to see it. Add two independent standard normal variables and divide by $\sqrt{2}$. The result is again a standard normal. So the normal shape is **stable** under "add and rescale". The CLT says that adding and rescaling any finite-variance variables pulls the shape toward this one stable shape.

## Picture

```text
  ##################         #              ###              ###
  ##################        ###             ###              ###
  ##################       #####            ###              ###
  ##################       #####           #####            #####
  ##################      #######          #####            #####
  ##################      #######          #####            #####
  ##################     #########        #######          #######
  ##################    ###########       #######          #######
  ##################    ###########      #########        #########
  ------------------    -----------    -------------    -------------
  n = 1 (one die)       n = 2          n = 5            n = 30

  gap 0.143             gap 0.083      gap 0.052        gap 0.021
```

One die is flat, two dice give a triangle, and from five dice on the picture is already a bell. The last line is measured exactly. The gap is the largest distance between the CDF of the standardized sum and the normal CDF, and it shrinks as $n$ grows.

## Definition

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, where $0 \lt \sigma^2 \lt \infty$. Let $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ be their sum and $\bar{X}_n = \frac{S_n}{n}$ their average. Define the **standardized sum**

$$
Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\sqrt{n}\thinspace(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma}
$$

**Central Limit Theorem (Lindeberg-Levy).** For every number $z$,

$$
P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. In symbols, $Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

where:

- $n\mu$ and $\sigma\sqrt{n}$ are the mean and the standard deviation of the sum $S_n$. So $Z_n$ has mean 0 and variance 1 for every $n$.
- $\sigma$ must be finite and positive. Nothing else is assumed about the shape of $X_i$.

**In words:** subtract the mean of the sum, divide by its standard deviation, and the result has a distribution that gets closer and closer to the standard bell curve.

**Practical forms.** For large $n$ we use the limit as an approximation:

| Quantity | Approximately distributed as |
|---|---|
| Sum $S_n$ | $\mathcal{N}(n\mu, \thinspace n\sigma^2)$ |
| Average $\bar{X}_n$ | $\mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \thinspace \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$ |
| Standardized sum $Z_n$ | $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ |

The second row says: the average has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, called the **standard error**. Here the second parameter of $\mathcal{N}$ is the variance, as always in this book.

## Why It Is True

### The Idea of the Proof

The full six-step proof with moment generating functions is Example 3 of [15.4 Uniqueness Theorem](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md). We do not repeat the algebra. Here is the idea in the language of this topic.

1. Standardize each piece: $Y_i = \frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma}$ has mean 0 and variance 1. Then $Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n)$.
2. The MGF of a sum of independent pieces is a product, so $M_{Z_n}(t) = \left[M_Y\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n$.
3. For small $s$, the MGF of $Y$ looks like $M_Y(s) \approx 1 + \frac{s^2}{2}$. Only the mean (0) and the variance (1) appear in this expansion. **All other details of the distribution are in smaller terms**, and they vanish in the limit. This is the heart of the CLT.
4. With $s = \frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}$ we get $M_{Z_n}(t) \approx \left(1 + \frac{t^2}{2n}\right)^n \to e^{t^2/2}$.
5. $e^{t^2/2}$ is the MGF of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and the continuity theorem ([15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)) turns convergence of MGFs into convergence of distributions.

The proof above needs the MGF to exist. The same five steps work with the characteristic function ([15.6](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)), which always exists. Then only $\sigma^2 \lt \infty$ is needed, and this is the theorem as stated.

### Why Only the Mean and the Variance Survive

Cumulants ([15.7](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.7-Cumulants.md)) show the same thing in one line. Cumulants of independent variables add, and $\kappa_k(aY) = a^k \kappa_k(Y)$. So for $Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum Y_i$:

$$
\kappa_k(Z_n) = n \cdot \frac{\kappa_k(Y)}{n^{k/2}} = \kappa_k(Y) \thinspace n^{1 - k/2}
$$

For $k = 1$ this is 0 and for $k = 2$ it is 1, for every $n$. For $k \ge 3$ the power $n^{1 - k/2}$ goes to 0. In particular, the skewness of $Z_n$ is $\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}$ and the excess kurtosis is $\frac{\kappa_4}{n}$, where $\gamma$ and $\kappa_4$ belong to one piece. The normal distribution is exactly the one whose cumulants of order 3 and higher are zero.

### How Fast Is It? Berry-Esseen

The CLT is a limit, so it does not say how good the approximation is at a given $n$. The **Berry-Esseen theorem** does. Let $\rho = E\lvert X - \mu \rvert^3$ be finite. Then for every $n$:

$$
\sup_z \big\lvert P(Z_n \le z) - \Phi(z) \big\rvert \le \frac{C \thinspace \rho}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{n}}
$$

where $C$ is a constant that is known to be below $0.48$ (and above $0.40$). We use it without proof.

**In words:** the largest error in the CDF shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, and it is proportional to a "skew" ratio $\frac{\rho}{\sigma^3}$. A skewed or heavy-tailed piece (large $\rho$) needs more terms than a symmetric one.

The bound is safe but not tight. For an $\text{Exp}(1)$ piece, $\rho = \frac{12}{e} - 2 \approx 2.415$ and $\sigma = 1$. The next table compares the bound (with $C = 0.4748$) with the exact largest error, computed from the Gamma distribution of $S_n$.

| $n$ | 1 | 2 | 5 | 30 | 100 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Berry-Esseen bound | $1.146$ | $0.811$ | $0.513$ | $0.209$ | $0.115$ | $0.036$ |
| Exact largest error | $0.1587$ | $0.0945$ | $0.0596$ | $0.0243$ | $0.0133$ | $0.0042$ |

The real error is about 9 times smaller than the bound, but it follows the same $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ law. The reason is that the first correction to the normal CDF is proportional to the skewness $\gamma$ of one piece. A rule of thumb is that the largest error is about $\frac{0.0665\thinspace\lvert\gamma\rvert}{\sqrt{n}}$ (this is the leading term of the Edgeworth expansion, which we do not prove). For $\text{Exp}(1)$, $\gamma = 2$ and the rule gives $\frac{0.133}{\sqrt{n}}$, which matches the table row (for $n = 30$: $0.0243$). Symmetric pieces ($\gamma = 0$) converge faster. This is why the rule $n \ge 30$ is a guess and not a law.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Average of 100 Die Rolls

**Problem.** You roll a fair die 100 times. Use the CLT to estimate the chance that the **total** is more than 370.

**Solution.**

1. One roll has $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma^2 = \frac{35}{12} = 2.9167$ ([17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).
2. The total $S_{100}$ has mean $100 \times 3.5 = 350$ and variance $100 \times 2.9167 = 291.67$, so standard deviation $\sqrt{291.67} = 17.078$.
3. Standardize the threshold 370:

$$
z = \frac{370 - 350}{17.078} = 1.171
$$

4. By the CLT, $P(S_{100} \gt 370) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.171) = 1 - 0.8792 = 0.1208$.

**Answer.** About $0.121$.

**Check.** The exact value (adding up the probabilities of all totals, by repeated convolution of the die distribution) is $P(S_{100} \ge 371) = 0.1152$. The CLT is off by about $0.006$. Part of this error comes from using a smooth curve for a total that takes only whole numbers. Using $370.5$ instead of $370$ (the continuity correction, [17.6](17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md)) gives $z = 1.200$ and $0.1150$, much closer.

### Example 2: Server Response Times

**Problem.** A web server takes on average 200 ms per request, with standard deviation 80 ms. The times are skewed (many fast requests, a few very slow ones), so they are **not** normal. (a) What is the chance that the average of 400 requests exceeds 210 ms? (b) How many requests are needed so that the average is within 5 ms of the true mean with probability 95%?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The standard error of the average is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{80}{\sqrt{400}} = \frac{80}{20} = 4$ ms. By the CLT, $\bar{X}_{400} \approx \mathcal{N}(200, 4^2)$.
2. The threshold 210 is $z = \frac{210 - 200}{4} = 2.5$ standard errors above the mean:

$$
P(\bar{X}_{400} \gt 210) \approx 1 - \Phi(2.5) = 0.0062
$$

3. (b) We want $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \le 5) \ge 0.95$. For a normal, 95% lies within 1.96 standard errors, so we need $1.96 \cdot \frac{80}{\sqrt{n}} \le 5$:

$$
\sqrt{n} \ge \frac{1.96 \times 80}{5} = 31.36 \implies n \ge 983.4
$$

**Answer.** (a) About $0.0062$. (b) $n = 984$ requests.

**Check.** If the times follow a Gamma distribution with the same mean and standard deviation (shape $6.25$, scale $32$), the exact answer to (a) is $0.0068$, close to $0.0062$. Note that the individual times are far from normal, yet the average of 400 of them is nearly normal.

### Example 3: Averaging a Skewed Variable

**Problem.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_{30}$ be i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1, so mean 1 and standard deviation 1). Estimate $P(\bar{X}_{30} \gt 1.3)$ with the CLT, and compare with the exact value.

**Solution.**

1. The standard error is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{30}} = 0.1826$. The threshold $1.3$ is $z = \frac{1.3 - 1}{0.1826} = 1.643$ standard errors above the mean.
2. CLT: $P(\bar{X}_{30} \gt 1.3) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.643) = 0.0502$.
3. Exactly, $S_{30} = 30\bar{X}_{30}$ has a $\text{Gamma}(30, 1)$ distribution ([9.4](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md)). The event is $S_{30} \gt 39$, and `scipy.stats.gamma.sf(39, 30)` gives $0.0591$.

**Answer.** The CLT says $0.050$, the exact value is $0.059$.

**Check.** The CLT underestimates the right tail by about 15%. The exponential has skewness 2, and the skewness of the average of 30 is $\frac{2}{\sqrt{30}} = 0.365$, so the right tail is still heavier than the normal one. The simulation below shows the same pattern at every $n$: the left tail is too light and the right tail too heavy, until the skewness has died away.

## Simulate It

The code averages $n$ draws from $\text{Exp}(1)$, standardizes, and compares three probabilities and the skewness with the standard normal values.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, skew

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# Average n draws from Exp(1) (mean 1, sd 1), standardize, and compare with N(0, 1).
print("    n   P(Z<=-1.96)   P(Z<=0)   P(Z<=1.96)   skewness (2/sqrt(n))")
for n in [1, 2, 5, 30, 100]:
    xbar = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_trials, n)).mean(axis=1)
    z = (xbar - 1.0) / (1.0 / np.sqrt(n))        # sqrt(n) (xbar - mu) / sigma
    print(f"{n:>5}   {np.mean(z <= -1.96):>11.4f}   {np.mean(z <= 0):>7.4f}   "
          f"{np.mean(z <= 1.96):>10.4f}   {skew(z):>6.3f} ({2 / np.sqrt(n):.3f})")
print(f"normal  {norm.cdf(-1.96):>11.4f}   {0.5:>7.4f}   {norm.cdf(1.96):>10.4f}   {0:>6.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
    n   P(Z<=-1.96)   P(Z<=0)   P(Z<=1.96)   skewness (2/sqrt(n))
    1        0.0000    0.6330       0.9485    2.017 (2.000)
    2        0.0000    0.5942       0.9523    1.418 (1.414)
    5        0.0004    0.5586       0.9570    0.885 (0.894)
   30        0.0143    0.5236       0.9661    0.366 (0.365)
  100        0.0196    0.5137       0.9699    0.198 (0.200)
normal       0.0250    0.5000       0.9750    0.000
```

Every column moves toward the normal row as $n$ grows. The skewness follows $\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}$ exactly, as the cumulant argument predicts. Even at $n = 100$ the left tail has $0.0196$ instead of $0.025$: the CLT is about the middle of the distribution, and the tails need more terms.

## Common Mistakes

- **Saying that the data become normal.** The CLT says the **sum** or the **average** becomes normal. A single value $X_i$ keeps its own distribution, however many you collect. A histogram of 10,000 exponential values is still skewed.
- **Using $\sigma$ instead of $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$.** The average has standard deviation $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, the sum has $\sigma\sqrt{n}$. Ask first: "am I asked about a sum or an average?"
- **Believing that $n = 30$ is always enough.** For a symmetric distribution $n = 5$ may be fine. For a very skewed one (the exponential, income, file sizes) $n = 30$ still leaves a 15% error in the tail, as in Example 3.
- **Trusting the far tails.** Berry-Esseen bounds the error in absolute terms ($\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$). A probability like $10^{-6}$ can be wrong by a factor of 10 or more. For tails use Chernoff or Hoeffding bounds ([16.6](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md), [16.7](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)).
- **Forgetting the assumptions.** The pieces must be independent (or weakly dependent) and have a finite variance. For the Cauchy distribution the theorem is false ([17.9](17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics and polls:** confidence intervals and the margin of error are $\bar{X}_n \pm 1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ ([17.8](17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md) explains what to do when $\sigma$ is unknown). A/B tests compare two averages with a z-test.
- **Machine learning:** the noise in a minibatch gradient is an average of many per-example gradients, so it is approximately Gaussian with variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{\text{batch size}}$. This is the basis of many models of SGD. Sums of many weights times inputs are also Gaussian, which is why careful weight initialization keeps activations at a stable scale.
- **Simulation and Monte Carlo:** error bars of a Monte Carlo estimate come from the CLT: $\pm 1.96 \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}$ ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)).
- **Physics and finance:** a particle that takes many small random steps ends up with a normal position (diffusion, [18.8](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.8-Brownian-Motion.md)). Daily returns add up to monthly returns.
- **Everyday life:** measurement errors, exam scores and heights are bell-shaped because each is the sum of many small independent effects.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A population has mean 100 and standard deviation 12. For a random sample of $n = 36$, find the approximate probability that the sample mean exceeds 104.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard error is $\frac{12}{\sqrt{36}} = 2$, and $z = \frac{104 - 100}{2} = 2$:

$$P(\bar{X}_{36} \gt 104) \approx 1 - \Phi(2) = 0.0228$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You roll a fair die 100 times. Estimate the probability that the average roll is between $3.3$ and $3.7$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean of the average is $3.5$ and the standard error is $\frac{\sqrt{35/12}}{10} = 0.1708$. The limits are $\pm 0.2$ from the mean, so $z = \pm\frac{0.2}{0.1708} = \pm 1.171$:

$$P(3.3 \lt \bar{X}_{100} \lt 3.7) \approx 2\Phi(1.171) - 1 = 2(0.8792) - 1 = 0.7584$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Measurements have standard deviation $\sigma = 20$. How many independent measurements do you need so that the average is within $2$ units of the true mean with probability about 95%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need $1.96 \cdot \frac{20}{\sqrt{n}} \le 2$, so $\sqrt{n} \ge \frac{1.96 \times 20}{2} = 19.6$ and $n \ge 384.2$. Take $n = 385$. (Chebyshev, [17.3](17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md), would ask for $n \ge \frac{400}{4 \times 0.05} = 2000$.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $S = U_1 + \cdots + U_{12}$ where the $U_i$ are independent uniform on $[0, 1]$. Find the mean and variance of $S$, and use the CLT to estimate $P(S - 6 \gt 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each $U_i$ has mean $\frac{1}{2}$ and variance $\frac{1}{12}$. So $E[S] = 12 \times \frac{1}{2} = 6$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 12 \times \frac{1}{12} = 1$. Thus $S - 6$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and

$$P(S - 6 \gt 1) \approx 1 - \Phi(1) = 0.1587$$

The exact value is $0.1607$. (This trick was used for years to generate normal random numbers.)

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $Z_n \xrightarrow{d} Z$ for the standardized sums of i.i.d. variables. Show that $Z$ must satisfy: if $Z'$ is an independent copy of $Z$, then $\frac{Z + Z'}{\sqrt{2}}$ has the same distribution as $Z$. (So the limit is stable, and the standard normal has this property.) Check that $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ has it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Split the first $2n$ variables into two groups of $n$: $S_{2n} - 2n\mu = (S_n - n\mu) + (S'_n - n\mu)$, where $S'_n$ is the sum of the next $n$ variables and is independent of $S_n$ with the same distribution. Dividing by $\sigma\sqrt{2n}$:

$$Z_{2n} = \frac{Z_n + Z'_n}{\sqrt{2}}$$

where $Z_n$ and $Z'_n$ are independent with the same distribution. As $n \to \infty$ the left side tends in distribution to $Z$, and the right side to $\frac{Z + Z'}{\sqrt{2}}$ (the two independent pieces converge separately). So the two have the same distribution.

For $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$: by independence, $\frac{Z + Z'}{\sqrt{2}}$ has mean 0 and variance $\frac{1 + 1}{2} = 1$ and is normal (a sum of independent normals is normal, [13.5](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md)). So it is $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate $S - 6$ from Problem 4 two million times. Compare $P(\lvert S - 6 \rvert \gt k)$ for $k = 2, 3, 4$ with the exact values and with the normal approximation. What happens in the tails?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import irwinhall, norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
s = rng.random((2_000_000, 12)).sum(axis=1) - 6          # sum of 12 uniforms, centered
print("  k   simulation   exact (Irwin-Hall)   normal")
for k in [2, 3, 4]:
    exact = irwinhall(12).sf(6 + k) + irwinhall(12).cdf(6 - k)
    print(f"  {k}   {np.mean(np.abs(s) > k):.6f}      {exact:.6f}          {2 * norm.sf(k):.6f}")
```

Output:

```text
  k   simulation   exact (Irwin-Hall)   normal
  2   0.044480      0.044551          0.045500
  3   0.001992      0.002014          0.002700
  4   0.000011      0.000017          0.000063
```

At $k = 2$ the normal curve is within about 2% of the exact value. At $k = 4$ the normal overestimates the tail by a factor of 3.7 (the simulated value is noisy, since only about 20 of the 2 million samples reach it), because $S - 6$ can never exceed 6 in size. The CLT is accurate in the middle and weaker in the tails.

</details>

## Summary

- The CLT: for i.i.d. values with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, the standardized sum $Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
- In practice: $\bar{X}_n \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$ and $S_n \approx \mathcal{N}(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$. The standard error of the average is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$.
- Why it works: only the mean and variance of one piece survive. The cumulants of order 3 and higher shrink like $n^{1-k/2}$.
- How fast: the error in the CDF is at most $\frac{C\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}}$ (Berry-Esseen), and it is about $\frac{0.0665\thinspace\lvert\gamma\rvert}{\sqrt{n}}$ in practice. Skewed data need more terms.
- It describes the middle of the distribution well and the far tails poorly. The full MGF proof is in [15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\sqrt{n}\thinspace(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)}
$$
