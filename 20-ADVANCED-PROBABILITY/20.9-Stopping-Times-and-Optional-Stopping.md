# 20.9 Stopping Times and Optional Stopping

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.9 Stopping Times and Optional Stopping

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 70 minutes · **Before this:** [20.8 Martingales](20.8-Martingales.md)

> **Big idea:** A stopping time is a rule for when to stop that uses only the past, and the optional stopping theorem says that, under mild conditions, stopping a fair game by such a rule leaves the average fortune unchanged, which proves in a few lines that the gambler's ruin probability is $\frac{k}{N}$.

---

## Why It Matters

In real games you do not play a fixed number of rounds. You stop when you are ahead by 10 taka, or when you are broke. A random number of rounds changes the sums: $E[S_T]$ is not $E[T]$ times the mean unless $T$ is a proper stopping rule. This topic keeps three promises. It re-derives the gambler's ruin probabilities and the expected duration ([18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md)) in a few lines. It proves **Wald's identity**, which fixes the random sum of [14.7](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.7-Random-Sums.md) when the number of terms is a stopping time. And it shows why "stop when you are ahead" does not give an edge in a fair game, but also why this fails with unlimited credit.

## Intuition

You play the fair coin game, starting with 3 taka, and use the rule: stop when you reach 10 taka or 0 taka. To decide whether to stop after round $n$, you only look at your fortune up to round $n$. That is a good rule: it does not need the future.

A bad rule is: stop at the moment your fortune is at its highest. Nobody can follow this rule, because to know that the present is the highest point you need to see the future. A **stopping time** is a rule of the first kind.

Now the surprise. In a fair game, your expected fortune is 3 taka at every fixed time. Does it stay 3 at the random time you stop? If you stop at 0 or 10, then

$$
3 = E[\text{fortune at stop}] = 10 \cdot P(\text{reach } 10) + 0 \cdot P(\text{reach } 0)
$$

and so $P(\text{reach } 10) = \frac{3}{10}$. This one line is the gambler's ruin formula. The idea works when the stopping rule is reasonable. It fails for the rule "stop when you are ahead by 1 taka": then you are sure to win 1 taka, so the average fortune at the stop is 4, not 3. The theorem tells us exactly when the idea is safe.

The decision "stop now" at time $n$ depends only on the path up to time $n$. After the stop, the fortune is frozen at the exit value.

## Definition

Let $(\mathcal{F}_n)$ be a filtration ([20.8](20.8-Martingales.md)). A random time $T$ with values in $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace \cup \lbrace \infty \rbrace$ is a **stopping time** if

$$
\lbrace T = n \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}_n \quad \text{for every } n
$$

**In words:** whether you stop exactly at time $n$ can be decided from the information available at time $n$.

For a process $(M_n)$, the **stopped value** is $M_T$ (the value at the stopping time) and the **stopped process** is $M_{T \wedge n}$, where $T \wedge n = \min(T, n)$. The stopped process follows $M$ until time $T$ and then stays frozen.

Examples:

| Rule | Stopping time? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| first $n$ with $M_n \in A$ (a **hitting time**) | yes | $\lbrace T = n \rbrace$ says $M_0, \dots, M_{n-1} \notin A$ and $M_n \in A$: all known at time $n$ |
| first time the fortune reaches 0 or $N$ | yes | a hitting time of $\lbrace 0, N \rbrace$ |
| a fixed time $n_0$ | yes | known in advance |
| the time of the maximum of $M_0, \dots, M_{100}$ | no | you must see all 100 values to know it |
| one round before the first time the fortune hits 0 | no | you would have to know that ruin comes next |

**Theorem 1 (the stopped process is a martingale).** If $(M_n)$ is a martingale and $T$ is a stopping time, then $(M_{T \wedge n})_n$ is a martingale. In particular $E[M_{T \wedge n}] = E[M_0]$ for every $n$.

**Theorem 2 (optional stopping).** Let $(M_n)$ be a martingale and $T$ a stopping time with $T \lt \infty$ almost surely. Then $E[M_T] = E[M_0]$ if at least one of these holds:

- **(a)** $T \le K$ for some constant $K$;
- **(b)** the stopped process is bounded: $\lvert M_{T \wedge n} \rvert \le C$ for all $n$;
- **(c)** $E[T] \lt \infty$ and the steps are bounded: $\lvert M_{n+1} - M_n \rvert \le c$ for all $n$.

**In words:** if you stop a fair game at a stopping time, the mean fortune at the stop is the mean fortune at the start, provided the stopping time is bounded, or the fortune cannot wander off to infinity before you stop, or the game is short on average and the steps are small. For a supermartingale the conclusion is $E[M_T] \le E[M_0]$.

**Wald's identity.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with $E\lvert X_1 \rvert \lt \infty$ and mean $\mu$, let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$, and let $T$ be a stopping time for $\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ with $E[T] \lt \infty$. Then

$$
E[S_T] = \mu \thinspace E[T]
$$

**In words:** the expected total of a random number of terms is the mean term times the expected number of terms, as in [14.7](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.7-Random-Sums.md), as long as the decision to stop uses only the terms already seen.

## Why It Is True

**Proof of Theorem 1.** Write the stopped process as a martingale transform ([20.8](20.8-Martingales.md)):

$$
M_{T \wedge n} = M_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} I_{\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace} (M_k - M_{k-1})
$$

The stake $H_k = I_{\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace} = 1 - I_{\lbrace T \le k-1 \rbrace}$ is $0$ or $1$, and it is known at time $k - 1$, because $\lbrace T \le k-1 \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}_{k-1}$ (a union of events $\lbrace T = j \rbrace$ with $j \le k-1$). So $H$ is predictable and bounded, and the transform is a martingale.

**Proof of Theorem 2.** By Theorem 1, $E[M_{T \wedge n}] = E[M_0]$ for each fixed $n$.

- (a) Take $n = K$. Then $T \wedge K = T$.
- (b) Since $T \lt \infty$ almost surely, $M_{T \wedge n} \to M_T$ almost surely. The variables are bounded by the constant $C$, which is integrable, so the dominated convergence theorem ([20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md)) gives $E[M_{T \wedge n}] \to E[M_T]$. Hence $E[M_T] = E[M_0]$.
- (c) We have $\lvert M_{T \wedge n} \rvert \le \lvert M_0 \rvert + \sum_{k \le T} \lvert M_k - M_{k-1} \rvert \le \lvert M_0 \rvert + cT$, which is integrable because $E[T] \lt \infty$. Use the dominated convergence theorem as in (b).

**Proof of Wald's identity.** Write $S_T = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} X_k I_{\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace}$. The event $\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace = \lbrace T \le k-1 \rbrace^c$ is decided by $X_1, \dots, X_{k-1}$, so it is independent of $X_k$. Therefore:

$$
E\Big[ \sum_{k} \lvert X_k \rvert I_{\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace} \Big] = \sum_{k} E\lvert X_k \rvert P(T \ge k) = E\lvert X_1 \rvert \sum_{k \ge 1} P(T \ge k) = E\lvert X_1 \rvert \thinspace E[T] \lt \infty
$$

(the first step is the monotone convergence theorem, the last is the tail-sum formula of [20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md)). Because this is finite, we may swap the sum and the expectation in $E[S_T]$ (dominated convergence):

$$
E[S_T] = \sum_{k} E[X_k] P(T \ge k) = \mu \sum_{k \ge 1} P(T \ge k) = \mu \thinspace E[T]
$$

If $T$ is not a stopping time, the independence of $X_k$ and $\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace$ can fail. In [14.7](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.7-Random-Sums.md), Example 3, the count $N = X_1$ looks at $X_1$ before deciding how many terms to add, and the formula is wrong.

**Gambler's ruin by optional stopping.** Let $S_0 = k$ with $0 \lt k \lt N$, steps $+1$ with probability $p$ and $-1$ with probability $q = 1 - p$. Let $T$ be the first time the walk is at $0$ or at $N$, and let $w = P(S_T = N)$ be the probability of winning.

*Step 0: the game ends, and $E[T] \lt \infty$.* This is the first result of [18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md). It also follows from the stopped martingales below: in the fair case, $S_n^2 - n$ gives $E[T \wedge n] = E[S_{T \wedge n}^2] - k^2 \le N^2$. In the biased case, $S_n - n(p - q)$ gives $E[T \wedge n] = \frac{E[S_{T \wedge n}] - k}{p - q}$, which is at most $\frac{N}{\lvert p - q \rvert}$. In both cases $E[T \wedge n]$ is bounded as $n \to \infty$, so $E[T] \lt \infty$ by the monotone convergence theorem, and $T \lt \infty$ almost surely.

*Fair game ($p = \frac{1}{2}$): the winning probability.* The walk $S_n$ is a martingale. Until time $T$ it stays in $[0, N]$, so $\lvert S_{T \wedge n} \rvert \le N$ (condition (b)). Optional stopping gives $E[S_T] = k$. But $S_T$ is $N$ with probability $w$ and $0$ otherwise:

$$
N w = k \implies \boxed{w = \frac{k}{N}}
$$

*Biased game: the winning probability.* Let $r = \frac{q}{p}$. The process $r^{S_n}$ is a martingale ([20.8](20.8-Martingales.md)), and it is bounded on $[0, N]$, so (b) holds. Optional stopping: $E[r^{S_T}] = r^k$. Since $S_T \in \lbrace 0, N \rbrace$:

$$
w \thinspace r^N + (1 - w) \cdot 1 = r^k \implies \boxed{w = \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}}
$$

*Fair game: the expected duration.* The process $S_n^2 - n$ is a martingale. Stopped at $T \wedge n$: $E[S_{T \wedge n}^2] - E[T \wedge n] = k^2$. Let $n \to \infty$. The first term converges to $E[S_T^2] = N^2 w = N k$ (bounded convergence), and $E[T \wedge n] \to E[T]$ (monotone convergence). So $E[T] = N k - k^2$:

$$
\boxed{E[T] = k (N - k)}
$$

*Biased game: the expected duration.* The process $S_n - n(p - q)$ is a martingale with bounded steps, and $E[T] \lt \infty$, so (c) holds: $E[S_T] - (p - q) E[T] = k$. With $E[S_T] = N w$:

$$
\boxed{E[T] = \frac{k - N w}{q - p}}
$$

These are exactly the formulas of [4.11.5](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md) and [18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md), obtained without solving a difference equation.

**When optional stopping fails.** Let $S_n$ be the fair walk from 0 and $T$ the first time $S_n = +1$. The walk is recurrent, so $T \lt \infty$ almost surely ([18.2.1](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md)), and $S_T = 1$ always. So $E[S_T] = 1 \ne 0 = E[S_0]$. Condition (b) fails because the stopped walk can go arbitrarily far below 0 before it reaches +1. Condition (c) fails because $E[T] = \infty$. The doubling strategy "bet until the first win" has the same problem: it wins 1 taka with certainty, but needs an unbounded credit line.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Gambler's Ruin with Numbers

**Problem.** You start with $k = 3$ taka and play until you reach $N = 10$ taka or lose everything. Find the probability of reaching 10 and the expected number of rounds, (a) in a fair game and (b) with win probability $p = 0.45$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) Fair game: $w = \frac{k}{N} = \frac{3}{10} = 0.3$, and $E[T] = k(N - k) = 3 \times 7 = 21$.
2. (b) Biased game: $q = 0.55$, $r = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{11}{9} \approx 1.2222$. Then $r^3 \approx 1.8258$ and $r^{10} \approx 7.4388$.
3. The winning probability:

$$
w = \frac{1 - r^3}{1 - r^{10}} = \frac{1 - 1.8258}{1 - 7.4388} = \frac{-0.8258}{-6.4388} \approx 0.1283
$$

4. The expected duration, with $q - p = 0.1$:

$$
E[T] = \frac{k - N w}{q - p} = \frac{3 - 10(0.1283)}{0.1} = \frac{3 - 1.283}{0.1} \approx 17.17
$$

**Answer.** (a) $0.3$ and 21 rounds. (b) about $0.128$ and about $17.2$ rounds.

**Check.** The simulation below gives $0.3011$ and $21.07$ for (a), and $0.1289$ and $17.19$ for (b). The slightly unfair game cuts the chance of winning from 30% to 13%, although it shortens the game by only 4 rounds.

### Example 2: A Random Sum (Wald)

**Problem.** Roll a fair die until the first 6 appears (including that roll). What is the expected sum of all the rolls?

**Solution.**

1. Let $X_i$ be the $i$-th roll, with mean $\mu = 3.5$. Let $T$ be the number of the roll that shows the first 6. The event $\lbrace T = n \rbrace$ depends only on rolls $1, \dots, n$, so $T$ is a stopping time.
2. $T$ has a geometric law with success probability $\frac{1}{6}$, so $E[T] = 6$, which is finite.
3. Wald's identity: $E[S_T] = \mu E[T] = 3.5 \times 6 = 21$.

**Answer.** The expected sum is 21.

**Check.** A direct count: the last roll is the 6. Before it, there are $E[T] - 1 = 5$ rolls on average, each a number from 1 to 5, with mean $3$. So $E[S_T] = 6 + 5 \times 3 = 21$. The simulation gives $21.02$. Note that the earlier rolls are not fair: stopping makes them smaller than 3.5 on average. Wald's identity still holds.

### Example 3: Waiting for HH and for HT

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin repeatedly. Show that the expected number of flips until HH first appears is 6, and until HT first appears is 4 (the numbers of [14.3](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)).

**Solution.** Use a **casino argument**. Before every flip $n = 1, 2, \dots$ a new gambler arrives with 1 taka. He bets it all on the next flip showing the first letter of the pattern, at fair odds (he doubles his money if he wins and loses it if not). If he wins, he bets all his winnings on the second letter, and so on, until he has bet on the whole pattern or loses. Each gambler's fortune is a martingale transform of a fair game, and so is the total net gain of all the gamblers. Let $T$ be the time the pattern first appears. Each step of the combined game is bounded (at most two gamblers are still in play, with stakes at most 2), and $E[T] \lt \infty$. By optional stopping (c), the total net gain at time $T$ has mean 0. So the total money held by the gamblers at time $T$ equals the total they paid in, which is $T$ taka, on average.

1. **HH.** At time $T$, the last two flips are HH. The gambler who arrived at flip $T - 1$ has won twice: he holds 4 taka. The gambler who arrived at flip $T$ has won once: he holds 2 taka. Earlier gamblers are out: a gambler who arrived at flip $T - 2$ and was still alive would have seen an H at $T - 2$ and an H at $T - 1$, and then HH would have appeared at time $T - 1$, before $T$. So the total held is $4 + 2 = 6$ taka.
2. Hence $E[T] = 6$.
3. **HT.** At time $T$ the last two flips are HT. The gambler who arrived at flip $T - 1$ bet on H, then on T, and has 4 taka. The gambler who arrived at flip $T$ bet on H and saw T, so he lost. No other gambler is alive. The total held is 4 taka, so $E[T] = 4$.

**Answer.** $E[T_{\text{HH}}] = 6$ and $E[T_{\text{HT}}] = 4$.

**Check.** The simulation below gives $5.98$ and $4.00$. The pattern HH takes longer because it overlaps itself (the first H can be the start of the next try), which leaves an extra gambler holding 2 taka at the end.

### Example 4: Why "Play Until You Win" Fails

**Problem.** In a fair game you bet 1 taka, and after every loss you double the bet, until the first win. Show that $T$, the round of the first win, is a stopping time with $T \lt \infty$ almost surely, and that your profit is exactly $+1$. Why does this not contradict $E[\text{profit}] = 0$?

**Solution.**

1. $\lbrace T = n \rbrace$ means "lose $n - 1$ times, then win", which is known at time $n$. So $T$ is a stopping time. $P(T = n) = \frac{1}{2^n}$ gives $P(T \lt \infty) = 1$.
2. After losing $n - 1$ rounds you have lost $1 + 2 + \dots + 2^{n-2} = 2^{n-1} - 1$ taka. The winning bet is $2^{n-1}$ taka, so the profit is $2^{n-1} - (2^{n-1} - 1) = 1$.
3. The profit is $+1$ with probability 1, so $E[\text{profit at } T] = 1$, while $E[\text{profit at time } 0] = 0$.
4. Optional stopping does not apply. The stopped fortune $M_{T \wedge n}$ is $-(2^n - 1)$ with probability $\frac{1}{2^n}$, which is not bounded by a constant (condition (b) fails). The bets are not bounded either (condition (c) fails), although $E[T] = 2$.

**Answer.** The profit is $+1$ for sure, and no theorem is violated.

**Check.** The mean of the bounded versions is 0: with at most $m$ bets the fortune is $+1$ with probability $1 - \frac{1}{2^m}$ and $-(2^m - 1)$ with probability $\frac{1}{2^m}$. The mean is exactly 0 for every $m$. The $+1$ is paid for with an infinitely deep pocket, which nobody has.

## Simulate It

The code simulates (1) gambler's ruin from $k = 3$ with $N = 10$ for the fair and the biased game, (2) the die-until-six sum with Wald's identity, and (3) the waiting times of four coin patterns.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Gambler's ruin: start at k = 3, stop at 0 or N = 10. Optional stopping predicts P(reach N) and E[T].
k, big_n, n_paths = 3, 10, 200_000
for p in [0.5, 0.45]:
    pos = np.full(n_paths, k)
    steps_taken = np.zeros(n_paths)
    active = np.ones(n_paths, bool)
    while active.any():
        pos[active] += np.where(rng.random(active.sum()) < p, 1, -1)
        steps_taken[active] += 1
        active &= (pos > 0) & (pos < big_n)
    r = (1 - p) / p
    w = k / big_n if p == 0.5 else (1 - r**k) / (1 - r**big_n)
    t = k * (big_n - k) if p == 0.5 else (k - big_n * w) / (2 * (1 - p) - 1)
    print(f"p = {p}: P(reach N) = {np.mean(pos == big_n):.4f} (exact {w:.4f}),"
          f" E[T] = {steps_taken.mean():.2f} (exact {t:.2f})")

# 2. Wald: roll a die until the first 6. E[sum of all rolls] = 3.5 * E[T] = 3.5 * 6 = 21.
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(100_000, 200))
t_six = (rolls == 6).argmax(axis=1) + 1                  # T = number of rolls up to and including the first 6
sum_to_t = np.take_along_axis(rolls.cumsum(axis=1), (t_six - 1)[:, None], axis=1).ravel()
print(f"Wald: E[T] = {t_six.mean():.3f} (exact 6), E[S_T] = {sum_to_t.mean():.3f} (exact 21)")

# 3. Waiting time for a pattern of fair coin flips (1 = H): 2^L plus overlap terms.
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(100_000, 250))
for name, pattern, exact in [("HH", [1, 1], 6), ("HT", [1, 0], 4), ("HHH", [1, 1, 1], 14), ("HTH", [1, 0, 1], 10)]:
    length = len(pattern)
    match = np.ones((flips.shape[0], 250 - length + 1), bool)
    for j, c in enumerate(pattern):
        match &= flips[:, j:250 - length + 1 + j] == c
    print(f"E[wait for {name}] = {(match.argmax(axis=1) + length).mean():.3f} (exact {exact})")
```

Output:

```text
p = 0.5: P(reach N) = 0.3011 (exact 0.3000), E[T] = 21.07 (exact 21.00)
p = 0.45: P(reach N) = 0.1289 (exact 0.1283), E[T] = 17.19 (exact 17.17)
Wald: E[T] = 6.008 (exact 6), E[S_T] = 21.021 (exact 21)
E[wait for HH] = 5.980 (exact 6)
E[wait for HT] = 3.998 (exact 4)
E[wait for HHH] = 14.008 (exact 14)
E[wait for HTH] = 9.992 (exact 10)
```

All the "exact" values come from optional stopping, Wald's identity and the casino argument, and the simulation agrees with each of them to within about 0.5%.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using a rule that peeks into the future.** "Stop at the highest point" is not a stopping time, and optional stopping does not apply.
- **Applying $E[M_T] = E[M_0]$ without checking conditions.** The walk stopped at $+1$ has $E[S_T] = 1 \ne 0$. Check (a), (b) or (c) every time.
- **Forgetting to show that $T$ is finite (almost surely, or even in mean).** Without $T \lt \infty$, $M_T$ is not defined.
- **Using Wald's identity for a count that looks at the terms it counts.** $T$ must be a stopping time. The count $N = X_1$ in [14.7](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.7-Random-Sums.md) is not.
- **Thinking that the terms before a stopping time are still average.** Before the first 6, the rolls are smaller than 3.5 on average. Only the total obeys $\mu E[T]$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Gambling and finance:** "no stopping rule beats a fair game" (with bounded credit), and fair pricing of options that can be exercised at a time of your choice.
- **Sequential testing:** an A/B test that stops as soon as the evidence is strong is analyzed with stopping times, and Wald's identity gives the expected sample size of the sequential probability ratio test.
- **Computer science:** the expected running time of a randomized algorithm that repeats a round until success, using Wald's identity: expected total work equals (expected number of rounds) $\times$ (mean work per round).
- **Machine learning:** early stopping rules in training and in bandit algorithms are stopping times. Their analysis uses the stopped-martingale theorem.
- **Biology and text search:** the expected waiting time for a given DNA motif or word in a random sequence is found by the casino argument.

<summary>Answer</summary>

$$8 w + 0 \cdot (1 - w) = 2 \implies w = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}$$

**3. (Medium)** A walk has $p = 0.6$ for $+1$ and $q = 0.4$ for $-1$. It starts at 5 and stops at 0 or 10. Find the probability of reaching 10 and the expected number of steps.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $r = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{2}{3}$. Use $r^{S_n}$ with $k = 5$, $N = 10$:

$$w = \frac{1 - r^5}{1 - r^{10}} = \frac{1}{1 + r^5} = \frac{1}{1 + 32/243} = \frac{243}{275} \approx 0.8836$$

For the duration, use $S_n - n(p - q)$ with $q - p = -0.2$:

$$E[T] = \frac{k - N w}{q - p} = \frac{5 - 8.8364}{-0.2} = \frac{3.8364}{0.2} \approx 19.18$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Roll a fair die repeatedly. You stop the first time you roll a 1 or a 2, and each roll pays you its face value in taka. What is the expected total payment?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The decision to stop depends only on the rolls so far, so $T$ is a stopping time. $T$ is geometric with success probability $\frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$, so $E[T] = 3$. By Wald's identity:

$$E[S_T] = 3.5 \times 3 = 10.5$$

Check by a direct count: the last roll is 1 or 2 (mean 1.5), and the 2 earlier rolls on average are from $\lbrace 3, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$ (mean 4.5): $1.5 + 2 \times 4.5 = 10.5$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Use the casino argument to find the expected number of fair coin flips until HTH first appears, and until HHH first appears.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**HTH.** At the time $T$ of the first HTH, the gambler who arrived at flip $T - 2$ has won three times and holds 8 taka. The gambler who arrived at flip $T - 1$ bet on H and saw T, so he lost. The gambler who arrived at flip $T$ bet on H and saw H, and holds 2 taka. Total: $8 + 2 = 10$, so $E[T] = 10$.

**HHH.** The gamblers who arrived at flips $T - 2$, $T - 1$ and $T$ hold $8$, $4$ and $2$ taka. Total: $14$, so $E[T] = 14$.

The expected waiting time is the sum of $2^j$ over the lengths $j$ for which the pattern's first $j$ letters equal its last $j$ letters (these are the self-overlaps, always including the full length). For HTH: $j = 1$ and $j = 3$ give $2 + 8 = 10$. For HHH: $j = 1, 2, 3$ give $2 + 4 + 8 = 14$. For HT: only $j = 2$, giving 4.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Let a fair walk start at 0 and stop at the first time it reaches $+1$, or at time 1,000, whichever comes first. Estimate $P(T \le 1000)$ and the mean of the stopped walk. Explain why the mean is 0 even though $S_T = 1$ almost always.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_paths, horizon = 20_000, 1_000
walk = rng.choice(np.array([-1, 1], dtype=np.int16), size=(n_paths, horizon)).cumsum(axis=1)
hit = walk.max(axis=1) >= 1                          # the walk reached +1 before the horizon
stopped = np.where(hit, 1, walk[:, -1])              # value of S at time min(T, horizon)
print(f"P(T <= {horizon}) = {hit.mean():.4f}   E[S at min(T, {horizon})] = {stopped.mean():+.3f}")
print(f"mean of S at the horizon among paths that never hit +1: {walk[~hit, -1].mean():.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(T <= 1000) = 0.9750   E[S at min(T, 1000)] = -0.014
mean of S at the horizon among paths that never hit +1: -39.6
```

The stopping time $T \wedge 1000$ is bounded, so optional stopping (a) holds and the mean is 0 (the simulation gives $-0.014$, which is noise). In 97.5% of the runs the walk stops at $+1$, gaining 1. In the other 2.5% it sits far below 0, at $-39.6$ on average, and $0.975 \times 1 + 0.025 \times (-39.6) \approx 0$. As the horizon grows, the bad runs become rarer but deeper, and the balance stays at 0 for every finite horizon. In the limit the bad runs disappear and the mean jumps to 1: the theorem fails there.

</details>

## Summary

- A **stopping time** $T$ satisfies $\lbrace T = n \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}_n$: you decide to stop using only the past. Hitting times are stopping times, and "the time of the maximum" is not.
- The **stopped process** $M_{T \wedge n}$ of a martingale is a martingale, because it is a martingale transform with stake $I_{\lbrace T \ge k \rbrace}$.
- **Optional stopping:** $E[M_T] = E[M_0]$ if $T \lt \infty$ and $T$ is bounded, or the stopped process is bounded, or $E[T] \lt \infty$ with bounded steps. It fails without conditions ($+1$ walk, play-until-you-win).
- **Gambler's ruin:** $w = \frac{k}{N}$ from the martingale $S_n$, $w = \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}$ from $r^{S_n}$, $E[T] = k(N - k)$ from $S_n^2 - n$, and $E[T] = \frac{k - Nw}{q - p}$ from $S_n - n(p - q)$.
- **Wald's identity:** $E[S_T] = \mu E[T]$ for a stopping time with $E[T] \lt \infty$. The casino argument gives pattern waiting times.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[M_T] = E[M_0] \quad \text{(for a martingale } M \text{ and a stopping time } T \text{ under conditions (a), (b) or (c))}}
$$
