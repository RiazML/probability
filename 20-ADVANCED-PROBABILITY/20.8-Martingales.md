# 20.8 Martingales

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.8 Martingales

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 75 minutes · **Before this:** [20.7 Kolmogorov's Zero-One Law](20.7-Kolmogorov-Zero-One-Law.md)

> **Big idea:** A martingale is a random process whose best prediction of the next value, given everything known so far, is the current value, which is the mathematics of a fair game, and it proves that no betting system can beat a fair game and that functions of independent variables concentrate.

---

## Why It Matters

A **martingale** is the right model of a fair game: your fortune in a game where each bet has mean zero. Its rule $E[M_{n+1} \mid \text{past}] = M_n$ was promised in [14.2](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md) and [14.6](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.6-Taking-Out-What-is-Known.md). Martingales give the shortest proofs of the gambler's ruin probabilities ([20.9](20.9-Stopping-Times-and-Optional-Stopping.md)), the full proof of McDiarmid's inequality ([16.8](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.8-Concentration-of-Measure.md)), the convergence of random series ([20.7](20.7-Kolmogorov-Zero-One-Law.md)), and the statement that "you cannot beat a fair game with any betting system". In finance, the fair price of an asset is a martingale. In machine learning, martingale tools analyze stochastic gradient descent, bandits and generalization bounds.

## Intuition

You start with 100 taka and bet 1 taka on a fair coin each round. After 3 rounds you have 102. What do you expect after round 4? You win 1 or lose 1 with equal chance, so you expect $\frac{103 + 101}{2} = 102$. Your best prediction of tomorrow's fortune is today's fortune. That is the whole idea. The fortune may go up or down, but there is no drift that you can predict from the past.

A **betting system** is a rule that uses the past to choose the next bet: "double after each loss", "bet more after a win", "stop when ahead by 10 taka". The remarkable theorem is that every such rule gives a fortune that is again a fair game. The coin does not care about your strategy.

To say "best prediction given everything known so far" with full precision, we need the conditional expectation given a $\sigma$-algebra, which models the information known at time $n$ ([20.2](20.2-Sigma-Algebras-and-Measurable-Spaces.md)).

## Picture

The fortune $M_n$ in the fair game, as a tree. From each node, the two next values average to the node's value:

```text
                                 +-- 1/2 --> 102
                      +-- 1/2 --> 101
                      |          +-- 1/2 --> 100
      100 ------------+
                      |          +-- 1/2 --> 100
                      +-- 1/2 --> 99

  From 101: (102 + 100) / 2 = 101.   From 99: (100 + 98) / 2 = 99.
  From 100: (101 + 99) / 2 = 100.    Every node equals the average below it.
```

A martingale has no predictable drift. The picture shows $E[M_{n+1} \mid M_n = m] = m$ for every $m$.

## Definition

**Conditional expectation given a $\sigma$-algebra.** Let $Y$ be integrable and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ a $\sigma$-algebra (the information we know). The **conditional expectation** $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the random variable $Z$ such that

1. $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable (it can be computed from the information in $\mathcal{G}$), and
2. $E[Z \thinspace I_G] = E[Y \thinspace I_G]$ for every event $G \in \mathcal{G}$.

**In words:** $Z$ is a prediction of $Y$ made from the information $\mathcal{G}$, and it has the right average on every event that $\mathcal{G}$ can describe.

Two facts make this a good definition:

- **It exists** (and is unique up to a null set): this follows from the Radon-Nikodym theorem ([20.10](20.10-Radon-Nikodym-and-Densities.md)).
- **It is unique.** If $Z$ and $Z'$ both satisfy 1 and 2, the event $G = \lbrace Z \gt Z' \rbrace$ is in $\mathcal{G}$, and $E[(Z - Z') I_G] = 0$ with $Z - Z' \gt 0$ on $G$. So $P(G) = 0$ ([20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md)). Symmetrically $P(Z \lt Z') = 0$.

**It agrees with Module 14.** If $\mathcal{G}$ is generated by a partition $B_1, B_2, \dots$ with $P(B_j) \gt 0$, then $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ equals $\frac{E[Y I_{B_j}]}{P(B_j)}$ on the block $B_j$: the group average of [14.2](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md). For a $\mathcal{G}$-event $G$ that is a union of blocks, summing the blocks gives property 2. We write $E[Y \mid X]$ for $E[Y \mid \sigma(X)]$.

**Rules.** For integrable $Y$ and $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$:

- **Linearity:** $E[aY + bW \mid \mathcal{G}] = a E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] + b E[W \mid \mathcal{G}]$.
- **Tower rule:** $E\big[ E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H} \big] = E[Y \mid \mathcal{H}]$. With $\mathcal{H}$ trivial: $E[E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]] = E[Y]$.
- **Taking out what is known:** if $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and bounded, $E[ZY \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z \thinspace E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$.
- **Independence:** if $Y$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, then $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] = E[Y]$.

**Filtration, adapted process.** A **filtration** is an increasing list of $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_2 \subseteq \cdots$. Think of $\mathcal{F}_n$ as everything known at time $n$. For variables $X_1, X_2, \dots$ the **natural filtration** is $\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$. A process $(M_n)$ is **adapted** if $M_n$ is $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable for every $n$.

**Martingale.** An adapted process $(M_n)_{n \ge 0}$ with $E\lvert M_n \rvert \lt \infty$ is a **martingale** if

$$
E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n \quad \text{for all } n \ge 0
$$

It is a **submartingale** if the equality is replaced by $\ge$ (a favorable game), and a **supermartingale** if it is replaced by $\le$ (an unfavorable game, such as a casino game from the player's side).

**In words:** the expected next value, given all the information up to now, is the present value.

By the tower rule, a martingale also satisfies $E[M_{n+m} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n$ for every $m \ge 0$ and $E[M_n] = E[M_0]$ for every $n$.

**Examples.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent, $\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$, and $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$.

| Process | Conditions | Why it is a martingale |
|---|---|---|
| $S_n$ | $E[X_i] = 0$ | $E[S_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = S_n + E[X_{n+1}] = S_n$ |
| $S_n^2 - n \sigma^2$ | $E[X_i] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2$ | $E[S_{n+1}^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = S_n^2 + 2 S_n E[X_{n+1}] + \sigma^2$ |
| $\left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^{S_n}$ | $P(X_i = 1) = p$, $P(X_i = -1) = q = 1 - p$ | $E\big[(q/p)^{X_{n+1}}\big] = p \cdot \frac{q}{p} + q \cdot \frac{p}{q} = 1$ |
| $S_n - n(p - q)$ | same walk | $E[X_i] = p - q$, so subtract the drift |
| $\prod_{i \le n} Y_i$ | $Y_i \ge 0$ independent with $E[Y_i] = 1$ | the next factor has mean 1 and is independent of the past |
| $E[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$ (Doob martingale) | $Z$ integrable | tower rule: $E[E[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_{n+1}] \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = E[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$ |

**Betting systems (martingale transform).** A **predictable** process $H_1, H_2, \dots$ has $H_k$ measurable with respect to $\mathcal{F}_{k-1}$: the stake for round $k$ is chosen before round $k$, using the past. If $M$ is a martingale and the $H_k$ are bounded, the **transform**

$$
(H \cdot M)_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} H_k (M_k - M_{k-1})
$$

(the fortune when you stake $H_k$ on round $k$) is again a martingale.

**In words:** whatever stake you choose from the past, a fair game stays fair. In particular $E[(H \cdot M)_n] = 0$ for every $n$.

## Why It Is True

**Rules of conditional expectation.** Each rule is checked with properties 1 and 2.

- **Tower rule.** Let $Z = E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ and $W = E[Y \mid \mathcal{H}]$. For $H \in \mathcal{H}$ we have $H \in \mathcal{G}$ too, so $E[Z I_H] = E[Y I_H] = E[W I_H]$. Also $W$ is $\mathcal{H}$-measurable. So $W$ satisfies 1 and 2 for $E[Z \mid \mathcal{H}]$.
- **Taking out what is known.** If $Z = I_A$ with $A \in \mathcal{G}$, then for $G \in \mathcal{G}$: $E[I_A Y I_G] = E[Y I_{A \cap G}] = E\big[E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] I_{A \cap G}\big]$ since $A \cap G \in \mathcal{G}$. So $I_A E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ has property 2 for $E[I_A Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$. Linearity extends this to simple $Z$, and the monotone and dominated convergence theorems of [20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md) extend it to bounded $Z$.
- **Independence.** The constant $E[Y]$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, and $E[Y I_G] = E[Y] P(G) = E\big[ E[Y] I_G \big]$ by independence.

**The examples.** Row 1: $S_n$ is $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable, and $E[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = E[X_{n+1}] = 0$ by independence, so $E[S_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = S_n + 0$. Row 2: $S_n$ is known given $\mathcal{F}_n$, so it comes out (taking out what is known), and $E[S_{n+1}^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = S_n^2 + 2 S_n \cdot 0 + \sigma^2$. Subtracting $(n+1)\sigma^2$ leaves $S_n^2 - n \sigma^2$. Row 3: $(q/p)^{S_{n+1}} = (q/p)^{S_n} (q/p)^{X_{n+1}}$, take the known factor out, and the other factor has mean 1. The rest are similar. Doob martingales are exactly the tower rule.

**Proof that betting systems cannot win.** The transform is adapted, and integrable since the $H_k$ are bounded. For the increment:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big[ (H \cdot M)_{n+1} - (H \cdot M)_n \mid \mathcal{F}_n \big] &= E\big[ H_{n+1} (M_{n+1} - M_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_n \big] \\
&= H_{n+1} \thinspace E[M_{n+1} - M_n \mid \mathcal{F}_n] && \text{(} H_{n+1} \text{ is known at time } n \text{)} \\
&= H_{n+1} \cdot 0 = 0 && \text{(martingale)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $(H \cdot M)$ is a martingale and $E[(H \cdot M)_n] = (H \cdot M)_0 = 0$. The step that matters is that $H_{n+1}$ is known at time $n$. A gambler who could see the next flip before betting would not be playing a fair game.

**Convergence of martingales (Doob, stated without proof).** If $(M_n)$ is a martingale with $\sup_n E\lvert M_n \rvert \lt \infty$, then $M_n$ converges almost surely to a finite random variable $M_{\infty}$. In particular a non-negative martingale always converges.

**In words:** a fair game with bounded average size cannot keep fluctuating forever. A key point: the limit can have a **smaller** mean than the process ($E[M_{\infty}] \lt E[M_0]$ is possible), as in Practice 4.

Application: the random harmonic series $S_n = \sum_{k \le n} \frac{\varepsilon_k}{k}$ of [20.7](20.7-Kolmogorov-Zero-One-Law.md) is a martingale with $E[S_n^2] = \sum_{k \le n} \frac{1}{k^2} \le \frac{\pi^2}{6}$. By Cauchy-Schwarz, $E\lvert S_n \rvert \le \sqrt{E[S_n^2]} \le 1.29$, which is bounded, so $S_n$ converges almost surely.

**Azuma-Hoeffding inequality.** Let $(M_k)$ be a martingale with $M_0$ constant, and suppose that each increment $D_k = M_k - M_{k-1}$ satisfies $A_k \le D_k \le A_k + c_k$, where $A_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_{k-1}$-measurable and $c_k$ is a constant. Then for every $t \gt 0$:

$$
P(M_n - M_0 \ge t) \le \exp\left( - \frac{2 t^2}{\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k^2} \right)
$$

**In words:** a martingale whose steps have bounded range stays near its start: the chance of drifting $t$ away is exponentially small.

*Proof.* We use **Hoeffding's lemma** ([16.7](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)): if $E[Y] = 0$ and $a \le Y \le b$, then $E[e^{sY}] \le e^{s^2 (b-a)^2 / 8}$. It holds conditionally too: if $E[D \mid \mathcal{G}] = 0$ and $A \le D \le A + c$ with $A$ being $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, then $E[e^{sD} \mid \mathcal{G}] \le e^{s^2 c^2 / 8}$. (Apply the lemma to the conditional law of $D$ given $\mathcal{G}$, where $A$ is just a fixed number.) Then, taking out the known factor $e^{s(M_{n-1} - M_0)}$:

$$
E\big[ e^{s(M_n - M_0)} \big] = E\Big[ e^{s(M_{n-1} - M_0)} \thinspace E\big[ e^{s D_n} \mid \mathcal{F}_{n-1} \big] \Big] \le e^{s^2 c_n^2 / 8} \thinspace E\big[ e^{s(M_{n-1} - M_0)} \big]
$$

Repeating this for $n-1, n-2, \dots, 1$ gives $E[e^{s(M_n - M_0)}] \le \exp\left( \frac{s^2}{8} \sum_k c_k^2 \right)$. Now use Markov's inequality on $e^{s(M_n - M_0)}$ (the Chernoff method of [16.6](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)). With $C = \sum_k c_k^2$:

$$
P(M_n - M_0 \ge t) \le e^{-st} \thinspace E\big[ e^{s(M_n - M_0)} \big] \le \exp\left( -st + \frac{s^2 C}{8} \right)
$$

The exponent is smallest at $s = \frac{4t}{C}$, where it equals $-\frac{4 t^2}{C} + \frac{2 t^2}{C} = -\frac{2 t^2}{C}$.

**McDiarmid's inequality (the full proof promised in [16.8](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.8-Concentration-of-Measure.md)).** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent and let $f$ satisfy the bounded-difference condition: changing only the $i$-th input changes $f$ by at most $c_i$. Then for $t \gt 0$:

$$
P\big( f(X_1, \dots, X_n) - E[f] \ge t \big) \le \exp\left( - \frac{2 t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i^2} \right)
$$

and the same bound holds for $E[f] - f \ge t$, so the two-sided probability is at most twice this.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{F}_k = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_k)$ and define the Doob martingale $W_k = E[f \mid \mathcal{F}_k]$. Then $W_0 = E[f]$ and $W_n = f$. By independence, $W_k = g_k(X_1, \dots, X_k)$ where $g_k(x_1, \dots, x_k) = E\big[ f(x_1, \dots, x_k, X_{k+1}, \dots, X_n) \big]$, an average over the later inputs only. Also $W_{k-1} = h(X_1, \dots, X_{k-1})$ with $h(x_1, \dots, x_{k-1}) = E[g_k(x_1, \dots, x_{k-1}, X_k)]$. So the increment is

$$
D_k = W_k - W_{k-1} = g_k(X_1, \dots, X_{k-1}, X_k) - h(X_1, \dots, X_{k-1})
$$

Given $X_1, \dots, X_{k-1}$, the only thing still random in $D_k$ is the last argument $X_k$ of $g_k$. Let $A_k = \inf_x g_k(X_1, \dots, X_{k-1}, x) - h(X_1, \dots, X_{k-1})$ (it is known at time $k - 1$). Then $A_k \le D_k \le A_k + \Delta$, where $\Delta$ is the largest difference $g_k(\dots, x) - g_k(\dots, x')$. For any two values $x, x'$ the difference is an average of differences of $f$ in the $k$-th input only, so it is at most $c_k$. Hence $A_k \le D_k \le A_k + c_k$. (A technical remark: to make the infimum measurable, take it over a countable dense set of $x$; we skip the details.) Azuma-Hoeffding now gives the bound for $W_n - W_0 = f - E[f]$. Applying it to $-f$ gives the lower tail.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Fortune in a Fair Game

**Problem.** You have $M_0 = 100$ taka and bet 1 taka on a fair coin each round, so $M_{n+1} = M_n + X_{n+1}$ with $X_{n+1} = \pm 1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, independent of the past. Show that $(M_n)$ is a martingale, and check the claim for $M_3 = 102$ and for the process $M_n^2 - n$ (with the fortune measured from 0, $S_n = M_n - 100$).

**Solution.**

1. $M_n = 100 + X_1 + \dots + X_n$ is a function of $X_1, \dots, X_n$, so it is adapted. It is integrable since $\lvert M_n \rvert \le 100 + n$.
2. $E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n + E[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n + E[X_{n+1}] = M_n + 0$, using independence of $X_{n+1}$ from the past.
3. Numeric check at $M_3 = 102$: the next value is 103 or 101 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, so $E[M_4 \mid M_3 = 102] = \frac{103 + 101}{2} = 102$.
4. For $S_n = M_n - 100$ and $Q_n = S_n^2 - n$: suppose $S_3 = 2$, so $Q_3 = 4 - 3 = 1$. Then $S_4 \in \lbrace 3, 1 \rbrace$ and $E[Q_4 \mid S_3 = 2] = \frac{9 + 1}{2} - 4 = 1 = Q_3$.

**Answer.** The fortune is a martingale, and so is $S_n^2 - n$.

**Check.** The simulation below confirms that the averages of $S_n$ and of $S_n^2 - n$ stay at 0 for $n = 5, 10, 20$.

### Example 2: A Biased Game Has a Hidden Martingale

**Problem.** In a game you win 1 taka with probability $p = 0.45$ and lose 1 taka with probability $q = 0.55$ on each round. Your fortune $S_n$ is not a martingale, since it drifts down by $0.1$ per round. Show that $M_n = \left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^{S_n}$ is a martingale, and check it when $S_n = 2$.

**Solution.**

1. Here $\frac{q}{p} = \frac{0.55}{0.45} = \frac{11}{9}$.
2. If $S_n = 2$, then $M_n = \left( \frac{11}{9} \right)^2$. The next value is $M_{n+1} = \left( \frac{11}{9} \right)^3$ with probability $0.45$ and $\left( \frac{11}{9} \right)^1$ with probability $0.55$.
3. Take the common factor out:

$$
E[M_{n+1} \mid S_n = 2] = \left( \tfrac{11}{9} \right)^2 \left[ 0.45 \cdot \tfrac{11}{9} + 0.55 \cdot \tfrac{9}{11} \right] = \left( \tfrac{11}{9} \right)^2 \left[ 0.55 + 0.45 \right] = M_n
$$

4. The same computation works for every value of $S_n$, because the bracket $p \cdot \frac{q}{p} + q \cdot \frac{p}{q} = q + p = 1$ does not depend on $S_n$.

**Answer.** $M_n = (q/p)^{S_n}$ is a martingale.

**Check.** The simulation below gives an average near 1 for all $n$, while the average of $S_n$ drifts down like $-0.1 n$. This martingale gives the biased gambler's ruin probability in [20.9](20.9-Stopping-Times-and-Optional-Stopping.md).

### Example 3: The Doubling Strategy

**Problem.** In a fair coin game you use the **doubling strategy**: bet 1 taka, and after each loss double the bet, until your first win, or until you have placed 3 bets. Find the distribution of your profit and its mean.

**Solution.**

1. The stake on round $k$ is $H_k = 2^{k-1}$ as long as you have not won yet, and 0 after the first win. Whether you have won is known from the past, so $H_k$ is predictable.
2. Win on round 1 (probability $\frac{1}{2}$): profit $+1$.
3. Lose, then win on round 2 (probability $\frac{1}{4}$): profit $-1 + 2 = +1$.
4. Lose twice, win on round 3 (probability $\frac{1}{8}$): profit $-1 - 2 + 4 = +1$.
5. Lose all three (probability $\frac{1}{8}$): profit $-1 - 2 - 4 = -7$.
6. Mean: $\frac{7}{8}(+1) + \frac{1}{8}(-7) = \frac{7}{8} - \frac{7}{8} = 0$.

**Answer.** The profit is $+1$ with probability $\frac{7}{8}$ and $-7$ with probability $\frac{1}{8}$, and the mean is exactly 0.

**Check.** This is the transform $(H \cdot M)_3$ with $E = 0$, as the theorem says. The strategy wins often but loses big rarely. With 10 bets it wins 1 taka with probability $1 - \frac{1}{1024}$ and loses 1,023 taka with probability $\frac{1}{1024}$, again with mean 0.

### Example 4: Hash Buckets Concentrate (McDiarmid)

**Problem.** Throw 100 keys independently and uniformly into 100 buckets. Let $N$ be the number of empty buckets. Bound $P(N - E[N] \ge 10)$.

**Solution.**

1. $N$ is a function of the 100 independent bucket choices $X_1, \dots, X_{100}$. Moving one key to another bucket changes $N$ by at most 1 (it can empty one bucket and fill another), so $c_i = 1$ and $\sum c_i^2 = 100$.
2. $E[N] = 100 \left( 1 - \frac{1}{100} \right)^{100} \approx 36.60$ (each bucket is empty with probability $0.99^{100}$).
3. McDiarmid with $t = 10$:

$$
P(N - E[N] \ge 10) \le \exp\left( - \frac{2 \cdot 10^2}{100} \right) = e^{-2} \approx 0.135
$$

**Answer.** At most $0.135$.

**Check.** The bound is safe but loose: simulation (Practice 6) gives about $0.0006$. McDiarmid does not use the variance, only the bounded changes.

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks three martingales for a fair walk ($p = 0.5$) and for a biased walk ($p = 0.45$). In the biased walk only $(q/p)^{S_n}$ keeps its mean. Part 2 runs the doubling strategy with 10 bets in a fair game. Part 3 is a multiplicative fair game (wealth times 1.5 or 0.5) whose mean stays 1 while the typical wealth collapses.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Three martingales built from a +-1 walk with P(step = +1) = p (fair: p = 0.5, biased: p = 0.45).
n_paths, n_steps = 400_000, 20
for p in [0.5, 0.45]:
    steps = np.where(rng.random((n_paths, n_steps)) < p, 1, -1)
    walk = steps.cumsum(axis=1)                              # S_1, ..., S_20 (column n-1 is S_n)
    ratio = (1 - p) / p
    print(f"p = {p}: E[M_n] for n = 5, 10, 20")
    for name, m in [("S_n", walk), ("S_n^2 - n", walk**2 - np.arange(1, n_steps + 1)),
                    ("(q/p)^S_n", ratio ** walk)]:
        print(f"   {name:<10}", "  ".join(f"{m[:, n - 1].mean():8.4f}" for n in (5, 10, 20)))
    if p == 0.5:
        print(f"   E[S_6 | S_5 = 1] = {walk[walk[:, 4] == 1, 5].mean():.4f}   (martingale says 1)")

# 2. Doubling strategy in a fair game: bet 1, 2, 4, ... until the first win, at most 10 bets.
win = rng.random((n_paths, 10)) < 0.5
has_win = win.any(axis=1)
profit = np.where(has_win, 1.0, -(2.0 ** 10 - 1))            # win 1 taka, or lose 1 + 2 + ... + 512
print(f"doubling: P(profit = +1) = {has_win.mean():.4f} (exact {1 - 2**-10:.4f}), mean profit = {profit.mean():+.4f}")

# 3. A multiplicative fair game: wealth x1.5 or x0.5 each round has mean 1, yet wealth tends to 0.
m20 = np.prod(np.where(rng.random((n_paths, 20)) < 0.5, 1.5, 0.5), axis=1)
print(f"M_20: mean {m20.mean():.3f} (exact 1), median {np.median(m20):.4f} (exact {0.75**10:.4f})")
```

```text
   S_n         -0.5059   -1.0048   -1.9981
   S_n^2 - n    0.1959    0.8481    3.7441
   (q/p)^S_n    0.9986    0.9978    0.9989
doubling: P(profit = +1) = 0.9990 (exact 0.9990), mean profit = -0.0342
M_20: mean 0.989 (exact 1), median 0.0563 (exact 0.0563)
```

In the fair walk, all three means stay at their starting values (0, 0 and 1), apart from small noise, and the conditional mean $E[S_6 \mid S_5 = 1] \approx 1$ is the martingale property itself. In the biased walk, $S_n$ drifts as $-0.1n$ (exact $-0.5, -1, -2$) and $S_n^2 - n$ is no longer a martingale, but $(q/p)^{S_n}$ still has mean 1. The doubling strategy wins 1 taka in 99.9% of the runs, and its mean profit is 0 up to noise (the rare big losses of 1,023 taka cause the noise). In the multiplicative game the mean is 1 but the median wealth is only $0.056$: the average is carried by rare huge values.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that a martingale does not move.** It moves a lot. It only has no predictable drift. The fortune in a fair game can end far from where it began.
- **Checking only that $E[M_n]$ is constant.** A martingale needs $E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n$ for **every** history, not just on average over all histories.
- **Letting a stake depend on the future.** In the transform, $H_{n+1}$ must be decided with information up to time $n$ only (predictable). Peeking at the next flip breaks the theorem.
- **Believing that the mean of the limit equals the limit of the means.** In the multiplicative game $E[M_n] = 1$ for all $n$, but $M_n \to 0$ almost surely (Practice 4). Convergence theorems of [20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md) need a dominating variable.
- **Forgetting integrability.** A martingale must have $E\lvert M_n \rvert \lt \infty$ so that the conditional expectation exists.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance:** under the risk-neutral measure, a discounted asset price is a martingale. Fair prices are conditional expectations. This is also why no betting system, or trading rule, can beat a fair market.
- **Machine learning:** the convergence proofs of stochastic gradient descent and Q-learning use the supermartingale convergence theorem. Bandit algorithms and generalization bounds use the Azuma-Hoeffding and McDiarmid inequalities.
- **Sequential testing and A/B tests:** the likelihood ratio of two hypotheses is a martingale under the null. This gives tests that remain valid however often you look at the data.
- **Algorithm analysis:** concentration of the number of empty hash buckets, the size of a random graph's largest clique, or the running time of a randomized algorithm uses the Doob martingale and McDiarmid.
- **Statistics:** the posterior mean $E[\theta \mid X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is a Doob martingale in $n$, and it converges as data accumulates.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Show that your fortune in a fair betting game is a martingale. You bet 1 taka on each of a sequence of independent rounds, winning or losing 1 taka with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, starting from $M_0 = 50$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $X_k = \pm 1$ be the result of round $k$. Then $M_n = 50 + X_1 + \dots + X_n$ is a function of $X_1, \dots, X_n$ (adapted), with $\lvert M_n \rvert \le 50 + n$ (integrable). By independence $E[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = E[X_{n+1}] = 0$, so:

$$E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n + E[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n + 0 = M_n$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A walk has steps $+1$ with probability $0.6$ and $-1$ with probability $0.4$. Find $E[S_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$ and a constant $c$ such that $S_n - cn$ is a martingale.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean step is $0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2$, so $E[S_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = S_n + 0.2$. This is a submartingale (a favorable game).

For $c = 0.2$: $E[S_{n+1} - 0.2(n+1) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = S_n + 0.2 - 0.2n - 0.2 = S_n - 0.2n$. So $S_n - 0.2n$ is a martingale.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $(M_n)$ be a martingale. Show that $E[M_{n+m} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n$ for every $m \ge 0$, and that $E[M_n] = E[M_0]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use induction on $m$. For $m = 0$ it is trivial, since $M_n$ is $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable. Suppose it holds for $m$. By the tower rule ($\mathcal{F}_n \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{n+m}$):

$$E[M_{n+m+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = E\big[ E[M_{n+m+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_{n+m}] \mid \mathcal{F}_n \big] = E[M_{n+m} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n$$

Taking expectations of $E[M_n \mid \mathcal{F}_0] = M_0$ with the tower rule gives $E[M_n] = E[M_0]$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $Y_1, Y_2, \dots$ be independent with $P(Y_i = 1.5) = P(Y_i = 0.5) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $M_n = Y_1 \cdots Y_n$. Show that $M_n$ is a martingale with $E[M_n] = 1$, and that $M_n \to 0$ almost surely. Why does this not contradict $E[M_n] = 1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[Y_i] = \frac{1.5 + 0.5}{2} = 1$, and $E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n E[Y_{n+1}] = M_n$, so it is a martingale with $E[M_n] = E[M_0] = 1$.

Take logarithms: $\ln M_n = \sum \ln Y_i$. The terms have mean $\frac{\ln 1.5 + \ln 0.5}{2} = \frac{\ln 0.75}{2} \approx -0.1438 \lt 0$. By the Strong Law, $\frac{1}{n} \ln M_n \to -0.1438$ almost surely, so $\ln M_n \to -\infty$ and $M_n \to 0$.

There is no contradiction. The limit of the means is 1, but the mean of the limit is 0. The martingale convergence theorem only gives $M_n \to M_{\infty}$ almost surely, with $E[M_{\infty}] \le 1$. No dominating integrable variable exists ([20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md), Example 2). The mean is carried by rarer and rarer huge values.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_N$ be independent fair coin flips (0 or 1) and $Z = X_1 + \dots + X_N$. Find the Doob martingale $M_n = E[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$, and use Azuma-Hoeffding to prove $P\big( Z - \frac{N}{2} \ge t \big) \le \exp\left( -\frac{2 t^2}{N} \right)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Given $X_1, \dots, X_n$, the first $n$ terms are known and the other $N - n$ have mean $\frac{1}{2}$ each, by independence:

$$M_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n + \frac{N - n}{2}$$

The increment is $D_n = M_n - M_{n-1} = X_n - \frac{1}{2} \in \lbrace -\frac{1}{2}, +\frac{1}{2} \rbrace$. So $A_n = -\frac{1}{2}$ and the range is $c_n = 1$, for every $n$. Then $\sum c_n^2 = N$, $M_0 = \frac{N}{2}$ and $M_N = Z$. Azuma-Hoeffding gives:

$$P\Big( Z - \frac{N}{2} \ge t \Big) \le \exp\Big( -\frac{2 t^2}{N} \Big)$$

This is Hoeffding's inequality for coin flips ([16.7](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)). For 100 flips and $t = 10$ it is $e^{-2} \approx 0.135$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Throw 100 keys into 100 buckets (200,000 trials). Estimate $P(N - E[N] \ge t)$ for $t = 5, 10, 15$, where $N$ counts the empty buckets, and compare with the McDiarmid bound.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_keys, n_buckets, n_trials = 100, 100, 200_000
buckets = rng.integers(0, n_buckets, size=(n_trials, n_keys))     # bucket of each key
empty = np.array([n_buckets - np.unique(row).size for row in buckets])
mean_exact = n_buckets * (1 - 1 / n_buckets) ** n_keys
print(f"E[N] = {empty.mean():.3f} (exact {mean_exact:.3f})")
for t in [5, 10, 15]:
    print(f"t = {t:>2}: P(N - E[N] >= t) = {np.mean(empty - mean_exact >= t):.5f}, "
          f"McDiarmid bound {np.exp(-2 * t**2 / n_keys):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[N] = 36.611 (exact 36.603)
t =  5: P(N - E[N] >= t) = 0.05758, McDiarmid bound 0.6065
t = 10: P(N - E[N] >= t) = 0.00064, McDiarmid bound 0.1353
t = 15: P(N - E[N] >= t) = 0.00001, McDiarmid bound 0.0111
```

The bound always holds and has the right shape (it falls like $e^{-2t^2/100}$), but it is far above the true probability, because the actual standard deviation of $N$ (about 3.1) is smaller than the worst case that McDiarmid allows.

</details>

## Summary

- **Conditional expectation given a $\sigma$-algebra** $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the $\mathcal{G}$-measurable variable with the right average on every event of $\mathcal{G}$. It has the tower, take-out and independence rules.
- A **martingale** has $E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n$: no predictable drift. Sub- and supermartingales model favorable and unfavorable games.
- Standard martingales: the fair walk, $S_n^2 - n\sigma^2$, $(q/p)^{S_n}$, products of mean-1 factors, and Doob martingales $E[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$.
- **No betting system beats a fair game:** the transform $\sum H_k (M_k - M_{k-1})$ of a martingale by a predictable stake is a martingale with mean 0.
- **Azuma-Hoeffding and McDiarmid:** bounded martingale steps give $\exp\left( -\frac{2t^2}{\sum c_k^2} \right)$ concentration. Bounded martingales converge almost surely.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n}
$$
