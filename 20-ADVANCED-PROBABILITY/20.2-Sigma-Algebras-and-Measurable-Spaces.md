# 20.2 Sigma-Algebras and Measurable Spaces

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.2 Sigma-Algebras and Measurable Spaces

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [20.1 Why Measure Theory](20.1-Why-Measure-Theory.md)

> **Big idea:** A $\sigma$-algebra is a collection of sets that is closed under complements and countable unions, and it is exactly the list of questions you are allowed to ask, and can answer, about an experiment.

---

## Why It Matters

[20.1](20.1-Why-Measure-Theory.md) showed that we cannot give every subset of $[0, 1]$ a probability, so we must choose a list of allowed events. The list must be rich enough that the opposite of $A$, the event $A$ or $B$, and the event $A_1$ or $A_2$ or $A_3$ or so on are again allowed. A collection with this property is a $\sigma$-algebra. It also has a second meaning that you will use all through Modules 14 and 20: it models **information**. A bigger $\sigma$-algebra means more is known. Conditional expectations, filtrations and martingales are all built on this idea.

## Intuition

Roll a fair die and suppose you cannot see it. A friend looks and tells you only whether the number is **odd or even**. What can you now say for sure? You can answer these questions: "Is it odd?", "Is it even?", "Is it odd or even?" (always yes), and "Is it both?" (never). You cannot answer "Is it 6?".

The questions you can answer are the four sets $\varnothing$, $\lbrace 1, 3, 5 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$ and $\lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$. Check the closure: the opposite of "odd" is "even" (a complement), and "odd or even" is a union. You never leave this list. That closed list is a $\sigma$-algebra.

If your friend tells you the exact number, you can answer every question, which is all $2^6 = 64$ subsets. If your friend tells you nothing, you can answer only the two trivial questions "anything?" (yes) and "nothing?" (no). More information gives a bigger list.

For a computer scientist: a **hash function** that sends each outcome to a bucket tells you exactly "which bucket?". The questions you can answer from the hash are the unions of buckets.

## Picture

Three levels of information about a die. A vertical bar `|` separates the groups that are not told apart:

```text
  Omega = {1,2,3,4,5,6}

  no information    |  1  2  3  4  5  6  |           2 events
                                                      (empty set, Omega)
  parity only       |  1  3  5  |  2  4  6  |         4 events
                                                      (unions of 2 blocks)
  the exact number  | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |         64 events
                                                      (unions of 6 blocks)

  More bars = finer information = more events you can ask about.
```

On a finite set, every $\sigma$-algebra is the collection of all unions of the blocks of a partition. With $k$ blocks you get $2^k$ events.

## Definition

Let $\Omega$ be a set (the sample space). A collection $\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a **$\sigma$-algebra** (read "sigma-algebra", also called a $\sigma$-field) if:

1. $\Omega \in \mathcal{F}$;
2. if $A \in \mathcal{F}$ then $A^c \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under complements);
3. if $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots \in \mathcal{F}$ then $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under countable unions).

**In words:** the whole space is allowed, the opposite of any allowed event is allowed, and the union of any countable list of allowed events is allowed.

The pair $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is a **measurable space**. The sets in $\mathcal{F}$ are called **measurable sets**, or **events**. Probability is only defined for events.

Here are the main examples:

| Collection on $\Omega$ | Is a $\sigma$-algebra? | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $\lbrace \varnothing, \Omega \rbrace$ | yes (the smallest) | no information |
| $\lbrace \varnothing, A, A^c, \Omega \rbrace$ | yes | you only know whether $A$ happened |
| all subsets of $\Omega$ (the power set) | yes (the largest) | complete information; used when $\Omega$ is finite or countable |
| all unions of blocks of a finite or countable partition | yes | information from a grouping, such as parity |

**Generated $\sigma$-algebra.** For any collection $\mathcal{C}$ of subsets, $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is the **smallest** $\sigma$-algebra that contains $\mathcal{C}$. It is the list of all questions you can build from the questions in $\mathcal{C}$ by "not" and countable "or".

**Borel $\sigma$-algebra.** On the real line, the **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all open intervals. Its sets are the **Borel sets**. It contains every set you can build from intervals by complements and countable unions and intersections. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ we use $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, generated by open boxes.

**In words:** Borel sets are all the "reasonable" subsets of the real line. Vitali's set is not one of them.

## Why It Is True

**Fact 1: more rules come for free.** Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a $\sigma$-algebra.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varnothing &= \Omega^c \in \mathcal{F} && \text{(rules 1 and 2)} \\
A \cup B &= A \cup B \cup \varnothing \cup \varnothing \cup \cdots \in \mathcal{F} && \text{(rule 3 with a list that ends in } \varnothing \text{)} \\
\bigcap_{n} A_n &= \Big( \bigcup_{n} A_n^c \Big)^c \in \mathcal{F} && \text{(De Morgan's law, rules 2 and 3)} \\
A \setminus B &= A \cap B^c \in \mathcal{F} && \text{(intersection and complement)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under finite and countable unions and intersections, complements and differences.

**Fact 2: the generated $\sigma$-algebra exists.** The intersection of any family of $\sigma$-algebras on $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra. Here is why. Let $\mathcal{F} = \bigcap_i \mathcal{F}_i$. The set $\Omega$ is in every $\mathcal{F}_i$, so it is in $\mathcal{F}$. If $A \in \mathcal{F}$ then $A \in \mathcal{F}_i$ for every $i$, so $A^c \in \mathcal{F}_i$ for every $i$, so $A^c \in \mathcal{F}$. Countable unions are the same. Now define $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ as the intersection of **all** $\sigma$-algebras that contain $\mathcal{C}$. (There is at least one, the power set.) It is a $\sigma$-algebra, contains $\mathcal{C}$, and sits inside every other $\sigma$-algebra that contains $\mathcal{C}$. So it is the smallest.

> **Note:** Unions of two $\sigma$-algebras are **not** $\sigma$-algebras in general. Always take the generated one: $\sigma(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2)$.

**Fact 3: a finite $\sigma$-algebra is a partition.** Let $\Omega$ be finite and $\mathcal{F}$ a $\sigma$-algebra on it. For each outcome $\omega$ let $B_{\omega}$ be the intersection of all sets of $\mathcal{F}$ that contain $\omega$. It is in $\mathcal{F}$ (a finite intersection), and it is the smallest event that contains $\omega$. These sets are the **atoms** of $\mathcal{F}$. We show that two atoms are equal or disjoint.

1. Suppose $\omega' \in B_{\omega}$. Then $B_{\omega}$ is an event that contains $\omega'$, so $B_{\omega'} \subseteq B_{\omega}$.
2. Suppose also that $\omega \notin B_{\omega'}$. Then $B_{\omega} \setminus B_{\omega'}$ is an event that contains $\omega$, so $B_{\omega} \subseteq B_{\omega} \setminus B_{\omega'}$. But $\omega' \in B_{\omega}$ and $\omega' \in B_{\omega'}$, so $\omega'$ is not in $B_{\omega} \setminus B_{\omega'}$. This is a contradiction. So $\omega \in B_{\omega'}$, hence $B_{\omega} \subseteq B_{\omega'}$, and the two atoms are equal.
3. If two atoms share a point $x$, then both equal $B_x$ by steps 1 and 2. So atoms are equal or disjoint.

Every event $A$ is the union of the atoms $B_{\omega}$ for $\omega \in A$, because $B_{\omega} \subseteq A$ for each such $\omega$. So the atoms are a partition, and every event is a union of atoms. With $k$ atoms there are $2^k$ events.

**Fact 4: the Borel sets include every kind of interval.** The set $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is closed under countable unions and complements, so:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a, b] &= \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a, b + \tfrac{1}{n} \right) && \text{(countable intersection of open intervals)} \\
\lbrace a \rbrace &= \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a - \tfrac{1}{n}, a + \tfrac{1}{n} \right) && \text{(a point is a Borel set)} \\
\lbrace c_1, c_2, \dots \rbrace &= \bigcup_{i} \lbrace c_i \rbrace && \text{(every countable set is Borel)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Closed intervals are complements of open sets, so they are Borel too. Half-lines such as $(-\infty, x]$ are Borel. And these half-lines alone are enough:

$$
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) = \sigma\big( \lbrace (-\infty, x] : x \in \mathbb{R} \rbrace \big)
$$

Why: $(a, b] = (-\infty, b] \setminus (-\infty, a]$ and $(a, b) = \bigcup_n (a, b - \frac{1}{n}]$ are in the right-hand side. Every open set is a countable union of open intervals with rational endpoints, so every open set is in it. The other direction holds because each half-line is closed, hence Borel. This is why the CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ determines everything about a random variable ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md) and [20.4](20.4-Random-Variables-as-Measurable-Functions.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Sigma-Algebra Generated by Two Events

**Problem.** Roll a die. Let $A = \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace$. List $\sigma(\lbrace A, B \rbrace)$.

**Solution.**

1. The atoms are the smallest events we can form. Look at which of $A$ and $B$ each face is in:

| Faces | in $A$? | in $B$? | Atom |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | yes | no | $\lbrace 1 \rbrace$ ($A \setminus B$) |
| 2 | yes | yes | $\lbrace 2 \rbrace$ ($A \cap B$) |
| 3 | no | yes | $\lbrace 3 \rbrace$ ($B \setminus A$) |
| 4, 5, 6 | no | no | $\lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$ ($(A \cup B)^c$) |

2. There are 4 atoms, so there are $2^4 = 16$ events: all unions of atoms.
3. For example, $\lbrace 1, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace = \lbrace 1 \rbrace \cup \lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$ is in the list, but $\lbrace 5 \rbrace$ is not.

**Answer.** $\sigma(\lbrace A, B \rbrace)$ has 16 events, built from the atoms $\lbrace 1 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 2 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 3 \rbrace$, $\lbrace 4, 5, 6 \rbrace$.

**Check.** You can tell "1", "2", "3" apart, but you cannot tell 4 from 5 from 6. The program below finds the same 16 sets.

### Example 2: Which Collections Are Sigma-Algebras

**Problem.** Decide for each collection whether it is a $\sigma$-algebra.

(a) On $\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$: $\mathcal{F}_1 = \lbrace \varnothing, \lbrace 1 \rbrace, \lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace, \Omega \rbrace$.

(b) On $\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$: $\mathcal{F}_2 = \lbrace \varnothing, \lbrace 1 \rbrace, \lbrace 2 \rbrace, \Omega \rbrace$.

(c) On $\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, \dots \rbrace$: $\mathcal{F}_3$ = all sets that are finite or have a finite complement.

**Solution.**

1. (a) $\Omega \in \mathcal{F}_1$. The complement of $\lbrace 1 \rbrace$ is $\lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}_1$, and the complement of $\varnothing$ is $\Omega$. Every union of two members is a member. So it is a $\sigma$-algebra (the partition $\lbrace 1 \rbrace, \lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace$).
2. (b) The complement of $\lbrace 1 \rbrace$ is $\lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace$, which is not in $\mathcal{F}_2$. Rule 2 fails.
3. (c) Rules 1 and 2 hold (the complement of a finite set is cofinite, and the other way round). But take $A_n = \lbrace 2n \rbrace$. Each $A_n$ is finite, and $\bigcup_n A_n$ is the set of all even numbers, which is infinite with an infinite complement. Rule 3 fails.

**Answer.** (a) yes, (b) no, (c) no.

**Check.** In (c) the collection is closed under **finite** unions only. Such a collection is called an **algebra**. The letter $\sigma$ in the name $\sigma$-algebra stands for countable unions. Algebra is not enough for limits, which is why we need rule 3.

### Example 3: A 2-Bit Sensor

**Problem.** A voltage $V$ in $[0, 1)$ is read by a 2-bit converter that reports which quarter $[0, 0.25)$, $[0.25, 0.5)$, $[0.5, 0.75)$, $[0.75, 1)$ the voltage is in. Which of these events can you decide from the reading: $E_1 = \lbrace V \ge 0.5 \rbrace$, $E_2 = \lbrace V \ge 0.6 \rbrace$? How many events can you decide in total?

**Solution.**

1. The reading splits $[0, 1)$ into 4 atoms. A decidable event is a union of atoms.
2. $E_1 = [0.5, 0.75) \cup [0.75, 1)$ is a union of two atoms, so it is decidable.
3. $E_2 = [0.6, 1)$ cuts the atom $[0.5, 0.75)$ in two, so it is not a union of atoms. The reading $[0.5, 0.75)$ does not tell whether $V \ge 0.6$.
4. With 4 atoms, the number of decidable events is $2^4 = 16$.

**Answer.** $E_1$ is decidable, $E_2$ is not, and there are 16 decidable events.

**Check.** A finer converter (3 bits, 8 atoms) has 256 decidable events and still cannot decide $E_2$, since 0.6 is not a multiple of $\frac{1}{8}$. Only with infinitely many bits could you decide whether $V \ge 0.6$.

## Simulate It

The code lists **every** collection of subsets of a 4-point set, keeps those that obey the three rules, and counts them. Then it builds $\sigma(\lbrace A, B \rbrace)$ from Example 1 by adding complements and unions until nothing new appears.

```python
# Part 1: count ALL sigma-algebras on a 4-point set (finite, so "countable unions" = finite unions).
n = 4
full = (1 << n) - 1                           # the subset Omega, written as a bit pattern
count = 0
for family in range(1 << (1 << n)):           # every collection of subsets: 2^16 of them
    sets = [s for s in range(1 << n) if family >> s & 1]
    members = set(sets)
    if full not in members:
        continue
    if any((full ^ s) not in members for s in sets):          # closed under complement?
        continue
    if any((a | b) not in members for a in sets for b in sets):  # closed under union?
        continue
    count += 1
print("sigma-algebras on 4 points:", count)

# Part 2: the sigma-algebra generated by {1,2} and {2,3} on a die (faces 1..6).
omega = frozenset(range(1, 7))
sigma = {frozenset(), omega, frozenset({1, 2}), frozenset({2, 3})}
changed = True
while changed:                                 # keep adding complements and unions
    changed = False
    for a in list(sigma):
        for b in list(sigma) + [omega - a]:
            for new in (omega - a, a | b):
                if new not in sigma:
                    sigma.add(new)
                    changed = True
print("size of generated sigma-algebra:", len(sigma))
atoms = sorted(sorted(s) for s in sigma if s and not any(t < s and t for t in sigma))
print("atoms:", atoms)
```

Output:

```text
sigma-algebras on 4 points: 15
size of generated sigma-algebra: 16
atoms: [[1], [2], [3], [4, 5, 6]]
```

There are exactly 15 $\sigma$-algebras on a 4-point set. By Fact 3 each one is a partition of the 4 points, and there are 15 partitions of a 4-element set (the Bell number $B_4 = 15$). The generated $\sigma$-algebra has 16 events and the four atoms from Example 1.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting that $\Omega$ and $\varnothing$ must be in the collection.** Rule 1 gives $\Omega$, and rule 2 then gives $\varnothing$.
- **Closing only under finite unions.** An algebra is not a $\sigma$-algebra (Example 2c). Rule 3 needs countably infinite unions.
- **Asking for uncountable unions.** A $\sigma$-algebra need not be closed under uncountable unions. If it were, the Borel sets would include every union of points, so every subset of $\mathbb{R}$. We would be back to the power set and Vitali's problem.
- **Thinking $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is all subsets of $\mathbb{R}$.** It is smaller. The power set of $\mathbb{R}$ has strictly more sets than the Borel sets do.
- **Taking the union of two $\sigma$-algebras.** It is usually not closed under unions of its sets. Use $\sigma(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2)$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Probability spaces:** the collection of events $\mathcal{F}$ in $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)).
- **Random variables:** $\sigma(X)$ is the information in $X$ ([20.4](20.4-Random-Variables-as-Measurable-Functions.md)), and conditioning on $X$ means conditioning on $\sigma(X)$ ([14.2](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md)).
- **Stochastic processes and reinforcement learning:** a **filtration** is an increasing list of $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \cdots$: what the agent knows at time $0, 1, 2, \dots$ ([20.8](20.8-Martingales.md)).
- **Computer science:** a hash function, a feature map or a quantizer defines a partition, and its $\sigma$-algebra is the set of questions answerable from the output.
- **Finance:** the information available at time $t$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, and a trading rule may use only that.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Is $\lbrace \varnothing, \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace, \lbrace 3, 4 \rbrace, \lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4 \rbrace \rbrace$ a $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4 \rbrace$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Yes. It contains $\Omega$. The complement of $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$ is $\lbrace 3, 4 \rbrace$ and the other way round, and the complement of $\varnothing$ is $\Omega$. The union of $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$ and $\lbrace 3, 4 \rbrace$ is $\Omega$. It is the $\sigma$-algebra of the partition $\lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace, \lbrace 3, 4 \rbrace$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** On $\Omega = \lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$, list $\sigma(\lbrace \lbrace 1 \rbrace \rbrace)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The generator $\lbrace 1 \rbrace$ forces its complement $\lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace$, and $\varnothing$ and $\Omega$:

$$\sigma(\lbrace \lbrace 1 \rbrace \rbrace) = \lbrace \varnothing, \lbrace 1 \rbrace, \lbrace 2, 3 \rbrace, \Omega \rbrace$$

These four sets already obey all three rules, so nothing else is added.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Show that the collection of all subsets of a finite set $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra. Then show that if $\mathcal{F}$ has $k$ atoms, then $\mathcal{F}$ has $2^k$ events.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the power set. Then $\Omega \subseteq \Omega$, so $\Omega \in \mathcal{F}$. If $A \subseteq \Omega$ then $A^c \subseteq \Omega$. A union of subsets of $\Omega$ is a subset of $\Omega$. So all three rules hold.

For the count: every event is a union of atoms (Fact 3), and different choices of atoms give different unions, because atoms are disjoint and non-empty. Each atom is either used or not:

$$2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2 = 2^k$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Prove that a $\sigma$-algebra is closed under countable intersections and under set differences.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}$. Their complements are in $\mathcal{F}$ (rule 2), so their union is in $\mathcal{F}$ (rule 3), so the complement of that union is in $\mathcal{F}$ (rule 2). By De Morgan's law:

$$\bigcap_{n} A_n = \Big( \bigcup_{n} A_n^c \Big)^c \in \mathcal{F}$$

For the difference, $A \setminus B = A \cap B^c$ is the intersection of two members of $\mathcal{F}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** On an uncountable set $\Omega$ (for example $\mathbb{R}$), let $\mathcal{C}$ be the collection of sets that are countable or whose complement is countable. Show that $\mathcal{C}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, that $\mathcal{C} = \sigma(\text{all singletons})$, and that $(0, 1) \notin \mathcal{C}$. What does this say about $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\Omega \in \mathcal{C}$ since its complement $\varnothing$ is countable. If $A \in \mathcal{C}$ then $A^c \in \mathcal{C}$ by the definition (the roles of $A$ and $A^c$ swap). For a list $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{C}$: if all are countable, the union is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. If some $A_j$ has a countable complement, then $\big(\bigcup_n A_n\big)^c \subseteq A_j^c$ is countable. Either way the union is in $\mathcal{C}$. So $\mathcal{C}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra.

Every singleton is in $\mathcal{C}$, so $\sigma(\text{singletons}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}$. Conversely, a countable set is a countable union of singletons, and a set with a countable complement is the complement of such a union. So $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \sigma(\text{singletons})$.

The interval $(0, 1)$ is not countable, and its complement $(-\infty, 0] \cup [1, \infty)$ is not countable. So $(0, 1) \notin \mathcal{C}$.

So "knowing every single point" does not give you intervals. $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is strictly bigger than $\mathcal{C}$: it needs intervals as generators, and it contains $(0, 1)$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Count the $\sigma$-algebras on $n = 1, 2, 3$ points by brute force. Compare with the number of partitions of an $n$-point set.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
for n in [1, 2, 3]:
    full = (1 << n) - 1
    count = 0
    for family in range(1 << (1 << n)):          # all collections of subsets, as bit masks
        sets = [s for s in range(1 << n) if family >> s & 1]
        ok = full in sets
        ok = ok and all((full ^ s) in sets for s in sets)          # complements
        ok = ok and all((a | b) in sets for a in sets for b in sets)   # unions
        count += ok
    print(f"n = {n}: {count} sigma-algebras")
```

Output:

```text
n = 1: 1 sigma-algebras
n = 2: 2 sigma-algebras
n = 3: 5 sigma-algebras
```

The numbers 1, 2, 5 are the numbers of partitions of a set with 1, 2, 3 elements (the Bell numbers). With $n = 4$ we found 15 above.

</details>

## Summary

- A **$\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{F}$ contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions. It is then also closed under countable intersections and differences.
- A **measurable space** $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is a set together with its allowed events. The sets of $\mathcal{F}$ are the measurable sets or events.
- $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra that contains $\mathcal{C}$. It always exists: it is the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras that contain $\mathcal{C}$.
- On a finite set, a $\sigma$-algebra is the same thing as a partition: its events are the unions of the blocks, and it describes **information**.
- The Borel $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is generated by open intervals, or just by the half-lines $(-\infty, x]$. It contains all points, countable sets, intervals and open and closed sets.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\Omega \in \mathcal{F}, \qquad A \in \mathcal{F} \implies A^c \in \mathcal{F}, \qquad A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F} \implies \bigcup_{n} A_n \in \mathcal{F}}
$$
