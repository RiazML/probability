# 20.7 Kolmogorov's Zero-One Law

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.7 Kolmogorov's Zero-One Law

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [20.6 Borel-Cantelli Lemmas](20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)

> **Big idea:** For independent random variables, any event that does not depend on the first $n$ variables, for every $n$ (a tail event), has probability exactly 0 or exactly 1, because it is independent of itself.

---

## Why It Matters

Many long-run questions are of the type: does a series converge, does an average settle, does something happen infinitely often? Such an event cannot depend on any finite number of terms. Kolmogorov's zero-one law says that for independent variables the answer is then never "maybe": the probability is 0 or 1. This explains why the Strong Law has a constant limit, why the Borel-Cantelli lemmas ([20.6](20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)) have only two possible answers, and why a random series either converges or diverges, with no middle case. The law uses independence in an essential way, and its proof is a beautiful use of the uniqueness theorem of [20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md).

## Intuition

Flip a fair coin forever, getting signs $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \dots \in \lbrace -1, +1 \rbrace$. Consider the random series

$$
\frac{\varepsilon_1}{1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon_3}{3} + \cdots
$$

Does it converge? Suppose someone secretly changes the first 100 signs. The first 100 terms add up to a different finite number, but the question "does the series converge?" has the same answer. Convergence is decided by the far tail of the sequence only.

Now the surprise. Because the flips are independent, the far tail is independent of the first 100 flips, for every 100. But the tail event "the series converges" is also fully decided by the flips from number 101 on, and also from 1001 on, and so on. Putting all this together, the event is independent of **everything**, including itself. An event $A$ with $P(A \cap A) = P(A) P(A)$ satisfies $P(A) = P(A)^2$, so $P(A)$ is 0 or 1.

## Picture

For each $n$, split the sequence into the first $n$ variables and the rest. A tail event is decided by "the rest" for **every** $n$:

```text
  X_1  X_2  X_3  ...  X_n | X_{n+1}  X_{n+2}  X_{n+3}  ...
  \_____ the past ______/   \_______ the future after n _____/

  Tail events live in the future after n  for  n = 1, 2, 3, ...

  future after 1:   X_2  X_3  X_4  X_5  ...      (A is decided here)
  future after 2:        X_3  X_4  X_5  ...      (and here)
  future after 3:             X_4  X_5  ...      (and here ...)
```

A tail event ignores any finite beginning, so it is independent of every finite beginning, and so of the whole sequence.

## Definition

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be random variables on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$. Let $\sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, \dots)$ be the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all $X_k$ with $k \ge n$ ([20.4](20.4-Random-Variables-as-Measurable-Functions.md)). The **tail $\sigma$-algebra** is

$$
\mathcal{T} = \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, \dots)
$$

An event $A \in \mathcal{T}$ is a **tail event**.

**In words:** a tail event is an event that is decided by the variables from number $n$ on, for every $n$. Changing finitely many of the variables never changes whether it happens.

**Theorem (Kolmogorov's zero-one law).** If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are **independent**, then every tail event $A$ has $P(A) = 0$ or $P(A) = 1$.

**In words:** for independent variables, a question about the far future has a sure answer, yes or no, but the theorem does not tell you which.

Examples of tail events, and of events that are not:

| Event | Tail event? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\lbrace \sum_n X_n \text{ converges} \rbrace$ | yes | the series converges if and only if the series from $k$ on converges |
| $\lbrace X_n \ge 1 \text{ infinitely often} \rbrace$ | yes | finitely many terms do not matter |
| $\lbrace \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i \le n} X_i \to 0 \rbrace$ | yes | changing the first $k$ terms adds a constant to $S_n$, and the constant divided by $n$ tends to 0 |
| $\lbrace \limsup_n X_n \gt 3 \rbrace$ | yes | the limsup ignores finitely many terms |
| $\lbrace X_1 \gt 0 \rbrace$ | no | decided by the first variable only |
| $\lbrace \sum_n X_n \text{ converges to a number } \gt 2 \rbrace$ | no | the value of the sum depends on the first terms |
| $\lbrace \sup_n X_n \gt 5 \rbrace$ | no | the first variable alone can make it true |

## Why It Is True

We use the uniqueness theorem of [20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md) twice.

**Step 1: the past is independent of the future.** For each $n$, the $\sigma$-algebras $\sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ and $\sigma(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots)$ are independent. Here is why. Fix $A \in \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$. For every $m$ and $B \in \sigma(X_{n+1}, \dots, X_{n+m})$, we have $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$, because $A$ and $B$ are decided by two different groups of independent variables. The union $\bigcup_m \sigma(X_{n+1}, \dots, X_{n+m})$ is closed under intersections (it is an increasing union of $\sigma$-algebras, so any two of its sets lie in a common one), so it is a $\pi$-system, and it generates $\sigma(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots)$. The two set functions $B \mapsto P(A \cap B)$ and $B \mapsto P(A) P(B)$ are finite measures with the same total mass $P(A)$, and they agree on this $\pi$-system. By the uniqueness theorem they agree on all of $\sigma(X_{n+1}, \dots)$.

**Step 2: a tail event is independent of everything.** Let $A \in \mathcal{T}$. For every $n$, $A \in \sigma(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots)$, so by Step 1 the event $A$ is independent of $\sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$. So $A$ is independent of every set in $\bigcup_n \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$. This union is a $\pi$-system (increasing union again) that generates $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$. By the same uniqueness argument ($B \mapsto P(A \cap B)$ against $B \mapsto P(A) P(B)$), $A$ is independent of every set in $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$.

**Step 3: independent of itself.** The event $A$ itself belongs to $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$, so $A$ is independent of $A$:

$$
P(A) = P(A \cap A) = P(A) P(A) = P(A)^2 \implies P(A) \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace
$$

**Corollary: tail variables are constant.** Let $Y$ be a random variable that is determined by the tail, such as $Y = \limsup_n X_n$ or $Y = \limsup_n \frac{S_n}{n}$ (it may take the values $\pm \infty$). For each $x$, the event $\lbrace Y \le x \rbrace$ is a tail event, so $P(Y \le x) \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$. The CDF of $Y$ jumps from 0 to 1 at one point $c$ (possibly $\pm \infty$), so $Y = c$ almost surely.

**Borel-Cantelli, again.** If $A_n$ are independent events, then $\lbrace A_n \text{ i.o.} \rbrace$ is a tail event (finitely many terms do not matter). So $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$ already follows from the zero-one law. The lemmas of [20.6](20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md) tell you **which** of the two values it is, by looking at $\sum_n P(A_n)$.

**Which value? Random series.** The zero-one law says that $\sum_n \varepsilon_n a_n$ (random signs, numbers $a_n$) converges with probability 0 or 1. It does not say which. The classical answer (Kolmogorov and Khinchin, stated here without proof; the convergence half is proved with martingales in [20.8](20.8-Martingales.md)) is:

$$
\sum_n \varepsilon_n a_n \text{ converges almost surely if } \sum_n a_n^2 \lt \infty, \text{ and diverges almost surely if } \sum_n a_n^2 = \infty
$$

**In words:** with random signs, a series converges when the squares of its terms have a finite sum. So $\sum \frac{\varepsilon_n}{n}$ (the random harmonic series) converges, because $\sum \frac{1}{n^2} \lt \infty$, but $\sum \frac{\varepsilon_n}{\sqrt{n}}$ diverges, because $\sum \frac{1}{n} = \infty$. The ordinary harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges, but random signs rescue it.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Which Events Are Tail Events?

**Problem.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be the flips of a fair coin (1 for heads, 0 for tails), and $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$. For each event, decide whether it is a tail event, and what its probability is.

(a) $\lbrace S_n / n \to \frac{1}{2} \rbrace$. (b) $\lbrace X_1 = 1 \rbrace$. (c) $\lbrace$ there are infinitely many $n$ with $X_n = X_{n+1} = 1 \rbrace$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) Change the first $k$ flips. Then $S_n$ changes by a number between $-k$ and $k$, and $\frac{S_n}{n}$ changes by at most $\frac{k}{n} \to 0$. So convergence to $\frac{1}{2}$ is not affected. It is a tail event. By the Strong Law ([17.4](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)), its probability is 1.
2. (b) The event is decided by the first flip alone. It is not a tail event. Its probability is $\frac{1}{2}$, which is allowed, because the zero-one law does not apply.
3. (c) Dropping any finite number of flips cannot make the event true or false, so it is a tail event. Its probability is 0 or 1. To find which, let $B_k$ be the event $X_{2k-1} = X_{2k} = 1$ (flips in disjoint pairs). The $B_k$ are independent with $P(B_k) = \frac{1}{4}$, so $\sum P(B_k) = \infty$ and by the second Borel-Cantelli lemma ([20.6](20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)) infinitely many $B_k$ happen almost surely. So the probability is 1.

**Answer.** (a) tail, probability 1. (b) not tail, probability $\frac{1}{2}$. (c) tail, probability 1.

**Check.** In (a) and (c) the probability is 0 or 1, as the law predicts. In (b) the probability $\frac{1}{2}$ is possible exactly because the event is not a tail event.

### Example 2: Independence Cannot Be Dropped

**Problem.** Let $X$ be a fair coin flip (0 or 1) and put $X_n = X$ for all $n$. Find a tail event with probability $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Solution.**

1. The event $A = \lbrace X_n = 1 \text{ for all large } n \rbrace$ is a tail event (it ignores finitely many terms).
2. Here every $X_n$ equals $X$, so $A = \lbrace X = 1 \rbrace$.
3. $P(A) = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Answer.** $P(A) = \frac{1}{2}$, neither 0 nor 1.

**Check.** The variables $X_n$ are not independent: all of them are the same coin. The proof of the theorem fails at Step 1, since $\sigma(X_1)$ and $\sigma(X_2, X_3, \dots)$ are the same $\sigma$-algebra.

### Example 3: The Random Harmonic Series

**Problem.** The series $S = \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{\varepsilon_n}{n}$ converges almost surely. What are the mean and variance of $S$? And what happens with $\sum \frac{\varepsilon_n}{\sqrt{n}}$?

**Solution.**

1. Each term has mean 0 and variance $\frac{1}{n^2}$. Terms are independent, so variances add. For the partial sum $S_N$: $E[S_N] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_N) = \sum_{n \le N} \frac{1}{n^2}$.
2. Since $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} \approx 1.645$, and the limit keeps these values (the partial sums are Cauchy in mean square), $E[S] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S) = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{1.645} \approx 1.283$.
3. For $\sum \frac{\varepsilon_n}{\sqrt{n}}$ the variance of the partial sum is $\sum_{n \le N} \frac{1}{n} \approx \ln N + 0.577$, which grows without bound, and the series diverges almost surely.
4. Cauchy test: the block $S_{4N} - S_N$ has variance $\sum_{N \lt n \le 4N} \frac{1}{n^2} \approx \frac{3}{4N} \to 0$ for the first series, but variance $\sum_{N \lt n \le 4N} \frac{1}{n} \approx \ln 4 = 1.386$ for the second. Blocks that do not shrink prevent convergence.

**Answer.** $E[S] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(S) = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$. The $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ series diverges.

**Check.** The simulation below shows the blocks of the first series shrinking and the blocks of the second staying near $\sqrt{\ln 4} \approx 1.177$.

## Simulate It

The code simulates 500 random signs sequences. For $N = 100, 1000, 10000$ it measures the standard deviation of the block $S_{4N} - S_N$ for the series with terms $\frac{\varepsilon_n}{n}$ and $\frac{\varepsilon_n}{\sqrt{n}}$. A convergent series needs blocks that shrink to 0.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs = 500

# Random signs e_n = +1 or -1 (fair, independent). Compare the series sum e_n / n and sum e_n / sqrt(n).
# If a series converges, the block S_{4N} - S_N must shrink as N grows (Cauchy test).
print("   N   sd of block, 1/n (exact)   sd of block, 1/sqrt(n) (exact)")
for big_n in [100, 1_000, 10_000]:
    n = np.arange(big_n + 1, 4 * big_n + 1)            # the terms n = N+1, ..., 4N
    signs = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(n_runs, n.size))
    block_1 = (signs / n).sum(axis=1)
    block_2 = (signs / np.sqrt(n)).sum(axis=1)
    print(f"{big_n:>6}   {block_1.std():>9.4f} ({np.sqrt(np.sum(1 / n**2)):.4f})"
          f"   {block_2.std():>14.4f} ({np.sqrt(np.sum(1 / n)):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
   N   sd of block, 1/n (exact)   sd of block, 1/sqrt(n) (exact)
   100      0.0860 (0.0863)           1.1678 (1.1758)
  1000      0.0262 (0.0274)           1.1348 (1.1773)
 10000      0.0084 (0.0087)           1.1667 (1.1774)
```

For the series with $\frac{1}{n}$ the blocks shrink like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$: the tail of the series dies out, so the series converges. For $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ the blocks keep a standard deviation near 1.18, so the partial sums keep moving by about 1 unit, however far you go. The zero-one law says that only these two outcomes exist, and the variance test tells us which one holds.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that the law tells you which value.** It only says 0 or 1. You need another tool (Borel-Cantelli, the Strong Law, a variance test) to decide.
- **Using it without independence.** In Example 2 all variables are equal, and a tail event has probability $\frac{1}{2}$.
- **Calling an event a tail event when the first terms matter.** The events that the sum is positive, or that the maximum is at least 5, depend on the beginning. Check: if I change $X_1$ only, can the event change?
- **Mixing up convergence of a series with its value.** Whether $\sum X_n$ converges is a tail event (probability 0 or 1). The value of the sum is a random variable that depends on every term, and it is not constant.
- **Reading probability 0 as impossible.** A tail event of probability 0 can still happen (the coin can show heads forever).

## Where It Is Used

- **Strong Law of Large Numbers:** the limit of $\frac{S_n}{n}$, when it exists, is a tail variable, hence constant almost surely. This is why the limit of the sample mean is the number $\mu$ and not a random variable ([17.4](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).
- **Borel-Cantelli:** it reduces the question whether $A_n$ happens infinitely often to two possible answers ([20.6](20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md)).
- **Machine learning:** the limit of an average reward, or a statement such as "the stochastic training noise has a convergent sum", is a tail statement. It can only be certain or impossible, never a coin flip.
- **Random series and signals:** random-sign series, random Fourier series and sums of independent noise terms either converge or diverge almost surely.
- **Statistics:** asymptotic properties of a sequence of i.i.d. observations, such as a limit existing, are all-or-nothing.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** For a fair coin $X_1, X_2, \dots$, which events are tail events? (a) heads infinitely often, (b) the first flip is heads, (c) $S_n / n \to \frac{1}{2}$, (d) there is never a pair of heads in a row.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Tail: finitely many flips do not matter. (b) Not tail: it is decided by the first flip. (c) Tail: changing the first $k$ flips changes $\frac{S_n}{n}$ by at most $\frac{k}{n}$. (d) Not tail: changing the first two flips can create a pair of heads, so it depends on the beginning.

(For the record, (d) has probability 0, by the same blocks argument as in Example 1(c). The zero-one law is not needed for that.)

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $X_n = X$ for all $n$, where $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Show that $\lbrace X_n \gt 0 \text{ for all large } n \rbrace$ is a tail event with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Why does this not contradict the zero-one law?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The event is $\lbrace X \gt 0 \rbrace$, which has probability $\frac{1}{2}$. It is a tail event because it ignores finitely many terms. It does not contradict the law, because the variables $X_n$ are not independent: they are all equal.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and $A_n$ events with $A_n \in \sigma(X_n)$. Show that $\lbrace A_n \text{ i.o.} \rbrace$ is a tail event, and conclude that its probability is 0 or 1 even if $\sum P(A_n)$ is hard to compute.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For every $k$, we have $\limsup A_n = \bigcap_{N \ge k} \bigcup_{n \ge N} A_n$, because dropping the first $k - 1$ terms of the outer intersection does not change it (the unions $\bigcup_{n \ge N} A_n$ decrease in $N$). The right side is built from $A_n$ with $n \ge k$, so it is in $\sigma(X_k, X_{k+1}, \dots)$ for every $k$. So it is a tail event. The events $A_n$ are independent (they are decided by different independent variables), so the zero-one law gives $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with $E\lvert X_1 \rvert \lt \infty$. The Strong Law says $\frac{S_n}{n} \to \mu$ almost surely. Use the zero-one law to explain why the limit must be a constant if it exists.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $Y = \limsup_n \frac{S_n}{n}$. Changing the first $k$ terms changes $S_n$ by a bounded amount, and dividing by $n$ makes this change vanish, so $Y$ is determined by the tail. By the corollary, $Y$ is almost surely a constant $c$. If $\frac{S_n}{n}$ converges, its limit equals $Y$, so the limit is the constant $c$. The Strong Law identifies this constant as $\mu$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with $P(X_n = 1) = p$ and $P(X_n = 0) = 1 - p$. For $q \in [0, 1]$ let $A_q = \lbrace \frac{S_n}{n} \to q \rbrace$. Find $P(A_q)$ for all $p$ and $q$, and explain the connection with the zero-one law.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$A_q$ is a tail event, so its probability is 0 or 1. By the Strong Law, $\frac{S_n}{n} \to p$ almost surely. So $P(A_p) = 1$. If $q \ne p$, the events $A_q$ and $A_p$ are disjoint (a sequence cannot converge to two limits), so $P(A_q) \le 1 - P(A_p) = 0$.

So for each $p$ exactly one $q$ (namely $q = p$) has probability 1, and all the others have probability 0. A fair coin ($p = \frac{1}{2}$) and a biased coin ($p = 0.6$) are two probability measures on the same space of sequences, and the event $A_{1/2}$ has probability 1 under one and 0 under the other. Two such measures are called **mutually singular** ([20.10](20.10-Radon-Nikodym-and-Densities.md)).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate 1,000 random harmonic series $\sum_{n \le 10^5} \frac{\varepsilon_n}{n}$. Compare the mean and standard deviation of the sums with $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{6}}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_max, n_runs = 100_000, 1_000
n = np.arange(1, n_max + 1)
total = np.zeros(n_runs)
for r in range(n_runs):                                # one random harmonic series per run
    total[r] = (rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=n_max) / n).sum()
print(f"mean {total.mean():+.3f} (exact 0), sd {total.std():.4f} (exact {np.sqrt(np.pi**2 / 6):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
mean -0.016 (exact 0), sd 1.2621 (exact 1.2825)
```

The standard deviation of the 1,000 sums is within about one standard error of $\sqrt{\frac{\pi^2}{6}} = 1.2825$, and the mean is near 0.

</details>

## Summary

- The **tail $\sigma$-algebra** is $\mathcal{T} = \bigcap_n \sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, \dots)$. Tail events ignore any finite beginning.
- **Kolmogorov's zero-one law:** for independent $X_n$, every tail event has probability 0 or 1. The proof shows that a tail event is independent of itself.
- Tail random variables (such as $\limsup X_n$ or $\limsup \frac{S_n}{n}$) are almost surely constant.
- Convergence of a series, "infinitely often" events, and existence of the limit of an average are tail events. The law says the answer is all or nothing, and other tools say which.
- Independence is essential. Random signs make $\sum \frac{\varepsilon_n}{n}$ converge and $\sum \frac{\varepsilon_n}{\sqrt{n}}$ diverge.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{A \in \bigcap_{n} \sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, \dots), \ X_n \text{ independent} \implies P(A) \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace}
$$
