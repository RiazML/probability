# 20.1 Why Measure Theory

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.1 Why Measure Theory

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 19: Simulation and Monte Carlo](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)

> **Big idea:** On a finite sample space every subset can have a probability, but on $[0, 1]$ no rule for "length" can give every subset a length and keep the usual rules, so we must decide which sets are allowed, and that is what measure theory does.

---

## Why It Matters

Until now you gave a probability to every event you could think of, and nothing went wrong. This topic shows that this is a lucky accident of small examples. For a random point in $[0, 1]$ there are sets that **cannot** have a probability at all, and for "a random whole number" there is no fair rule even for easy sets. The rest of Module 20 is the repair: we choose a collection of allowed events ([20.2](20.2-Sigma-Algebras-and-Measurable-Spaces.md)), put a probability on it ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)), and then everything you learned in Modules 03 to 19 becomes exact. This module is optional for most readers, but it is what turns a user of probability into someone who can prove things.

## Intuition

Roll a fair die. There are 6 outcomes, and you can ask about any of the $2^6 = 64$ subsets: "even", "at least 5", "1 or 6", "nothing", "anything". Each subset gets the probability (size) $\div$ 6. The rules of probability hold for all 64 questions. No problem.

Now pick a random point $U$ in $[0, 1]$, as in [3.8 Geometric Probability](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.8-Geometric-Probability.md). The probability that $U$ lands in an interval is its length. We would like the same for **every** subset: each set $A \subseteq [0, 1]$ gets a "length" $P(A)$. We want four things:

1. An interval $[a, b]$ has length $b - a$.
2. **Countable additivity:** if $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are disjoint, the length of the union is the sum of the lengths (the same rule as in [3.2 Kolmogorov Axioms](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.2-Kolmogorov-Axioms.md)).
3. **Fairness:** sliding a set around a circle (adding a number and wrapping around at 1) does not change its length.
4. **Every** subset has a length.

Each wish is natural. Together they are impossible. A famous set built by Giuseppe Vitali in 1905 shows that you cannot have all four. In practice we give up wish 4: only "nice" sets get a length. All sets you meet in real problems (intervals, countable unions, limits of such sets) are nice.

A second warning comes from whole numbers. Can you pick a whole number "uniformly at random", so that every number is equally likely? Counting the share of numbers up to $N$ looks promising: half of the numbers are even, a third are multiples of 3. But we will see that it breaks countable additivity, so it is not a probability.

## Picture

The Vitali set $V$ and its copies. Each column is one point of $[0, 1)$. Each row is a copy of $V$ moved by a rational number $q$ (with wrap-around). Every column has exactly one `x`:

```text
  point of [0,1) ->    a    b    c    d    e    f    g    h    ...
  copy V          :    x    .    .    x    .    .    .    x
  copy V + q1     :    .    x    .    .    .    x    .    .
  copy V + q2     :    .    .    x    .    .    .    .    .
  copy V + q3     :    .    .    .    .    x    .    x    .
   ...                 (countably many rows, one for each rational q)

  Each column has exactly ONE x:  the rows are disjoint and cover [0,1).
  So  1 = length(V) + length(V+q1) + length(V+q2) + ...
        = (the same number) added countably many times.
  That number must be 0 (the sum would be 0) or positive (the sum is
  infinite). It can never be 1.
```

The copies of $V$ tile the circle, but all copies have the same length, and no number works.

## Definition

**Lebesgue measure** (the usual idea of "length") is a rule that gives a length $m(A)$ to the sets $A$ in a collection that we call the **measurable sets**. The next theorem says that this collection cannot contain every subset.

**Theorem (Vitali, 1905).** There is no function $m$ that gives every subset $A$ of $[0, 1)$ a number $m(A) \in [0, \infty]$ and satisfies all of these:

$$
m([0, 1)) = 1, \qquad m\Big( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \Big) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} m(A_n) \ \text{ for disjoint } A_n, \qquad m(A + q) = m(A)
$$

where $A + q = \lbrace x + q \text{ (wrapped around to stay in } [0, 1) \text{)} : x \in A \rbrace$ is the set $A$ moved by $q$ with wrap-around, which we call a **shift** of $A$.

**In words:** if every set has a length, and lengths add over countably many disjoint pieces, and moving a set around the circle does not change its length, then the length of the whole circle would not be 1.

The standard repair is this:

- keep only a smaller collection $\mathcal{F}$ of **measurable** sets, which is closed under complements and countable unions ([20.2](20.2-Sigma-Algebras-and-Measurable-Spaces.md));
- demand additivity only inside $\mathcal{F}$ ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)).

> **Note:** The Vitali set needs the **axiom of choice** (pick one number from each of uncountably many groups). Nobody can write down a formula for it. This is why you never meet it in an exam, and also why you cannot simulate it.

## Why It Is True

**Step 1: group the numbers.** Call $x$ and $y$ in $[0, 1)$ **equivalent** if $x - y$ is a rational number. This rule splits $[0, 1)$ into disjoint groups. For example $0.2$ and $0.7$ are in the same group, since $0.7 - 0.2 = 0.5$ is rational. But $0.2$ and $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ are not, since their difference is irrational.

**Step 2: choose one from each group.** Let $V$ be a set that contains exactly one number from each group. (This is the step that needs the axiom of choice.)

**Step 3: the copies are disjoint.** For each rational $q$ in $[0, 1)$ let $V_q = V + q$. Suppose $V_q$ and $V_{q'}$ share a point. Then $v + q = v' + q'$ (with wrap-around) for some $v, v' \in V$, so $v - v'$ is rational. Then $v$ and $v'$ are in the same group, and $V$ has only one number per group, so $v = v'$ and $q = q'$.

**Step 4: the copies cover everything.** Take any $x \in [0, 1)$. It is in some group, and $V$ has a number $v$ from that group. Then $x - v$ is rational, so $x = v + q$ (with wrap-around) for a rational $q$ in $[0, 1)$, namely $q = x - v$ if $x \ge v$ and $q = x - v + 1$ otherwise. So $x \in V_q$.

**Step 5: the contradiction.** There are countably many rationals $q_1, q_2, \dots$ in $[0, 1)$. Suppose $m$ exists. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 = m([0, 1)) &= m\Big( \bigcup_{n} V_{q_n} \Big) && \text{(Steps 3 and 4: the copies tile } [0, 1) \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} m(V_{q_n}) && \text{(countable additivity, disjoint copies)} \\
&= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} m(V) && \text{(moving does not change length)}
\end{aligned}
$$

If $m(V) = 0$ the sum is $0$. If $m(V) \gt 0$ the sum is $\infty$. Neither equals 1. So no such $m$ exists. This ends the proof.

> **Warning:** Look at which assumption we give up. We keep 1, 2 and 3 and drop 4. Giving up countable additivity would be worse: it breaks continuity of probability ([3.9](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.9-Continuity-of-Probability.md)) and with it every limit theorem.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Finite Space Has No Problem

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. How many events are there, and does the additivity rule hold for the events $A = \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$ and $B = \lbrace 5 \rbrace$?

**Solution.**

1. Each of the 6 outcomes is in a subset or not, so there are $2^6 = 64$ subsets.
2. $P(A) = \frac{2}{6}$ and $P(B) = \frac{1}{6}$. The events are disjoint, so $A \cup B = \lbrace 1, 2, 5 \rbrace$ has 3 outcomes.
3. $P(A \cup B) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = P(A) + P(B)$.

**Answer.** There are 64 events, and additivity holds for all of them.

**Check.** On a finite set, "countably many disjoint events" means finitely many non-empty ones, so the infinite rule is just the finite rule. Trouble starts only when $\Omega$ is infinite.

### Example 2: A Random Point Is Never Rational

**Problem.** Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$. Show that $P(U \text{ is rational}) = 0$, using only that lengths of intervals are their widths and that lengths add over countable unions.

**Solution.**

1. The rationals in $[0, 1]$ can be listed: $q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots$. (A countable set; see [1.9](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.9-Finite-Countable-and-Uncountable-Spaces.md).)
2. Pick any $\varepsilon \gt 0$. Put an interval $I_n$ of width $\frac{\varepsilon}{2^n}$ around $q_n$.
3. The rationals are inside $\bigcup_n I_n$. A set inside another set has a smaller or equal length, and the length of a union is at most the sum of the lengths, so:

$$
P(U \text{ is rational}) \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\varepsilon}{2^n} = \varepsilon \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} = \varepsilon \cdot 1 = \varepsilon
$$

4. This holds for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$, so $P(U \text{ is rational}) = 0$.

**Answer.** $P(U \text{ is rational}) = 0$, and so $P(U \text{ is irrational}) = 1$.

**Check.** The rationals are dense: between any two numbers there is a rational. Yet they have total length zero. This is why a continuous random variable never hits a fraction ([8.2](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.2-Why-Point-Probabilities-are-Zero.md)). The same argument works for any countable set.

### Example 3: No Uniform Whole Number

**Problem.** Show that there is no probability $P$ on the whole numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots$, with countable additivity, that gives every number the same probability.

**Solution.**

1. Suppose $P(\lbrace n \rbrace) = c$ for every $n$.
2. The whole numbers are the countable disjoint union of the singletons $\lbrace 1 \rbrace, \lbrace 2 \rbrace, \dots$. By countable additivity:

$$
1 = P(\text{all numbers}) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(\lbrace n \rbrace) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c
$$

3. If $c = 0$ the sum is $0$. If $c \gt 0$ the sum is $\infty$. Neither is 1.

**Answer.** No such $P$ exists.

**Check.** The share-of-numbers idea (the simulation below) gives each number the "probability" $0$, yet the shares of $\lbrace n \gt M \rbrace$ tend to 1 for every $M$. So the shares are not countably additive.

## Simulate It

We cannot simulate a Vitali set, but we can watch the second paradox. Define the **density** of a set $A$ of whole numbers by $d_N(A) = \frac{\text{how many of } 1, \dots, N \text{ are in } A}{N}$. It works for easy sets, but singletons get 0 and the tails get 1.

```python
import numpy as np

# "Pick a whole number at random": try d_N(A) = (how many of 1..N are in A) / N.
print("      N   d(even)  d(mult of 3)  d(even or mult of 3)  d({7})    d(n > 1000)")
for n_max in [1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    numbers = np.arange(1, n_max + 1)
    evens = numbers % 2 == 0
    mult3 = numbers % 3 == 0
    print(f"{n_max:>7,}   {evens.mean():.4f}      {mult3.mean():.4f}"
          f"           {(evens | mult3).mean():.4f}          {(numbers == 7).mean():.6f}"
          f"   {(numbers > 1000).mean():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
      N   d(even)  d(mult of 3)  d(even or mult of 3)  d({7})    d(n > 1000)
  1,000   0.5000      0.3330           0.6670          0.001000   0.0000
 10,000   0.5000      0.3333           0.6667          0.000100   0.9000
100,000   0.5000      0.3333           0.6667          0.000010   0.9900
1,000,000   0.5000      0.3333           0.6667          0.000001   0.9990
```

The densities behave like probabilities for finite unions: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$. But the density of every single number tends to 0, so adding up all singletons gives 0, not 1. And the sets $\lbrace n \gt M \rbrace$ shrink to the empty set, yet their density tends to 1 for every $M$. Finite additivity is not enough.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Measure theory says some events have no probability, so probability is broken."** Only sets that need the axiom of choice are excluded. Every set you can describe with intervals, unions, intersections, complements and limits is fine.
- **Using finite additivity where countable additivity is needed.** Finite additivity cannot prove that $P(\text{a head eventually}) = 1$ or that a continuous variable misses every rational. Countable additivity can.
- **Adding up uncountably many points.** Each point of $[0, 1]$ has probability 0, but you may not add up uncountably many zeros to get the probability of an interval. The rule adds only countably many.
- **Thinking "probability 0" means "impossible".** The uniform point $U$ lands on $0.5$ with probability 0, but $0.5$ is a possible value.
- **Thinking "uniform on the whole numbers" is just hard to compute.** It does not exist (Example 3). Use a geometric or Poisson law instead.

## Where It Is Used

- **Foundations:** Kolmogorov's axioms ([3.2](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.2-Kolmogorov-Axioms.md)) use countable additivity and a collection of allowed events because of Vitali's theorem.
- **AI and machine learning:** learning theory often says "assume the hypothesis class is measurable". This is a technical condition that keeps events such as "the best classifier in the class has large error" legal.
- **Computer science:** a randomized algorithm that uses infinitely many random bits (an infinite coin sequence, [20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)) needs a probability on infinite sequences. Measure theory supplies it.
- **Bayesian statistics:** a "uniform prior over all whole numbers" is not a probability (Example 3). Such priors are used with care as "improper priors".
- **Everyday life:** nothing practical changes. This topic explains why the rules you use are safe.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A sample space has 4 outcomes. How many subsets (events) does it have if every subset is allowed?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each of the 4 outcomes is either in a subset or not:

$$2^4 = 16$$

So there are 16 events, including the empty set and the whole space.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $U$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. What is $P(U \in \lbrace \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{4}, \dots \rbrace)$? Which rule did you use?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The set is countable, and each point has probability 0. Countable additivity gives:

$$P(U \in \lbrace \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3}, \dots \rbrace) = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} P\big(U = \tfrac{1}{n}\big) = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} 0 = 0$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In the proof of Vitali's theorem, where is countable additivity used, and where is fairness (moving does not change length) used? Would the proof still work if we only had finite additivity?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Countable additivity is used in the second line of Step 5, to split the length of the circle into the sum over all (countably many) copies $V_{q_n}$. Fairness is used in the third line, to replace $m(V_{q_n})$ by $m(V)$.

With only finite additivity, we could add finitely many copies, and $N \cdot m(V) \le 1$ would only give $m(V) \le \frac{1}{N}$ for every $N$. That is consistent with $m(V) = 0$. No contradiction appears. So the countable sum is the key.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that the length of an interval is unchanged when it is rotated around the circle $[0, 1)$ by adding $q$ and wrapping around.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $[a, b) \subseteq [0, 1)$ and a number $q \in [0, 1)$. Moving by $q$ gives $[a + q, b + q)$. If $b + q \le 1$ this is an interval of the same width $b - a$. If $a + q \ge 1$, subtract 1 from both ends: the width is again $b - a$.

If $a + q \lt 1 \lt b + q$, the interval is cut by the wrap-around into $[a + q, 1)$ and $[0, b + q - 1)$. Their widths add up:

$$(1 - a - q) + (b + q - 1) = b - a$$

So the length is unchanged in every case.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Natural density $d(A) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{\lvert A \cap \lbrace 1, \dots, N \rbrace \rvert}{N}$ is finitely additive on the sets where the limit exists. Show that it is not countably additive, and that continuity from above fails.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each singleton has $d(\lbrace n \rbrace) = \lim \frac{1}{N} = 0$, so the sum over all singletons is $0$. But the union of all singletons is the whole set, with $d = 1$. So countable additivity fails.

For continuity, let $B_M = \lbrace n \gt M \rbrace$. The sets decrease and $\bigcap_M B_M = \varnothing$. Yet:

$$d(B_M) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{N - M}{N} = 1 \quad \text{for every } M$$

so $\lim_M d(B_M) = 1 \ne 0 = d(\varnothing)$. Continuity from above fails. This is exactly what [3.9](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.9-Continuity-of-Probability.md) says: continuity is equivalent to countable additivity.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Use a sieve to compute the density $d_N(\text{primes})$ for $N = 1{,}000{,}000$ and compare it with $\frac{1}{\ln N}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

n_max = 1_000_000
is_prime = np.ones(n_max + 1, dtype=bool)
is_prime[:2] = False
for k in range(2, int(n_max ** 0.5) + 1):       # sieve of Eratosthenes
    if is_prime[k]:
        is_prime[k * k::k] = False

print(f"d_N(primes) = {is_prime[1:].mean():.5f}")
print(f"1 / ln N    = {1 / np.log(n_max):.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
d_N(primes) = 0.07850
1 / ln N    = 0.07238
```

The density goes to 0 slowly, like $\frac{1}{\ln N}$ (the prime number theorem). So the primes have density 0, although there are infinitely many of them.

</details>

## Summary

- On a finite sample space every subset can be an event. On $[0, 1]$ it cannot: Vitali's set shows that "length for every subset" contradicts countable additivity and fairness.
- The repair is to allow only **measurable** sets: this collection is closed under complements and countable unions ([20.2](20.2-Sigma-Algebras-and-Measurable-Spaces.md)).
- Countable additivity is essential. Finite additivity alone allows "uniform on the whole numbers", but then the singletons add to 0 and continuity fails.
- Every countable set has length 0, by covering the $n$-th point with an interval of width $\frac{\varepsilon}{2^n}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P\Big( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \Big) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(A_n) \ \text{ for disjoint } A_n \text{ in the collection of events}}
$$
