# 20.10 Radon-Nikodym and Densities

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.10 Radon-Nikodym and Densities

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 65 minutes · **Before this:** [20.9 Stopping Times and Optional Stopping](20.9-Stopping-Times-and-Optional-Stopping.md)

> **Big idea:** A density is the ratio of two measures, $f = \frac{dP}{d\mu}$, and the Radon-Nikodym theorem says that this ratio exists exactly when $P$ puts no mass where $\mu$ puts none, which explains densities, importance weights, mixed laws, and why some distributions have no density at all.

---

## Why It Matters

Where do densities come from? In Module 08 a density was given as a function whose integral is a probability. The Radon-Nikodym theorem explains when such a function **exists**. It also settles three open points. Mixed distributions ([8.9](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md)) have a density with respect to a different reference measure, and there are even "singular" laws with no density of any usual kind. The importance weight $\frac{p}{q}$ of [19.6](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.6-Importance-Sampling.md) is a Radon-Nikodym derivative. And the conditional expectation $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ of [20.8](20.8-Martingales.md) exists because of this theorem.

## Intuition

Take two dice. The fair die $Q$ gives each face mass $\frac{1}{6}$. A loaded die $P$ gives the face 6 mass $0.5$ and each other face $0.1$. How much more likely is each face under $P$ than under $Q$? Divide the masses:

- face 6: $\frac{0.5}{1/6} = 3$ times as likely;
- faces 1 to 5: $\frac{0.1}{1/6} = 0.6$ times as likely.

This list of ratios $(0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 3)$ is the density of $P$ with respect to $Q$. Multiply the ratio by the $Q$-mass of any set and you get its $P$-mass.

For a continuous variable, the density at $x$ is the mass of a tiny interval around $x$ divided by its length. It is again a ratio: (probability) over (length). That is the definition of a density with respect to Lebesgue measure.

This ratio can only exist if $P$ has no mass where the reference has none. If the loaded die could show a 7, which the fair die never shows, the ratio $\frac{P(7)}{Q(7)}$ would be $\frac{\text{something}}{0}$. In that case, no density exists.

## Picture

Three kinds of distribution on the real line, and the reference measure each one has a density against:

```text
  Law              CDF looks like        Density exists against
  --------------   -------------------   ---------------------------------
  discrete         staircase (jumps)     counting measure (the PMF)
  continuous       smooth curve          Lebesgue measure (the PDF)
  mixed            jumps + smooth parts  Lebesgue measure + point masses
  Cantor           continuous, no jumps  nothing usual: it is SINGULAR
```

The Cantor distribution is the surprising one. Its CDF (the Cantor function) is continuous and climbs from 0 to 1, but it is flat on every removed middle third, so all the mass sits on a set of length zero:

```text
   1.0 |                                                      *
       |                                                     *
       |                                                 ****
  0.75 |                                          *******
       |                                      ****
       |                                     *
   0.5 |                  *******************
       |                 *
       |             ****
  0.25 |      *******
       |  ****
       | *
   0.0 |*
       +-------------------------------------------------------
       0                1/3               2/3                1  x
```

The Cantor distribution is neither discrete nor continuous in the sense of Module 08. It is **singular**.

## Definition

Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be measures on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)). The measure $\mu$ is **$\sigma$-finite** if $\Omega$ is a countable union of sets of finite $\mu$-measure. Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$ and counting measure on a countable set are $\sigma$-finite.

- $\nu$ is **absolutely continuous** with respect to $\mu$, written $\nu \ll \mu$, if $\mu(A) = 0$ implies $\nu(A) = 0$.
- $\nu$ and $\mu$ are **mutually singular**, written $\nu \perp \mu$, if there is a set $N$ with $\mu(N) = 0$ and $\nu(N^c) = 0$: they live on different sets.

**In words:** $\nu \ll \mu$ means that $\nu$ puts no mass on any set that $\mu$ ignores. $\nu \perp \mu$ means that $\nu$ puts all its mass on a set that $\mu$ ignores.

**Theorem (Radon-Nikodym).** Let $\mu$ be $\sigma$-finite and $\nu$ a finite measure with $\nu \ll \mu$. Then there is a measurable function $f \ge 0$ such that

$$
\nu(A) = \int_A f \thinspace d\mu \quad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{F}
$$

and $f$ is unique up to a $\mu$-null set. It is called the **Radon-Nikodym derivative** or **density** of $\nu$ with respect to $\mu$, and is written $f = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$.

**In words:** if $\nu$ ignores everything $\mu$ ignores, then $\nu$ is $\mu$ reweighted by a density $f$: the mass that $\nu$ gives to a set is the integral of $f$ over it.

**Lebesgue decomposition.** Any finite $\nu$ splits uniquely as $\nu = \nu_{ac} + \nu_s$, where $\nu_{ac} \ll \mu$ and $\nu_s \perp \mu$. (We state this without proof.)

The usual densities are special cases:

| Law of $X$ | Reference measure $\mu$ | Density $\frac{dP_X}{d\mu}$ |
|---|---|---|
| discrete on $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$ | counting measure | the PMF $p(k)$ |
| continuous (Module 08) | Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ | the PDF $f(x)$ |
| mixed: atoms $a_i$ plus density $g$ | $\lambda + \sum_i \delta_{a_i}$ | $g(x)$ off the atoms, $p_i$ at $a_i$ |
| Cantor | Lebesgue measure | does not exist (singular) |

Here $\delta_a$ is the **point mass** (Dirac measure): $\delta_a(A) = 1$ if $a \in A$ and 0 otherwise.

**Change of measure.** If $Q \ll P$ are probability measures with density $L = \frac{dQ}{dP}$, then for every measurable $g \ge 0$ (or integrable):

$$
E_Q[g(X)] = E_P\left[ g(X) \thinspace \frac{dQ}{dP}(X) \right]
$$

**In words:** an average under $Q$ is an average under $P$ weighted by the ratio $\frac{dQ}{dP}$. This is importance sampling.

## Why It Is True

**The density is unique.** If $\int_A f \thinspace d\mu = \int_A g \thinspace d\mu$ for all $A$ (with finite values), take $A = \lbrace f \gt g \rbrace$. Then $\int_A (f - g) \thinspace d\mu = 0$ with $f - g \gt 0$ on $A$, so $\mu(A) = 0$. In the same way $\mu(f \lt g) = 0$. So $f = g$ $\mu$-almost everywhere.

**Densities imply absolute continuity.** If $\nu(A) = \int_A f \thinspace d\mu$ and $\mu(A) = 0$, the integral over a null set is 0. So $\nu \ll \mu$.

**Existence on a finite or countable $\Omega$.** Let $\mu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) \gt 0$ for the points where $\mu$ puts mass. Define $f(\omega) = \frac{\nu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace)}{\mu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace)}$ at those points. At a point with $\mu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) = 0$ we have $\nu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) = 0$ by absolute continuity, and we set $f(\omega) = 0$. Then for any $A$:

$$
\nu(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} \nu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) = \sum_{\omega \in A} f(\omega) \mu(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) = \int_A f \thinspace d\mu
$$

This is the loaded die: the density is the ratio of the masses of the points.

**The idea of the general proof (not a full proof).** Cut $\Omega = [0, 1)$ into $2^n$ blocks $B$ of equal length and let $f_n = \frac{\nu(B)}{\mu(B)}$ on each block: the block-wise ratio of masses, as for the dice. When we go from level $n$ to $n + 1$, each block is split in two, and the $\mu$-weighted average of the two new ratios is the old ratio. In the language of [20.8](20.8-Martingales.md), $(f_n)$ is a non-negative martingale under $\mu$. By Doob's theorem it converges almost surely to some $f$. The question is whether the limit still carries all the mass of $\nu$, that is, whether $\nu(A) = \int_A f \thinspace d\mu$. This holds exactly when $\nu \ll \mu$. (The full proof is a short argument with the Hilbert space $L^2$, or with a maximal construction. We skip it.)

**The singular case shows the other side.** For the Cantor law $\nu$ (defined next), the ratios are $f_n = \left( \frac{3}{2} \right)^n$ on the blocks (cut into thirds) that contain Cantor mass, and 0 elsewhere. Under Lebesgue measure, $f_n$ has mean $\left( \frac{2}{3} \right)^n \left( \frac{3}{2} \right)^n = 1$ but tends to 0 almost everywhere: it is exactly the multiplicative martingale of [20.8](20.8-Martingales.md), Practice 4. The limit is $f = 0$, which carries no mass at all. So no density exists.

**The Cantor distribution.** Let $B_1, B_2, \dots$ be independent fair bits ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)) and

$$
X = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{2 B_i}{3^i}
$$

**In words:** $X$ is a number in $[0, 1]$ whose base-3 expansion uses only the digits 0 and 2, each chosen by a fair coin.

Here are its properties.

- **It lives on a set of length 0.** After $n$ digits, $X$ lies in one of $2^n$ intervals of length $3^{-n}$, with total length $\left( \frac{2}{3} \right)^n \to 0$. So $X \in C$ almost surely, where $C = \bigcap_n C_n$ is the Cantor set and $\lambda(C) = 0$.
- **It has no atoms.** $P(X = x) \le P(X \in \text{one of the } n\text{-th level intervals}) = 2^{-n} \to 0$. So $P(X = x) = 0$ for every $x$, and the CDF is continuous.
- **It has no density.** If $f$ existed, then $1 = P(X \in C) = \int_C f \thinspace d\lambda = 0$, because $\lambda(C) = 0$. A contradiction.
- **Mean and variance.** $E[X] = \sum \frac{2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}}{3^i} = \sum 3^{-i} = \frac{1}{2}$, and since the terms are independent, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sum \frac{4 \cdot \frac{1}{4}}{9^i} = \sum 9^{-i} = \frac{1}{8}$.

So "continuous CDF" does not mean "has a density". A CDF with a density is called **absolutely continuous**, and this is stronger.

**Why the change-of-measure formula holds.** Use the four steps of [20.5](20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md). For $g = I_A$: $E_Q[I_A(X)] = Q(A) = \int_A L \thinspace dP = E_P[I_A(X) L]$, by the definition of $L$. By linearity it holds for simple $g$, by the monotone convergence theorem for $g \ge 0$, and by splitting into positive and negative parts for integrable $g$.

**Conditional expectation exists.** Let $Y \ge 0$ be integrable and $\mathcal{G}$ a smaller $\sigma$-algebra. Define $\nu(G) = E[Y I_G]$ for $G \in \mathcal{G}$. It is a finite measure on $(\Omega, \mathcal{G})$ and $\nu \ll P$ (if $P(G) = 0$ then $E[Y I_G] = 0$). The Radon-Nikodym theorem gives a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $f = \frac{d\nu}{dP}$ with $E[f I_G] = E[Y I_G]$. That is exactly the definition of $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ in [20.8](20.8-Martingales.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Loaded Die

**Problem.** The loaded die $P$ gives faces $1, \dots, 5$ probability $0.1$ each, and face 6 probability $0.5$. The fair die $Q$ gives $\frac{1}{6}$ to each face. Find $\frac{dP}{dQ}$, check that $P \ll Q$, and compute $E_P[X]$ (the mean face under $P$) both directly and by reweighting $Q$.

**Solution.**

1. $Q$ gives positive mass to every face, so $P \ll Q$.
2. The density: $\frac{dP}{dQ}(i) = \frac{0.1}{1/6} = 0.6$ for $i = 1, \dots, 5$, and $\frac{dP}{dQ}(6) = \frac{0.5}{1/6} = 3$.
3. Check: the $Q$-average of the density is $\frac{1}{6}(5 \times 0.6 + 3) = \frac{6}{6} = 1$, as it must be for a probability.
4. Directly: $E_P[X] = 0.1(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5) + 0.5 \times 6 = 1.5 + 3 = 4.5$.
5. By reweighting: $E_Q\left[ X \frac{dP}{dQ} \right] = \frac{1}{6}\big( 0.6(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5) + 3 \times 6 \big) = \frac{1}{6}(9 + 18) = 4.5$.

**Answer.** The density is $(0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 3)$ and $E_P[X] = 4.5$ both ways.

**Check.** The reverse density $\frac{dQ}{dP}$ also exists (since $P$ gives positive mass to every face), and it is $\frac{1}{0.6}$ and $\frac{1}{3}$, the reciprocals. If the loaded die never showed a 3, then $Q \ll P$ would fail, and $\frac{dQ}{dP}$ would not exist.

### Example 2: The Traffic Light, a Mixed Law

**Problem.** The wait $X$ at a traffic light is 0 with probability 0.4, and otherwise uniform on $(0, 60]$ seconds ([8.9](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md), Example 1). Find a density of $P_X$ with respect to $\mu = \lambda + \delta_0$ and use it to find $E[X]$.

**Solution.**

1. $P_X \ll \mu$: if $\mu(A) = 0$ then $0 \notin A$ and $\lambda(A) = 0$, so $P_X(A) = 0$.
2. At $x = 0$: $\mu(\lbrace 0 \rbrace) = \lambda(\lbrace 0 \rbrace) + \delta_0(\lbrace 0 \rbrace) = 0 + 1 = 1$, and $P_X(\lbrace 0 \rbrace) = 0.4$. So $f(0) = \frac{0.4}{1} = 0.4$.
3. On $(0, 60]$ the law has the density $\frac{0.6}{60} = 0.01$ with respect to $\lambda$, and $\delta_0$ does not charge this set. So $f(x) = 0.01$ for $0 \lt x \le 60$, and $f(x) = 0$ elsewhere.
4. Check the total: $\int f \thinspace d\mu = 0.4 \times 1 + 0.01 \times 60 = 0.4 + 0.6 = 1$.
5. Mean, by the change-of-variables rule: $E[X] = \int x f(x) \thinspace d\mu = 0 \times 0.4 + \int_0^{60} x \cdot 0.01 \thinspace dx = 0.01 \times \frac{60^2}{2} = 0.01 \times 1800 = 18$.

**Answer.** $f = 0.4$ at 0 and $0.01$ on $(0, 60]$, with respect to $\lambda + \delta_0$; $E[X] = 18$ seconds.

**Check.** The Lebesgue decomposition of $P_X$ is $0.4 \delta_0$ (singular) plus the part with density $0.01$ on $(0, 60]$ (absolutely continuous). Also $E[X] = 0.6 \times 30 = 18$, as in [8.9](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md).

### Example 3: Importance Sampling as a Radon-Nikodym Derivative

**Problem.** Estimate $P(Z \gt 5)$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, where the exact value is $2.867 \times 10^{-7}$, by sampling from $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(5, 1)$ and reweighting by $\frac{dP}{dQ}$.

**Solution.**

1. Let $P = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Q = \mathcal{N}(5, 1)$. Both have densities that are positive everywhere, so $P \ll Q$.
2. The density of $P$ with respect to $Q$ is the ratio of the two Lebesgue densities:

$$
\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) = \frac{e^{-y^2/2}}{e^{-(y-5)^2/2}} = e^{-y^2/2 + (y^2 - 10 y + 25)/2} = e^{-5y + 12.5}
$$

3. Change of measure with $g = I_{(5, \infty)}$:

$$
P(Z \gt 5) = E_Q\left[ I_{\lbrace Y \gt 5 \rbrace} e^{-5Y + 12.5} \right]
$$

4. Under $Q$, half of the samples have $Y \gt 5$, and on those the weight is at most $e^{-12.5} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{-6}$. Averaging weighted indicators gives the estimate.

**Answer.** The importance sampling estimate with 100,000 samples is $2.871 \times 10^{-7}$, within 0.2% of the exact value (see "Simulate It" and Practice 6). Plain Monte Carlo with the same number of samples sees a single sample above 5, and gives $1 \times 10^{-5}$, which is 35 times too large.

**Check.** The weight $\frac{p}{q}$ of [19.6](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.6-Importance-Sampling.md) is the Radon-Nikodym derivative. The condition "the proposal must cover the target" in [19.6](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.6-Importance-Sampling.md) is the condition $P \ll Q$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 samples the Cantor distribution and checks its mean, variance and CDF at $\frac{1}{3}$. Part 2 shows the block ratios $f_n = \frac{\nu(\text{block})}{\text{length}}$ at random points $U$. For the smooth law with density $6x(1 - x)$, $f_n(0.3)$ settles at the density value $1.26$. For the Cantor law, $f_n$ has mean about 1, but it is non-zero only with probability $\left( \frac{2}{3} \right)^n$, so the limit carries no mass.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Cantor distribution: X = sum 2 B_i / 3^i with fair bits B_i (digits 0 and 2 only, never 1).
bits = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(200_000, 40))
cantor = (2 * bits / 3.0 ** np.arange(1, 41)).sum(axis=1)
print(f"Cantor: mean {cantor.mean():.4f} (exact 0.5), variance {cantor.var():.4f} (exact {1/8:.4f})")
print(f"        P(X <= 1/3) = {np.mean(cantor <= 1/3):.4f} (exact 0.5000)")
print(f"        P(1/3 < X < 2/3) = {np.mean((cantor > 1/3 + 1e-9) & (cantor < 2/3 - 1e-9)):.4f} (exact 0)")

# 2. Density as a limit of ratios nu(block) / length(block) on finer and finer blocks, at random points U.
u = rng.random(1_000_000)
ternary = np.floor(u[:, None] * 3.0 ** np.arange(1, 21)) % 3          # first 20 ternary digits of U
no_one = np.cumprod(ternary != 1, axis=1).astype(bool)                # no digit 1 among the first n digits
cdf = lambda x: 3 * x**2 - 2 * x**3                                   # smooth law with density 6x(1-x)
print("  n   Cantor: mean f_n (P f_n>0)    smooth: f_n(0.3) (limit 1.26)")
for n in [1, 5, 10, 20]:
    f_n = np.where(no_one[:, n - 1], 1.5 ** n, 0.0)                   # nu(block) / length(block) for the Cantor law
    a = np.floor(0.3 * 2**n) / 2**n                                   # dyadic block of length 2^-n containing 0.3
    smooth = (cdf(a + 2.0**-n) - cdf(a)) * 2**n
    print(f"{n:>3}   {f_n.mean():>8.3f}       ({np.mean(f_n > 0):.4f})        {smooth:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Cantor: mean 0.4997 (exact 0.5), variance 0.1249 (exact 0.1250)
        P(X <= 1/3) = 0.5005 (exact 0.5000)
        P(1/3 < X < 2/3) = 0.0000 (exact 0)
  n   Cantor: mean f_n (P f_n>0)    smooth: f_n(0.3) (limit 1.26)
  1      1.000       (0.6667)        1.0000
  5      1.000       (0.1317)        1.2520
 10      1.001       (0.0174)        1.2607
 20      1.014       (0.0003)        1.2600
```

The Cantor sample has the predicted mean $\frac{1}{2}$, variance $\frac{1}{8}$, a jump-free CDF with $F(\frac{1}{3}) = \frac{1}{2}$, and no mass in the middle third. For the smooth law the block ratios converge to the density value $f(0.3) = 6 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 1.26$. For the Cantor law the ratios have mean 1 at every level (the total mass is kept), but the fraction of points where the ratio is not 0 equals $\left( \frac{2}{3} \right)^n$ ($0.6667$, $0.1317$, $0.0174$, $0.0003$): the density would have to be $0$ almost everywhere, yet carry mass 1. This is impossible, so no density exists.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a density as a probability.** $f(x)$ is a ratio of mass to length. It can exceed 1 ([8.3](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.3-Densities-Can-Exceed-One.md)). Only integrals of $f$ are probabilities.
- **Believing that a continuous CDF always has a density.** The Cantor CDF is continuous, increasing from 0 to 1, but has no density. Only an **absolutely continuous** CDF has one.
- **Taking the density to be unique.** It is unique only almost everywhere. Changing $f$ at one point, or on a null set, changes no probability.
- **Mixing up the direction.** $\frac{dP}{dQ}$ needs $P \ll Q$. $\frac{dQ}{dP}$ needs $Q \ll P$. For importance sampling with $P$ as the target, the proposal $Q$ must give positive mass wherever the target does (on the set where $g \ne 0$).
- **Forgetting the reference measure.** A PMF is a density with respect to counting measure, a PDF is a density with respect to Lebesgue measure. "The density" always needs "with respect to what".

## Where It Is Used

- **Importance sampling and Monte Carlo:** the weight $\frac{p}{q}$ is $\frac{dP}{dQ}$ ([19.6](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.6-Importance-Sampling.md)). Off-policy reinforcement learning uses the same ratio to evaluate one policy with data from another.
- **Statistics and Bayes:** a likelihood ratio is a Radon-Nikodym derivative, and the Neyman-Pearson test and the Bayesian update (posterior density with respect to the prior is proportional to the likelihood) both use it. The KL divergence is $E_P\left[ \ln \frac{dP}{dQ} \right]$.
- **Machine learning:** image data lies close to a low-dimensional surface in pixel space, so the data distribution is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure and has no density there. This is one reason why generative models add noise or work in a latent space.
- **Conditional expectation and martingales:** $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ exists by Radon-Nikodym ([20.8](20.8-Martingales.md)).
- **Mixed models:** spike-and-slab priors and zero-inflated models have densities with respect to Lebesgue measure plus a point mass.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Divide by $\frac{1}{6}$: the density is $0.2 \times 6 = 1.2$ for $i = 1, 2, 3, 5$ and $0.1 \times 6 = 0.6$ for $i = 4, 6$. The $Q$-average:

$$E_Q\left[ \tfrac{dP}{dQ} \right] = \tfrac{1}{6}(4 \times 1.2 + 2 \times 0.6) = \tfrac{1}{6}(4.8 + 1.2) = 1$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $P = \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and $Q = \text{Unif}(0, 2)$. Which of $P \ll Q$ and $Q \ll P$ holds? Find the density when it exists.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Q(1, 2] = \frac{1}{2}$ but $P(1, 2] = 0$, so $Q \ll P$ fails. But if $Q(A) = 0$ then $\lambda(A \cap [0, 2]) = 0$, so $P(A) = 0$. So $P \ll Q$ holds. The densities with respect to Lebesgue measure are $1$ on $[0, 1]$ for $P$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ on $[0, 2]$ for $Q$. Their ratio:

$$\frac{dP}{dQ}(x) = \frac{1}{1/2} = 2 \text{ on } [0, 1], \qquad 0 \text{ elsewhere}$$

Check: $E_Q\left[ \frac{dP}{dQ} \right] = 2 \cdot Q([0, 1]) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Show that the PMF is the density of a discrete law with respect to counting measure, and that $E[g(X)] = \sum_k g(k) p(k)$ follows from the general formula.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $X$ take values in $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$ and let $\mu$ be counting measure on this set. If $\mu(A) = 0$ then $A$ is empty (within the set), so $P_X \ll \mu$. By the finite/countable case, $f(k) = \frac{P(X = k)}{\mu(\lbrace k \rbrace)} = P(X = k) = p(k)$. The integral with respect to counting measure is a sum, so:

$$E[g(X)] = \int g \thinspace dP_X = \int g(k) p(k) \thinspace d\mu(k) = \sum_{k} g(k) p(k)$$

This is LOTUS for discrete variables.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $P_2 = \text{Exp}(2)$ and $P_1 = \text{Exp}(1)$ (rates 2 and 1). Find $\frac{dP_2}{dP_1}$, check that its $P_1$-mean is 1, and use it to compute $E_{P_2}[X]$ from $P_1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The Lebesgue densities are $2e^{-2x}$ and $e^{-x}$ on $x \ge 0$, so:

$$\frac{dP_2}{dP_1}(x) = \frac{2 e^{-2x}}{e^{-x}} = 2 e^{-x}$$

Its $P_1$-mean: $\int_0^{\infty} 2 e^{-x} \cdot e^{-x} \thinspace dx = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1$. And:

$$E_{P_2}[X] = E_{P_1}\left[ X \cdot 2 e^{-X} \right] = \int_0^{\infty} x \cdot 2 e^{-2x} \thinspace dx = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$

which is the mean $\frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{2}$ of $\text{Exp}(2)$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that the Cantor distribution has a continuous CDF but no density, and that $P(X \le \frac{1}{3}) = \frac{1}{2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

After $n$ digits, $X$ lies in one of $2^n$ intervals of length $3^{-n}$, and each is chosen with probability $2^{-n}$. So $P(X = x) \le 2^{-n}$ for every $n$, hence $P(X = x) = 0$ and the CDF has no jumps: it is continuous.

Let $C_n$ be the union of the $2^n$ intervals. Then $P(X \in C_n) = 1$ and $\lambda(C_n) = \left( \frac{2}{3} \right)^n$. The Cantor set $C = \bigcap_n C_n$ has $\lambda(C) = 0$ and $P(X \in C) = 1$ (continuity from above). If a density $f$ existed: $1 = P(X \in C) = \int_C f \thinspace d\lambda = 0$, since $\lambda(C) = 0$. This contradiction shows that there is no density.

For $F(\frac{1}{3})$: if $B_1 = 0$ then $X \le \sum_{i \ge 2} 2 \cdot 3^{-i} = \frac{1}{3}$. If $B_1 = 1$ then $X \ge \frac{2}{3}$. So $\lbrace X \le \frac{1}{3} \rbrace = \lbrace B_1 = 0 \rbrace$, which has probability $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate $P(Z \gt 5)$, $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, with 100,000 samples, by plain Monte Carlo and by importance sampling from $\mathcal{N}(5, 1)$ with weight $\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) = e^{-5y + 12.5}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100_000
exact = norm.sf(5)                                   # P(Z > 5) for Z ~ N(0, 1)

z = rng.standard_normal(n)                           # plain Monte Carlo from p = N(0, 1)
hits = int(np.sum(z > 5))

y = rng.normal(5.0, 1.0, size=n)                     # proposal q = N(5, 1): sample where the event lives
weight = np.exp(-5 * y + 12.5)                       # dP/dQ(y) = p(y) / q(y)
estimate = np.mean((y > 5) * weight)
print(f"exact        {exact:.3e}")
print(f"plain MC     {hits / n:.3e}   ({hits} hit in {n:,} samples)")
print(f"importance   {estimate:.3e}   (relative error {abs(estimate - exact) / exact:.2%})")
```

Output:

```text
exact        2.867e-07
plain MC     1.000e-05   (1 hit in 100,000 samples)
importance   2.871e-07   (relative error 0.17%)
```

An event of probability $2.9 \times 10^{-7}$ is almost never seen with 100,000 plain samples (the expected number of hits is 0.03), so plain Monte Carlo is useless here. The weighted estimate from the shifted law is accurate.

</details>

## Summary

- $\nu \ll \mu$ means that $\nu$ ignores every set that $\mu$ ignores. $\nu \perp \mu$ means that they live on different sets. Every finite $\nu$ splits as $\nu_{ac} + \nu_s$.
- **Radon-Nikodym:** if $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite and $\nu \ll \mu$, there is a density $f = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \ge 0$, unique almost everywhere, with $\nu(A) = \int_A f \thinspace d\mu$.
- A PMF is a density with respect to counting measure, a PDF is a density with respect to Lebesgue measure, and a mixed law has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure plus point masses.
- The Cantor distribution has a continuous CDF but is singular: no atoms and no density.
- **Change of measure:** $E_Q[g] = E_P\left[ g \frac{dQ}{dP} \right]$ is importance sampling, and the existence of $E[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$ comes from the same theorem.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\nu(A) = \int_A \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \thinspace d\mu, \qquad E_Q[g] = E_P\left[ g \cdot \frac{dQ}{dP} \right]}
$$
