# 20.5 Lebesgue Integral and Expectation

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 20: Advanced Probability](README.md) / 20.5 Lebesgue Integral and Expectation

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 65 minutes · **Before this:** [20.4 Random Variables as Measurable Functions](20.4-Random-Variables-as-Measurable-Functions.md)

> **Big idea:** Expectation is the Lebesgue integral $E[X] = \int X \thinspace dP$, built from indicators by sums and limits, and three convergence theorems (monotone, Fatou, dominated) tell you exactly when a limit may be moved inside an expectation.

---

## Why It Matters

Until now $E[X]$ was a sum for discrete variables and an ordinary integral of $x f(x)$ for continuous ones. What about a variable that is part discrete and part continuous ([8.9](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md)), or neither? And when can you write $\lim E[X_n] = E[\lim X_n]$? This move is used constantly: in the Strong Law, in differentiating $E[f_\theta(X)]$ with respect to $\theta$ (policy gradients, the reparametrization trick), and in Monte Carlo ([19.3](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md)). The Lebesgue integral gives one definition that covers every case, and its convergence theorems say when the limit move is legal. It also proves the general LOTUS that [8.6](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md) postponed.

## Intuition

You have a row of coins and want the total value. You can add them in the order they lie, or you can **sort them by value first**. The second way is Lebesgue's idea: group the outcomes by the value of $X$, then add (value) $\times$ (probability of the group).

```text
  Coins in a row (taka):   5  1  5  2  1  5  2  5

  In the order they lie:   5+1+5+2+1+5+2+5 = 26

  Sorted by value:   value 1:  2 coins  ->  1 x 2 =  2
                     value 2:  2 coins  ->  2 x 2 =  4
                     value 5:  4 coins  ->  5 x 4 = 20
                                                    ----
                                                      26
```

For a die, $E[X] = \sum_x x \thinspace P(X = x)$ is already the second way. Lebesgue's trick is to keep this recipe when $X$ takes uncountably many values: approximate $X$ from below by variables that take **finitely many** values, compute their expectations as sums, and take the limit.

## Picture

Approximating $X = U^2$ from below by a staircase $s_2$ with steps of height $\frac{1}{4}$. The curve is drawn with `*` and the staircase with `=` (a `#` means that they touch). The staircase never goes above the curve:

```text
  1.00 |                                                        #
       |                                                      **
       |                                                    **
       |                                                  **
  0.75 |                                                *#======
       |                                              **
       |                                            **
       |                                         ***
  0.50 |                                       *#========
       |                                    ***
       |                                 ***
       |                              ***
  0.25 |                           *##==========
       |                       ****
       |                  *****
       |          ********
  0.00 |##########==================
       +---------------------------------------------------------  outcome U
```

The expectation of the staircase is a finite sum (step height times the probability of the step), and the staircases rise to the curve as the steps get thinner. So the expectation of $X$ is the limit of those sums.

## Definition

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space. We define $E[X] = \int_{\Omega} X \thinspace dP$ in four steps.

**Step 1: indicators.** For an event $A$: $E[I_A] = P(A)$.

**Step 2: simple variables.** A **simple** variable takes finitely many values: $s = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i I_{A_i}$ with $a_i \ge 0$ and disjoint events $A_i$. Then

$$
E[s] = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i \thinspace P(A_i)
$$

**Step 3: non-negative variables.** For $X \ge 0$ (the value $+\infty$ is allowed):

$$
E[X] = \sup \lbrace E[s] : s \text{ simple}, \ 0 \le s \le X \rbrace \in [0, \infty]
$$

**Step 4: general variables.** Write $X = X^+ - X^-$, where $X^+ = \max(X, 0)$ and $X^- = \max(-X, 0)$ (both are non-negative). If at least one of $E[X^+]$ and $E[X^-]$ is finite, define $E[X] = E[X^+] - E[X^-]$. The variable $X$ is **integrable** if $E\lvert X \rvert = E[X^+] + E[X^-] \lt \infty$. The collection of integrable variables is called $L^1$.

**In words:** the expectation of a non-negative variable is the largest value you can reach with finite-valued variables below it. A general variable is split into its positive and negative parts. The expectation does not exist when it would be $\infty - \infty$.

For $X \ge 0$ the staircases $s_n = \min\big(n, \thinspace 2^{-n} \lfloor 2^n X \rfloor\big)$ increase to $X$, and $E[s_n]$ increases to $E[X]$ (this follows from Theorem 1 below).

**Properties.** For integrable $X, Y$ and numbers $a, b$:

- **Linearity:** $E[aX + bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y]$.
- **Monotonicity:** $X \le Y$ almost surely implies $E[X] \le E[Y]$. Also $\lvert E[X] \rvert \le E\lvert X \rvert$.
- **Null sets do not matter:** if $X = Y$ almost surely, then $E[X] = E[Y]$.
- **Zero:** if $X \ge 0$ and $E[X] = 0$, then $X = 0$ almost surely.
- **Markov's inequality:** for $X \ge 0$ and $a \gt 0$, $a \thinspace I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace} \le X$, so $a P(X \ge a) \le E[X]$ ([16.1](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)).

**Three convergence theorems.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ and $X$ be random variables.

1. **Monotone convergence theorem (MCT).** If $0 \le X_1 \le X_2 \le \cdots$ and $X_n \to X$ almost surely, then $E[X_n]$ increases to $E[X]$ (the value $\infty$ is allowed).
2. **Fatou's lemma.** If $X_n \ge 0$, then $E[\liminf_n X_n] \le \liminf_n E[X_n]$.
3. **Dominated convergence theorem (DCT).** If $X_n \to X$ almost surely and there is an integrable $Y$ with $\lvert X_n \rvert \le Y$ for all $n$, then $E[X_n] \to E[X]$ (and even $E\lvert X_n - X \rvert \to 0$).

**In words:** you may swap a limit and an expectation if the variables only grow (MCT), or if one fixed integrable variable bounds all of them (DCT). Without a condition, the limit of the expectations can be larger than the expectation of the limit (Fatou, and Example 2).

**Change of variables (general LOTUS).** If $g$ is a Borel function and $E\lvert g(X) \rvert \lt \infty$ (or $g \ge 0$), then

$$
E[g(X)] = \int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x) \thinspace dP_X(x)
$$

where $P_X$ is the law of $X$ ([20.4](20.4-Random-Variables-as-Measurable-Functions.md)). If $P_X$ has a density $f$ (see [20.10](20.10-Radon-Nikodym-and-Densities.md)), this is $\int g(x) f(x) \thinspace dx$. If $X$ is discrete, it is $\sum_x g(x) p(x)$.

**In words:** to average $g(X)$ you do not need to know $\Omega$. The law of $X$ is enough.

## Why It Is True

**Proof of the MCT.** Take $0 \le X_1 \le X_2 \le \cdots$ with $X_n \to X$. Since $X_n \le X$, we have $E[X_n] \le E[X]$ for all $n$, and the sequence $E[X_n]$ increases, so $L = \lim E[X_n] \le E[X]$. For the other direction, take any simple $s$ with $0 \le s \le X$ and any $c \in (0, 1)$. Put $A_n = \lbrace X_n \ge c s \rbrace$. These events increase with $n$ and their union is $\Omega$: where $s(\omega) = 0$ the point is in every $A_n$; where $s(\omega) \gt 0$ we have $X(\omega) \ge s(\omega) \gt c s(\omega)$, and $X_n(\omega)$ increases to $X(\omega)$, so $\omega \in A_n$ eventually. Now $X_n \ge c s \thinspace I_{A_n}$, so with $s = \sum a_i I_{B_i}$:

$$
E[X_n] \ge c \thinspace E[s \thinspace I_{A_n}] = c \sum_{i} a_i P(B_i \cap A_n) \to c \sum_i a_i P(B_i) = c \thinspace E[s]
$$

The limit is by continuity from below ([20.3](20.3-Probability-Measures.md)). So $L \ge c \thinspace E[s]$. Let $c$ increase to 1 and take the supremum over $s$: $L \ge E[X]$.

**Proof of Fatou's lemma.** Let $Y_n = \inf_{k \ge n} X_k$. Then $0 \le Y_1 \le Y_2 \le \cdots$ with $Y_n \to \liminf X_k$, and $Y_n \le X_n$, so $E[Y_n] \le E[X_n]$. By the MCT, $E[\liminf X_k] = \lim E[Y_n] \le \liminf E[X_n]$.

**Proof of the DCT.** The variables $Y + X_n$ and $Y - X_n$ are $\ge 0$. Apply Fatou to $Y + X_n$, whose liminf is $Y + X$:

$$
E[Y] + E[X] \le \liminf \big( E[Y] + E[X_n] \big) \implies E[X] \le \liminf E[X_n]
$$

Apply Fatou to $Y - X_n$, whose liminf is $Y - X$:

$$
E[Y] - E[X] \le E[Y] - \limsup E[X_n] \implies \limsup E[X_n] \le E[X]
$$

So $\limsup E[X_n] \le E[X] \le \liminf E[X_n]$, and the limit exists and equals $E[X]$.

**Proof of the change of variables.** Check it in the four steps of the definition. For $g = I_B$: $E[I_B(X)] = P(X \in B) = P_X(B) = \int I_B \thinspace dP_X$. For simple $g$ it follows by linearity. For $g \ge 0$ take increasing simple functions $g_n$ with $g_n \to g$ and use the MCT on both sides. For general $g$ split $g = g^+ - g^-$.

**A tail-sum formula (from the MCT).** If $X$ takes values $0, 1, 2, \dots$, then $X = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} I_{\lbrace X \ge n \rbrace}$. The partial sums are non-negative and increase, so by the MCT we may take the expectation inside the sum:

$$
E[X] = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(X \ge n)
$$

**In words:** the mean of a whole-number variable is the sum of its tail probabilities. For any $X \ge 0$, apply this to $\lfloor X \rfloor \le X \le \lfloor X \rfloor + 1$: $\sum_{n \ge 1} P(X \ge n) \le E[X] \le 1 + \sum_{n \ge 1} P(X \ge n)$. We use this in [20.6](20.6-Borel-Cantelli-Lemmas.md).

**Riemann versus Lebesgue.** If a function on $[0, 1]$ has a Riemann integral, then it also has a Lebesgue integral, with the same value. But Lebesgue's integral covers more functions. The Dirichlet function $I_{\mathbb{Q}}$ (1 on rationals, 0 elsewhere) has no Riemann integral, since every strip contains both values. Its Lebesgue integral is $E[I_{\mathbb{Q}}(U)] = P(U \in \mathbb{Q}) = 0$ for $U$ uniform ([20.1](20.1-Why-Measure-Theory.md), Example 2).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Building the Mean of U Squared from Staircases

**Problem.** Let $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and $X = U^2$. Compute $E[s_n]$ for $s_n = 2^{-n} \lfloor 2^n X \rfloor$ with $n = 2$, and show that $E[s_n] \to \frac{1}{3} = \int_0^1 u^2 \thinspace du$.

**Solution.**

1. For $n = 2$ the staircase has steps $\frac{1}{4}$. Since $X \le 1$, $s_2$ takes the values $0, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{4}, 1$, and equals 1 only when $X = 1$ (probability 0).
2. $P(X \ge a) = P(U \ge \sqrt{a}) = 1 - \sqrt{a}$. The thresholds are $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$, $\sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.7071$, $\sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.8660$.
3. The probabilities of the steps:

| Value of $s_2$ | Event | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $X \lt \frac{1}{4}$ | $0.5000$ |
| $\frac{1}{4}$ | $\frac{1}{4} \le X \lt \frac{1}{2}$ | $0.7071 - 0.5000 = 0.2071$ |
| $\frac{1}{2}$ | $\frac{1}{2} \le X \lt \frac{3}{4}$ | $0.8660 - 0.7071 = 0.1589$ |
| $\frac{3}{4}$ | $\frac{3}{4} \le X \lt 1$ | $1 - 0.8660 = 0.1340$ |

4. Add (value) $\times$ (probability):

$$
E[s_2] = 0.25(0.2071) + 0.5(0.1589) + 0.75(0.1340) = 0.0518 + 0.0795 + 0.1005 \approx 0.2317
$$

5. The same recipe for general $n$ gives $E[s_n] = \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{j=1}^{2^n - 1} \big( 1 - \sqrt{j / 2^n} \big)$. This is a Riemann sum for $\int_0^1 (1 - \sqrt{t}) \thinspace dt = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$. Computing it gives $0.1464$ for $n = 1$, $0.2317$ for $n = 2$, $0.3052$ for $n = 4$, $0.3314$ for $n = 8$.

**Answer.** $E[s_2] \approx 0.2317$, and $E[s_n]$ increases to $\frac{1}{3}$.

**Check.** The simulation below gives the same staircase expectations.

### Example 2: The Moving Bump

**Problem.** Let $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and $X_n = n \thinspace I_{\lbrace U \lt 1/n \rbrace}$. Find $\lim X_n$ and $\lim E[X_n]$. Which theorem fails?

**Solution.**

1. For a fixed outcome $u \gt 0$, we have $u \ge \frac{1}{n}$ once $n \ge \frac{1}{u}$, so $X_n(u) = 0$ from then on. So $X_n \to 0$ for every $u \gt 0$, that is, almost surely.
2. $E[X_n] = n \cdot P(U \lt \frac{1}{n}) = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$ for every $n$.
3. So $\lim E[X_n] = 1$, but $E[\lim X_n] = E[0] = 0$.
4. The MCT does not apply, since $X_n$ is not increasing. The DCT does not apply, since no integrable $Y$ bounds all $X_n$: the smallest bound is $\sup_n X_n = n$ on $\lbrace \frac{1}{n+1} \le U \lt \frac{1}{n} \rbrace$, and $E[\sup_n X_n] = \sum_n n \big( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} \big) = \sum_n \frac{1}{n+1} = \infty$.
5. Fatou holds, with a strict inequality: $0 = E[\liminf X_n] \lt 1 = \liminf E[X_n]$.

**Answer.** $X_n \to 0$ a.s., yet $E[X_n] = 1$. The limit cannot be moved inside because there is no integrable bound.

**Check.** All the mass moves into a narrower and narrower spike that grows taller. In machine learning terms: a gradient estimator can have a bounded mean while being zero on almost every sample. This is how rare huge values break the swap of limit and expectation.

### Example 3: The St. Petersburg Game and the MCT

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin until the first head, at flip $K$. You are paid $X = 2^K$ taka. Show that $E[X] = \infty$ by looking at the truncated payoffs $X \wedge 2^m = \min(X, 2^m)$.

**Solution.**

1. $P(K = k) = \frac{1}{2^k}$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$.
2. For $k \le m$, the payoff $2^k$ is below the cap, and it contributes $2^k \cdot \frac{1}{2^k} = 1$. That is $m$ in total.
3. For $k \gt m$ the payoff is capped at $2^m$. The probability of $K \gt m$ is $\frac{1}{2^m}$, so this part contributes $2^m \cdot \frac{1}{2^m} = 1$.
4. So $E[X \wedge 2^m] = m + 1$.
5. The truncated payoffs increase to $X$ as $m \to \infty$. By the MCT, $E[X] = \lim (m + 1) = \infty$.

**Answer.** $E[X] = \infty$. Truncating at $2^m$ gives an average payoff of only $m + 1$ taka, so a cap of about $2^{20}$ (one million) makes the game worth just 21 taka.

**Check.** The simulation below gives $11$ for $m = 10$.

## Simulate It

First we check the staircases of Example 1: their means rise towards $\frac{1}{3}$. Then we check Example 2: the mean of $X_n$ stays near 1 although the chance that $X_n$ is non-zero goes to 0. Finally we check the St. Petersburg truncation of Example 3 with $m = 10$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
u = rng.random(2_000_000)

# Lebesgue's staircases from below: s_n = floor(2^n X) / 2^n, with X = U^2.
x = u ** 2
print("n   E[s_n]   (E[X] = 1/3)")
for n in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
    s_n = np.floor(2.0 ** n * x) / 2.0 ** n          # takes finitely many values, and s_n <= X
    print(f"{n}   {s_n.mean():.4f}")

# Moving bump: X_n = n on (0, 1/n), 0 elsewhere. X_n -> 0 for every outcome, yet E[X_n] = 1.
print("n      E[X_n]   P(X_n != 0)")
for n in [10, 100, 1000]:
    x_n = n * (u < 1 / n)
    print(f"{n:<6} {x_n.mean():.3f}    {np.mean(x_n != 0):.4f}")

# St. Petersburg payoff 2^K truncated at 2^10: the exact mean is 10 + 1 = 11.
k = rng.geometric(0.5, size=1_000_000)               # K = flips until the first head
print(f"E[min(X, 2^10)] simulated {np.minimum(2.0 ** k, 2.0 ** 10).mean():.3f}, exact 11")
```

Output:

```text
n   E[s_n]   (E[X] = 1/3)
1   0.1463
2   0.2316
4   0.3051
8   0.3313
n      E[X_n]   P(X_n != 0)
10     0.997    0.0997
100    0.995    0.0099
1000   0.997    0.0010
E[min(X, 2^10)] simulated 10.977, exact 11
```

The staircase means $0.1463, 0.2316, 0.3051, 0.3313$ match the exact $0.1464, 0.2317, 0.3052, 0.3314$ and climb to $\frac{1}{3}$. The bump keeps a mean near 1 (the small gap to 1 is simulation noise) while the fraction of non-zero values falls like $\frac{1}{n}$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Swapping a limit and an expectation without a reason.** $\lim E[X_n] = E[\lim X_n]$ needs monotone growth (MCT) or a dominating integrable variable (DCT). Example 2 breaks it.
- **Writing $E[X]$ when $E[X^+] = E[X^-] = \infty$.** The mean of a Cauchy variable does not exist ([9.9](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md)). Do not call it zero because the density is symmetric.
- **Reversing Fatou's inequality.** It is $E[\liminf X_n] \le \liminf E[X_n]$, not the other way round, and it needs $X_n \ge 0$.
- **Thinking that Lebesgue and Riemann integrals disagree.** Where the Riemann integral exists, they are equal. Lebesgue's integral just exists more often.
- **Thinking that $E[X] = 0$ means $X = 0$.** This holds for $X \ge 0$ only (almost surely). A fair coin payoff of plus or minus 1 has mean 0.

## Where It Is Used

- **Machine learning:** moving a derivative inside an expectation, $\nabla_\theta E[f_\theta(X)] = E[\nabla_\theta f_\theta(X)]$ (the score-function and reparametrization tricks), is justified by the DCT when the derivatives have an integrable bound.
- **Limit theorems:** the Strong Law and the proofs that $L^2$ convergence implies convergence in probability use these theorems ([17.2](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.2-Relationships-Between-Modes.md)).
- **Statistics and simulation:** every Monte Carlo average ([19.3](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md)) estimates a Lebesgue integral $\int g \thinspace dP_X$, whatever the type of $X$.
- **Finance and insurance:** truncated or capped payoffs and their limits, as in the St. Petersburg game.
- **Mixed and singular laws:** $E[g(X)]$ is the same formula $\int g \thinspace dP_X$ for discrete, continuous, mixed ([8.9](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md)) and singular distributions ([20.10](20.10-Radon-Nikodym-and-Densities.md)).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X$ is a simple variable with values 3 on $A$, 5 on $B$ and 0 elsewhere:

$$E[X] = 3(0.2) + 5(0.5) + 0(0.3) = 0.6 + 2.5 = 3.1$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find $E[I_{\mathbb{Q}}(U)]$ and explain why the Riemann integral of $I_{\mathbb{Q}}$ on $[0, 1]$ does not exist.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[I_{\mathbb{Q}}(U)] = P(U \in \mathbb{Q}) = 0$, since the rationals are a countable set ([20.1](20.1-Why-Measure-Theory.md)).

For the Riemann integral, every interval contains both rational and irrational numbers. So every lower sum is 0 and every upper sum is 1. They never meet as the strips get thinner, so the Riemann integral does not exist.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Prove Markov's inequality in the form $P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}$ for $X \ge 0$ and $a \gt 0$, using monotonicity.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For every outcome, $a \thinspace I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace} \le X$: if $X \ge a$ the left side is $a \le X$, and otherwise it is $0 \le X$. By monotonicity and $E[I_A] = P(A)$:

$$a \thinspace P(X \ge a) = E\big[ a \thinspace I_{\lbrace X \ge a \rbrace} \big] \le E[X]$$

Divide by $a$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Use the DCT to find $\lim E[X^n]$, and compare with the exact value. Then show that the DCT cannot be used for $Y_n = (n + 1) X^n$, and compute $\lim E[Y_n]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We have $0 \le X^n \le 1$ and $X^n \to 0$ for every $X \lt 1$, so almost surely. The constant 1 is integrable, so the DCT gives:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} E[X^n] = E[0] = 0$$

Exactly, $E[X^n] = \int_0^1 x^n \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{n + 1} \to 0$.

For $Y_n = (n + 1) X^n$: $Y_n \to 0$ almost surely again, but $E[Y_n] = (n + 1) \cdot \frac{1}{n + 1} = 1$ for every $n$. So $\lim E[Y_n] = 1 \ne 0$. No integrable variable dominates all the $Y_n$, as in the moving bump.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be random variables with $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} E\lvert X_n \rvert \lt \infty$. Prove that $\sum_n X_n$ converges absolutely almost surely, and that $E\big[\sum_n X_n\big] = \sum_n E[X_n]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $S = \sum_n \lvert X_n \rvert$. The partial sums are non-negative and increasing, so by the MCT:

$$E[S] = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} E\lvert X_n \rvert \lt \infty$$

A non-negative variable with a finite mean is finite almost surely (otherwise $P(S = \infty) \gt 0$ would give $E[S] = \infty$). So $\sum_n \lvert X_n \rvert \lt \infty$ almost surely, and the series converges absolutely there.

For the second claim, the partial sums $T_N = \sum_{n \le N} X_n$ satisfy $\lvert T_N \rvert \le S$, and $S$ is integrable. By the DCT, $E[\lim T_N] = \lim E[T_N] = \sum_n E[X_n]$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the St. Petersburg payoff $X = 2^K$ truncated at $2^m$ for $m = 5, 10, 15$ and compare the sample mean with $m + 1$.

```text
m = 10: simulated mean  11.022, exact 11

```

The means grow only like $m$ (a logarithm of the cap), and the larger caps are noisier, because the huge payoffs are rare.

</details>

## Summary

- The **Lebesgue integral** (expectation) is built in four steps: indicators, simple variables, non-negative variables as a supremum, general variables as $X^+ - X^-$. It covers discrete, continuous, mixed and singular laws with one formula.
- **MCT:** monotone limits pass through $E$. **Fatou:** $E[\liminf X_n] \le \liminf E[X_n]$ for $X_n \ge 0$. **DCT:** an integrable bound allows the swap.
- Without a bound, $E[\lim X_n] \ne \lim E[X_n]$ is possible (the moving bump).
- **General LOTUS:** $E[g(X)] = \int g \thinspace dP_X$.
- For whole-number $X$, $E[X] = \sum_{n \ge 1} P(X \ge n)$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X] = \int_{\Omega} X \thinspace dP, \qquad E[g(X)] = \int g(x) \thinspace dP_X(x)}
$$
