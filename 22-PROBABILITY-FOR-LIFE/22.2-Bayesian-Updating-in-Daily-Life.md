# 22.2 Bayesian Updating in Daily Life

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [22.1 Base Rates](22.1-Base-Rates.md). You need [4.5 Odds Form of Bayes](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md) and [4.6 Sequential Bayesian Updating](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md).

> **Big idea:** A good thinker keeps a number for "how sure am I?", and moves it up or down by a fair amount each time a new clue arrives: not too little, not too much.

---

## Why It Matters

Life gives you clues one at a time: a mock test score, a late reply, a review, a weather sign. Some people ignore each clue and never change their mind. Others jump to a new opinion after every clue. **Bayesian updating** is the middle path, and Module 04 proves why it is the right one. This file shows how to do it with simple numbers, in your head or on paper. It builds on the base rate of [22.1](22.1-Base-Rates.md) and prepares the habit of keeping score in [22.15 Calibration](22.15-Calibration.md).

## Intuition

You are preparing for an exam. Before any news, you think you have a 60% chance to pass, based on how last year's students did. That is your **prior** belief.

Then you score 70 or more in a mock test. How much should that change your mind? Ask one question: **"How much more likely is this clue if I will pass than if I will fail?"** Suppose students who pass score 70 or more 80% of the time, and students who fail do so 30% of the time. The clue is about 2.7 times more likely for a future passer. So your belief should go up, but not to 100%.

Next you find out that you studied less than 5 hours last week. Passers do this 30% of the time, failers 60% of the time. This clue is twice as likely for a failer. Your belief goes down a little. Each clue pushes your belief up or down by its own strength, and the new belief becomes the starting point for the next clue.

## Picture

```text
  Step                          odds    probability
  start (prior)                 1.5     60.0%   |############........|
  mock score 70+   (x 2.67)     4.0     80.0%   |################....|
  little study     (x 0.5)      2.0     66.7%   |#############.......|
                                                 0%              100%
```

Each clue multiplies the odds by its strength (the likelihood ratio). A number above 1 pushes belief up, and a number below 1 pushes it down.

## Definition

The **odds** of an event $H$ are the chance that it happens divided by the chance that it does not:

$$
\text{odds}(H) = \frac{P(H)}{1 - P(H)}
$$

**In words:** 60% means odds of $\frac{0.6}{0.4} = 1.5$, which is "3 to 2". To go back, $P(H) = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}}$.

The **likelihood ratio** (LR) of a clue $E$ tells how much more likely the clue is when $H$ is true than when it is false:

$$
\text{LR} = \frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}
$$

**In words:** an LR of 3 means "this clue is 3 times as likely if $H$ is true". An LR of 1 means the clue tells you nothing. An LR of $\frac{1}{3}$ means the clue points the other way, with the same strength.

**Bayes' rule in odds form** ([4.5](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)) is:

$$
\underbrace{\text{odds}(H \mid E)}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\text{odds}(H)}_{\text{prior odds}} \times \text{LR}
$$

**In words:** new odds equal old odds times the strength of the clue. If several clues are independent given $H$, multiply all their LRs.

## Why It Is True

Write Bayes' theorem for $H$ and for $H^c$, and divide the two:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(H^c \mid E)} &= \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H) / P(E)}{P(E \mid H^c) \thinspace P(H^c) / P(E)} && \text{(Bayes for } H \text{ and } H^c \text{)} \\
&= \frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)} \cdot \frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)} && \text{(} P(E) \text{ cancels)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The hard part of Bayes, the number $P(E)$, disappears. That is why the odds form is easy to do in your head.

For two clues $E_1$ and $E_2$ that are **conditionally independent given $H$** ([4.9](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.9-Conditional-Independence.md)), we have $P(E_1, E_2 \mid H) = P(E_1 \mid H) \thinspace P(E_2 \mid H)$, and the same for $H^c$. So the LR of the pair is the product of the two LRs:

$$
\text{odds}(H \mid E_1, E_2) = \text{odds}(H) \times \text{LR}_1 \times \text{LR}_2
$$

Two facts follow at once. The **order of the clues does not matter**, because multiplication does not care about order. And **a prior of 0 or 1 never moves**: odds of 0 stay 0 after any multiplication, and odds of infinity stay infinity. That is why careful people avoid saying "0%" or "100%" about anything that is not logic or math.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Exam

**Problem.** Prior chance of passing: 60%. Clue 1 (mock score of 70 or more): $P(E_1 \mid \text{pass}) = 0.80$ and $P(E_1 \mid \text{fail}) = 0.30$. Clue 2 (under 5 hours of study last week): $P(E_2 \mid \text{pass}) = 0.30$ and $P(E_2 \mid \text{fail}) = 0.60$. Find the chance of passing after each clue. (Made-up numbers.)

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds: $\frac{0.60}{0.40} = 1.5$.
2. Clue 1: $\text{LR}_1 = \frac{0.80}{0.30} \approx 2.667$. New odds: $1.5 \times 2.667 = 4$. As a probability: $\frac{4}{1 + 4} = 0.8$.
3. Clue 2: $\text{LR}_2 = \frac{0.30}{0.60} = 0.5$. New odds: $4 \times 0.5 = 2$. As a probability: $\frac{2}{1 + 2} \approx 0.667$.

**Answer.** 60% at the start, 80% after the good mock score, and about 67% after the news about low study time.

**Check.** The order does not matter: $1.5 \times 0.5 \times 2.667 = 2$ as well. The simulation in Practice problem 6 gives $0.8001$ and $0.6658$.

### Example 2: Is This Email a Phishing Attempt?

**Problem.** Made-up numbers: 5% of the emails that reach your inbox with a link are phishing. Clue A: the email creates a strong feeling of urgency. This happens in 4 times more phishing emails than in normal ones ($\text{LR}_A = 4$). Clue B: the sender's domain has a spelling error, which is 20 times more common in phishing ($\text{LR}_B = 20$). Clue C: the email greets you by your real name, which is twice as common in normal emails ($\text{LR}_C = 0.5$). Find the chance of phishing after each clue.

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds: $\frac{0.05}{0.95} \approx 0.0526$.
2. After A: $0.0526 \times 4 = 0.2105$, which is $\frac{0.2105}{1.2105} \approx 0.174$.
3. After B: $0.2105 \times 20 = 4.2105$, which is $\frac{4.2105}{5.2105} \approx 0.808$.
4. After C: $4.2105 \times 0.5 = 2.1053$, which is $\frac{2.1053}{3.1053} \approx 0.678$.

**Answer.** 5%, then 17%, then 81%, then 68%. The domain error is the clue that matters most. The friendly greeting lowers the chance but does not remove the danger.

**Check.** A clue with an LR near 1 hardly moves the odds. A clue with an LR of 20 moves them a lot. The multiplication also assumes the clues are independent given "phishing". In real email the clues are often linked (a phishing kit often adds urgency and a fake domain together), and then multiplying counts the same information twice. This is the weakness of naive Bayes ([21.1.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md)).

### Example 3: Why "0%" Is a Trap

**Problem.** Rafi is sure a new scheme is a fraud and says the chance it is honest is 0%. A friend gives him strong proof that is 100 times more likely if the scheme is honest. Another person has a prior of 0.1% and gets the same proof. Compare them.

**Solution.**

1. Rafi's prior odds are $0$. After the proof: $0 \times 100 = 0$. His belief stays at 0%, whatever the proof.
2. The other person: prior odds $\frac{0.001}{0.999} \approx 0.001001$. After the proof: $0.001001 \times 100 = 0.1001$. As a probability: $\frac{0.1001}{1.1001} \approx 0.091$.

**Answer.** Rafi cannot learn anything. The other person moves from 0.1% to about 9%: a big change in odds that still leaves a lot of doubt.

**Check.** A small but non-zero prior is the honest way to say "I think this is very unlikely, but I can be shown wrong". Your belief can then move when the proof is strong.

## Simulate It

A teammate is either reliable (on time 90% of the weeks) or not (60% of the weeks). You start with 50-50. The code watches 10 weeks, updates the odds each week, and checks that the beliefs are fair.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people, n_weeks = 200_000, 10
p_good, p_bad = 0.90, 0.60            # on-time rate of a reliable / unreliable teammate

is_reliable = rng.random(n_people) < 0.5                       # prior: 50-50
on_time = rng.binomial(n_weeks, np.where(is_reliable, p_good, p_bad))

def posterior_after(k):
    # Odds form: prior odds 1, times 1.5 per on-time week and 0.25 per late week.
    odds = (p_good / p_bad) ** k * ((1 - p_good) / (1 - p_bad)) ** (n_weeks - k)
    return odds / (1 + odds)

posterior = posterior_after(on_time)
k = np.arange(n_weeks + 1)
for name, truth, p in [("reliable", True, p_good), ("unreliable", False, p_bad)]:
    exact = np.sum(binom.pmf(k, n_weeks, p) * posterior_after(k))
    print(f"Mean posterior, truly {name:>10}: simulation {posterior[is_reliable == truth].mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

# Bayes is calibrated: the posterior matches the real share of reliable people.
for weeks in [7, 8, 9]:
    group = on_time == weeks
    print(f"{weeks} weeks on time: posterior {posterior_after(weeks):.3f}, truly reliable {is_reliable[group].mean():.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
Mean posterior, truly   reliable: simulation 0.8257, exact 0.8255
Mean posterior, truly unreliable: simulation 0.1738, exact 0.1745
7 weeks on time: posterior 0.211, truly reliable 0.207
8 weeks on time: posterior 0.616, truly reliable 0.620
9 weeks on time: posterior 0.906, truly reliable 0.907
```

Two lessons. First, ten weeks of data do not give certainty: a reliable teammate ends with a belief of about 83% on average, not 100%. Second, the updated beliefs are honest: among all people with 8 on-time weeks, about 62% are reliable, just as the number said.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not updating enough.** People often keep their first opinion even after strong clues. If the clue has an LR of 10, your odds should go up by 10 times. Write the number down and do the multiplication.
- **Updating too much.** One loud clue (a single bad review, one late reply) can feel huge. Ask for its LR. A late reply may be only 1.5 times as likely if your friend is upset, because most late replies come from busy days.
- **Counting the same clue twice.** Three news articles that all repeat one source are one clue, not three. Multiply LRs only for clues that are independent given the hypothesis.
- **Swapping the two probabilities.** The LR uses $P(E \mid H)$, "how likely is the clue if I am right", not $P(H \mid E)$. This is the same swap as in [22.1](22.1-Base-Rates.md).
- **Starting at 0% or 100%.** With a prior of exactly 0 or 1, no clue can ever change your mind.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a spam filter or a recommender keeps a belief and updates it with each new word or click. Bayesian inference and Bayesian optimization are this idea with more clues ([21.1.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md), [21.1.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)).
- **Computer science:** debugging is updating. A crash that happens only on one machine multiplies the odds toward "environment problem" and away from "logic bug".
- **Science and medicine:** a doctor starts from the base rate of each illness and updates it with every symptom and test. This is a model, not medical advice.
- **Sport:** after 3 matches, is a new cricket player a star or lucky? Belief moves with each match, and it moves slowly when the sample is small ([22.7](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md)).
- **Everyday life:** reading reviews, judging a new colleague, deciding if the monsoon has started.
- **Try this today:** pick one thing you are unsure about (for example "my project will finish on time"). Write your first percentage. After each piece of news, write "clue, strength (LR), new percentage". Never use 0% or 100%.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Convert: (a) a probability of 0.8 to odds; (b) odds of 3 to 2 to a probability.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $\text{odds} = \frac{0.8}{0.2} = 4$, which is "4 to 1".

(b) Odds of $\frac{3}{2} = 1.5$ give $P = \frac{1.5}{1 + 1.5} = 0.6$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You think there is a 10% chance that a restaurant is excellent. A reviewer's praise is 5 times as likely if it is excellent. What is your new belief?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds: $\frac{0.10}{0.90} = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$. After the clue: $0.111 \times 5 = 0.5556$.

$$P = \frac{0.5556}{1 + 0.5556} \approx 0.357$$

About 36%. The praise is helpful, but the low prior keeps the belief below one half.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Prior: 5% that an email is phishing. Two independent clues have LR 3 and LR 4. What is the posterior?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds: $\frac{0.05}{0.95} = 0.05263$. Multiply by $3 \times 4 = 12$: $0.05263 \times 12 = 0.6316$.

$$P = \frac{0.6316}{1.6316} \approx 0.387$$

About 39%.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** The chance of rain today is 30% from the forecast. A cloudy morning happens on 90% of rainy days and 40% of dry days. Then the barometer falls, which has LR 2 for rain. What is the chance of rain after each clue?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds: $\frac{0.3}{0.7} = 0.4286$. Cloudy: $\text{LR} = \frac{0.9}{0.4} = 2.25$, so the odds are $0.4286 \times 2.25 = 0.9643$ and $P = \frac{0.9643}{1.9643} \approx 0.491$.

Barometer: the odds become $0.9643 \times 2 = 1.9286$ and $P = \frac{1.9286}{2.9286} \approx 0.659$.

So 30%, then 49%, then 66%.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that if $E_1$ and $E_2$ are conditionally independent given $H$ and given $H^c$, then updating on $E_1$ first and then on $E_2$ gives the same result as updating on both at once. Also show that a prior of $P(H) = 0$ stays at 0.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Updating on both at once gives $\text{odds}(H) \cdot \frac{P(E_1, E_2 \mid H)}{P(E_1, E_2 \mid H^c)}$. By conditional independence:

$$\frac{P(E_1, E_2 \mid H)}{P(E_1, E_2 \mid H^c)} = \frac{P(E_1 \mid H) P(E_2 \mid H)}{P(E_1 \mid H^c) P(E_2 \mid H^c)} = \text{LR}_1 \cdot \text{LR}_2$$

Updating one at a time gives $\left( \text{odds}(H) \cdot \text{LR}_1 \right) \cdot \text{LR}_2$, which is the same product. Multiplication is commutative, so the order of the clues does not matter either. If $P(H) = 0$, then $\text{odds}(H) = 0$ and $0 \times \text{LR}_1 \times \text{LR}_2 = 0$, as long as the LRs are finite numbers.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 1 by simulation: 60% of students pass; the mock-score clue appears for 80% of passers and 30% of failers; the low-study clue appears for 30% of passers and 60% of failers, independently given pass or fail.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000
passes = rng.random(n) < 0.60                                    # prior: 60% pass
high_mock = rng.random(n) < np.where(passes, 0.80, 0.30)         # clue 1: mock score of 70 or more
little_study = rng.random(n) < np.where(passes, 0.30, 0.60)      # clue 2: under 5 hours last week

print(f"P(pass | clue 1)        = {passes[high_mock].mean():.4f}   (exact 0.8000)")
both = high_mock & little_study
print(f"P(pass | clue 1 and 2)  = {passes[both].mean():.4f}   (exact 0.6667)")
```

Output:

```text
P(pass | clue 1)        = 0.8001   (exact 0.8000)
P(pass | clue 1 and 2)  = 0.6658   (exact 0.6667)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Keep a number for how sure you are, and update it with every clue.
- Use odds: new odds equal old odds times the likelihood ratio of the clue.
- A clue with LR near 1 is weak. An LR of 10 or more, or $\frac{1}{10}$ or less, is strong.
- Multiply the LRs of several clues only if they are independent given the hypothesis. The order does not matter.
- Never start at 0% or 100%, because then no clue can move you.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times \frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}}
$$
