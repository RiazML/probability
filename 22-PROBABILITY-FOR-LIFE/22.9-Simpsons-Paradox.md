# 22.9 Simpson's Paradox

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.9 Simpson's Paradox

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [22.8 Correlation vs Causation](22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md). You need [4.11.4 Simpson's Paradox](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.4-Simpsons-Paradox.md), which proves the paradox. Here we ask what to do about it.

> **Big idea:** When a trend reverses after you split the data into groups, both tables are true, and the way the data came about (the cause story) tells you which table answers your question.

---

## Why It Matters

Two surgeons, two batters, two coaching centres, two versions of an app: in each case one can beat the other inside every group and still lose overall. [4.11.4](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.4-Simpsons-Paradox.md) shows why this is possible: an overall rate is a weighted average, and the two sides can have very different weights. That file leaves one question open: **which table should you believe?** This file answers it with a simple rule, shows the number that "removes" the mix, and warns about the case where splitting the data is the wrong move.

## Intuition

Two batters, A and B, are compared by how often they score a fifty. The bowling is of two kinds: pace (hard) and spin (easier). Batter A opens, so he faces mostly new-ball pace. Batter B comes in later and faces mostly spin.

Against pace, A scores a fifty in 30% of his innings and B in 25%. Against spin, A does it in 60% and B in 55%. So A is better against both kinds of bowling. But overall, A has 36% and B has 49%! B looks better only because most of his innings were against the easier bowling.

The overall number mixes two things: how good the batter is, and how hard his innings were. A fair comparison must hold the difficulty equal.

## Picture

```text
                     vs pace (hard)    vs spin (easier)    overall
  Batter A            24/80 = 30%       12/20 = 60%       36/100 = 36%
  Batter B             5/20 = 25%       44/80 = 55%       49/100 = 49%
                       A better          A better           B better

  A's innings:  [########## pace 80 ##########][## spin 20 ##]   mostly hard
  B's innings:  [## pace 20 ##][########## spin 80 ##########]   mostly easy
```

A wins each column but loses the last one, because the last column weights the two columns differently for A and B.

## Definition

Let two options $A$ and $B$ be tried in groups $g = 1, \dots, k$. Write $u_g$ for the share of the cases of $A$ that fall in group $g$, $v_g$ for the same share for $B$, and $r_{Ag}$, $r_{Bg}$ for the success rates inside group $g$. The overall rates are weighted averages:

$$
r_A = \sum_{g} u_g \thinspace r_{Ag}, \qquad r_B = \sum_{g} v_g \thinspace r_{Bg}
$$

**In words:** each overall rate is the average of the group rates, weighted by how many of that option's cases fell in each group. **Simpson's paradox** is the case where $r_{Ag} \gt r_{Bg}$ for every $g$ and still $r_A \lt r_B$.

To remove the effect of different mixes, we use **standardization**: compare both options with the **same** weights $w_g$ (for example, the mix of all cases together):

$$
r_A^{\text{adj}} = \sum_{g} w_g \thinspace r_{Ag}, \qquad r_B^{\text{adj}} = \sum_{g} w_g \thinspace r_{Bg}
$$

**In words:** ask "what would each rate be if both had faced the same mix of hard and easy cases?"

## Why It Is True

Split the overall difference into two parts by adding and subtracting $\sum_g u_g r_{Bg}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_A - r_B &= \sum_g u_g r_{Ag} - \sum_g v_g r_{Bg} && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \underbrace{\sum_g u_g (r_{Ag} - r_{Bg})}_{\text{like-for-like difference}} + \underbrace{\sum_g (u_g - v_g) \thinspace r_{Bg}}_{\text{mix effect}} && \text{(add and subtract } \textstyle\sum_g u_g r_{Bg} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**In words:** the overall gap equals the average within-group gap, plus an effect from the two options facing different mixes.

If $A$ is better in every group, the first part is positive. The paradox needs the second part to be negative and bigger. With two groups, $u_2 - v_2 = -(u_1 - v_1)$, so the mix effect is $(u_1 - v_1)(r_{B1} - r_{B2})$. It is nonzero **only if** the mixes differ ($u_1 \ne v_1$) **and** the groups have different success rates ($r_{B1} \ne r_{B2}$). If both options have the same mix, the mix effect is zero and no reversal is possible.

**Which table to trust.** The numbers cannot decide. The story of how the data arose can. Ask: **"Does the grouping variable come before the choice, or after it?"**

- **The group is set before the choice and affects both the choice and the result** (a confounder, [22.8](22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md)): the type of bowling, the device of the visitor, the size of the stone. Then **compare inside the groups**, or use standardized rates.
- **The group is a result of the choice** (a step on the way from the choice to the result): then splitting hides part of the effect, and the **overall** comparison is the right one if the choice was made by chance (Example 3).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Two Batters, Fairly

**Problem.** Use the table in the Picture. Find the overall difference, split it into the like-for-like part and the mix effect, and find the adjusted rates with the same 50-50 mix of pace and spin.

**Solution.**

1. $r_A = \frac{36}{100} = 0.36$ and $r_B = \frac{49}{100} = 0.49$, so $r_A - r_B = -0.13$.
2. The weights: $u = (0.8, 0.2)$ and $v = (0.2, 0.8)$. Like-for-like part: $0.8 \times (0.30 - 0.25) + 0.2 \times (0.60 - 0.55) = 0.04 + 0.01 = +0.05$.
3. Mix effect: $(0.8 - 0.2) \times 0.25 + (0.2 - 0.8) \times 0.55 = 0.15 - 0.33 = -0.18$.
4. Check: $0.05 - 0.18 = -0.13$.
5. Adjusted rates with a 50-50 mix: $r_A^{\text{adj}} = 0.5 \times 0.30 + 0.5 \times 0.60 = 0.45$ and $r_B^{\text{adj}} = 0.5 \times 0.25 + 0.5 \times 0.55 = 0.40$.

**Answer.** Overall A trails B by 13 points, but A is 5 points better like for like. The mix effect is $-18$ points: B's easy bowling. On equal terms A scores a fifty in 45% of innings and B in 40%.

**Check.** The simulation below gives $0.450$ and $0.400$ for the equal mix. The batting average of real players mixes many more factors, so this is a simple model.

### Example 2: Which App Design?

**Problem.** An online shop shows an old design (A) and a new design (B). Made-up counts:

| | Mobile visitors | Desktop visitors | All visitors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old design A | 40 of 1,000 buy | 400 of 4,000 buy | 440 of 5,000 |
| New design B | 200 of 4,000 buy | 110 of 1,000 buy | 310 of 5,000 |

Which design is better?

**Solution.**

1. Mobile: A buys at rate $\frac{40}{1000} = 0.04$ (4%) and B at rate $\frac{200}{4000} = 0.05$ (5%). B is better.
2. Desktop: A has $\frac{400}{4000} = 0.10$ (10%) and B has $\frac{110}{1000} = 0.11$ (11%). B is better.
3. Overall: A has $\frac{440}{5000} = 0.088$ (8.8%) and B has $\frac{310}{5000} = 0.062$ (6.2%). A looks better.
4. The device is known before the visitor sees any design. It changes the chance of buying (desktop buyers are more likely), and it changed who saw which design (B was shown mostly on mobile). So the device is a confounder.

**Answer.** Trust the split table: B is better on both devices (by 1 point each). The overall numbers punish B because it was shown to more mobile visitors, who buy less.

**Check.** With equal mixes of 50% mobile and 50% desktop: A has $0.5 \times 0.04 + 0.5 \times 0.10 = 0.07$ and B has $0.5 \times 0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.11 = 0.08$. The best fix is not the arithmetic, but to **randomize** which design each visitor sees, so that both designs get the same mix.

### Example 3: When Splitting Is the Mistake

**Problem.** A drug is given to half of the patients, chosen by a coin. The drug works by bringing the blood pressure (BP) to normal: it makes normal BP happen in 80% of treated patients and in 20% of untreated patients. Recovery depends only on BP: 90% recover with normal BP, and 40% with high BP, whatever the drug. (Made-up numbers.) Compare treated and untreated patients overall, and inside the BP groups.

**Solution.**

1. Overall, treated: $0.8 \times 0.9 + 0.2 \times 0.4 = 0.72 + 0.08 = 0.80$.
2. Overall, untreated: $0.2 \times 0.9 + 0.8 \times 0.4 = 0.18 + 0.32 = 0.50$.
3. Inside the normal-BP group: both recover at 90%. Inside the high-BP group: both recover at 40%.

**Answer.** Overall, the drug raises recovery from 50% to 80%. Inside each BP group the difference is zero.

**Check.** The BP group is **a result of the drug**, not a cause. Comparing inside BP groups compares people who got the same BP, and it removes exactly the route through which the drug works. Since the treatment was assigned by a coin, the overall comparison is the fair one: +30 points. The rule is "split on things that come before the choice, not on things that come after it".

## Simulate It

The code simulates both batters and the drug example, and prints the rates overall and by group.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000

# Fifty rate against pace (hard) and spin (easier). Batter A faces mostly pace, B mostly spin.
rate = {"A": (0.30, 0.60), "B": (0.25, 0.55)}        # (vs pace, vs spin)
share_pace = {"A": 0.80, "B": 0.20}
overall = {}
for b in "AB":
    vs_pace = rng.random(n) < share_pace[b]
    fifty = rng.random(n) < np.where(vs_pace, rate[b][0], rate[b][1])
    overall[b] = fifty.mean()
    print(f"{b}: vs pace {fifty[vs_pace].mean():.3f}, vs spin {fifty[~vs_pace].mean():.3f}, overall {overall[b]:.3f}")
print(f"Exact overall: A {0.8 * 0.30 + 0.2 * 0.60:.3f}, B {0.2 * 0.25 + 0.8 * 0.55:.3f}")
print(f"Same mix (50% pace) for both: A {0.5 * 0.30 + 0.5 * 0.60:.3f}, B {0.5 * 0.25 + 0.5 * 0.55:.3f}")

# A drug is given by coin. It works through blood pressure (BP): the groups are a RESULT of the drug.
drug = rng.random(n) < 0.5
bp_normal = rng.random(n) < np.where(drug, 0.8, 0.2)
recover = rng.random(n) < np.where(bp_normal, 0.9, 0.4)       # recovery depends only on BP
print(f"Overall recovery: drug {recover[drug].mean():.3f}, no drug {recover[~drug].mean():.3f}")
for name, g in [("normal BP", bp_normal), ("high BP", ~bp_normal)]:
    print(f"{name:>9}: drug {recover[drug & g].mean():.3f}, no drug {recover[~drug & g].mean():.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
A: vs pace 0.301, vs spin 0.599, overall 0.360
B: vs pace 0.250, vs spin 0.551, overall 0.491
Exact overall: A 0.360, B 0.490
Same mix (50% pace) for both: A 0.450, B 0.400
Overall recovery: drug 0.800, no drug 0.499
normal BP: drug 0.900, no drug 0.900
  high BP: drug 0.401, no drug 0.399
```

The first part shows the reversal: A wins both columns and loses overall, and wins again with equal mixes. The second part is the opposite warning: here the group table shows no effect, and the overall table shows the true +30 points.

## Common Mistakes

- **Always trusting the split table.** Splitting is right when the group is set before the choice (a confounder). It is wrong when the group is something the choice changed (Example 3).
- **Always trusting the overall table.** Overall rates mix the quality of the option with the mix of the cases. When the mixes differ, the overall comparison is unfair (Examples 1 and 2).
- **Looking only at the numbers.** The same table can need different answers for different causal stories. Write the story first: "which variable comes first, and who chose?"
- **Splitting too much.** If you keep cutting the data into smaller groups, each group has few cases and its rate is noisy ([22.7](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md)). And with many cuts, some will show a reversal by luck.
- **Thinking that the paradox only appears in weird data.** It appears whenever the mixes differ and the groups differ in difficulty, which is common in real data on hospitals, schools, sports and products.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a model can win on every subgroup of the data and lose on the total, or the other way round, when the test sets of two models have a different mix. Compare models on the same test set, and report results per group ([4.11.4](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.4-Simpsons-Paradox.md), [21.1.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)).
- **Computer science:** A/B tests must randomize users. If one design is shown more on phones, the overall numbers mix device and design (Example 2).
- **Medicine:** treatments are compared inside groups of patients with the same severity, or in randomized trials. (This file is not medical advice.)
- **Education and sport:** school pass rates, hospital survival rates and batting records all depend on the mix of hard and easy cases.
- **Everyday life:** "Our shop has the better average rating" may be only a different mix of products. When two averages are compared, ask who is in each group.
- **Try this today:** when a comparison of two averages surprises you, ask two questions: "Is there a variable that comes **before** the choice and changes the result?" and "Do the two options have the same mix of it?" If not, compare inside the groups.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Use the batters of Example 1 with a mix of 70% pace and 30% spin for both. Find the adjusted rates.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$r_A^{\text{adj}} = 0.7 \times 0.30 + 0.3 \times 0.60 = 0.21 + 0.18 = 0.39$$

$$r_B^{\text{adj}} = 0.7 \times 0.25 + 0.3 \times 0.55 = 0.175 + 0.165 = 0.34$$

A is better by 5 points, for any common mix, because he is better by 5 points in each group.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** What two conditions must hold for Simpson's paradox to be possible?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(1) The two options have different mixes of the groups ($u_g \ne v_g$). (2) The groups have different success rates ($r_{B1} \ne r_{B2}$). If either condition fails, the mix effect is zero and no reversal can happen.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Centre P has 90 passes out of 100 students who took the easy paper, and 10 out of 50 who took the hard paper. Centre Q has 19 out of 20 (easy) and 40 out of 100 (hard). Find the pass rates by paper and overall. Which centre would you choose for a student who will take the hard paper?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Easy paper: P has 90% and Q has 95%. Hard paper: P has 20% and Q has 40%. Overall: P has $\frac{100}{150} = 0.667$ and Q has $\frac{59}{120} = 0.492$.

Q is better on each paper, but worse overall because most of its students took the hard paper. For a student who will take the hard paper, choose Q (40% against 20%).

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Split the overall difference $r_Q - r_P$ of problem 3 into the like-for-like part and the mix effect, with $u$ the weights of Q and $v$ the weights of P.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Weights of Q (easy, hard): $u = \left(\frac{1}{6}, \frac{5}{6}\right)$. Weights of P: $v = \left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{3}\right)$.

Like-for-like: $\frac{1}{6}(0.95 - 0.90) + \frac{5}{6}(0.40 - 0.20) = 0.00833 + 0.16667 = +0.175$.

Mix effect: $\left(\frac{1}{6} - \frac{2}{3}\right)(0.90) + \left(\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{3}\right)(0.20) = -0.45 + 0.10 = -0.35$.

Total: $0.175 - 0.35 = -0.175$, and indeed $0.4917 - 0.6667 = -0.175$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove the decomposition $r_A - r_B = \sum_g u_g (r_{Ag} - r_{Bg}) + \sum_g (u_g - v_g) r_{Bg}$. Then show that with two groups the mix effect is $(u_1 - v_1)(r_{B1} - r_{B2})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand the right side:

$$\sum_g u_g r_{Ag} - \sum_g u_g r_{Bg} + \sum_g u_g r_{Bg} - \sum_g v_g r_{Bg} = \sum_g u_g r_{Ag} - \sum_g v_g r_{Bg} = r_A - r_B$$

The two middle sums cancel. For two groups, $u_1 + u_2 = 1$ and $v_1 + v_2 = 1$, so $u_2 - v_2 = -(u_1 - v_1)$. Then:

$$\sum_g (u_g - v_g) r_{Bg} = (u_1 - v_1) r_{B1} - (u_1 - v_1) r_{B2} = (u_1 - v_1)(r_{B1} - r_{B2})$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 3 by simulation. Use the shares of easy-paper students $\frac{2}{3}$ for P and $\frac{1}{6}$ for Q.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000

# Pass rates (easy paper, hard paper). Centre Q is better in both, but takes mostly hard-paper students.
pass_rate = {"P": (0.90, 0.20), "Q": (0.95, 0.40)}
share_easy = {"P": 2 / 3, "Q": 1 / 6}
for c in "PQ":
    easy = rng.random(n) < share_easy[c]
    passed = rng.random(n) < np.where(easy, pass_rate[c][0], pass_rate[c][1])
    exact = share_easy[c] * pass_rate[c][0] + (1 - share_easy[c]) * pass_rate[c][1]
    print(f"Centre {c}: easy {passed[easy].mean():.3f}, hard {passed[~easy].mean():.3f}, "
          f"overall {passed.mean():.3f} (exact {exact:.3f})")
```

Output:

```text
Centre P: easy 0.900, hard 0.200, overall 0.667 (exact 0.667)
Centre Q: easy 0.950, hard 0.401, overall 0.492 (exact 0.492)
```

</details>

## Summary

- An overall rate is a weighted average, so different mixes of easy and hard cases can reverse a comparison.
- Overall gap = like-for-like gap + mix effect. The paradox needs different mixes and different group rates.
- If the grouping variable comes before the choice and affects the result, compare inside groups (or standardize to a common mix).
- If the grouping variable is a result of the choice, do not split: the overall table is the fair one when the choice was random.
- The best protection is to randomize the choice, which gives equal mixes.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{r_A - r_B = \sum_g u_g (r_{Ag} - r_{Bg}) + \sum_g (u_g - v_g) \thinspace r_{Bg}}
$$
