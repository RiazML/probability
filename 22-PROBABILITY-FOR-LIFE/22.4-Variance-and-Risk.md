> **Big idea:** Two choices can have the same average and very different risk, so always ask both "what do I get on average?" and "how bad can it get, and how likely is that?".

---

## Why It Matters

The average hides the swings. A route that is 2 minutes faster on average can make you late for an exam much more often. A job with a bigger average pay can leave you short of rent in a bad month. **Risk** is about the spread of the results and especially about the bad end of it. This file shows how to measure the spread with the standard deviation, how to turn it into the chance of a bad result, and why splitting a risk into independent parts helps. It also shows the limit: parts that move together cannot cancel each other.

## Intuition

You must reach an exam hall in 40 minutes. By bus, the trip takes 30 minutes on average, and it hardly changes from day to day (the standard deviation is 5 minutes). By motorbike, the trip takes 28 minutes on average, but traffic and parking make it vary a lot (standard deviation 10 minutes). The motorbike wins on average by 2 minutes. Which one do you choose on exam day?

The bus. On the bus you are late only if the trip is 2 standard deviations above its average. On the motorbike you are late if it is only 1.2 standard deviations above. In numbers, the bus makes you late on about 2.3% of days and the motorbike on about 11.5% of days: five times more often. (Normal travel times are a simplification, but the idea does not depend on it.)

When the cost of a bad day is high, choose the option with the smaller spread, even if its average is a bit worse.

Each row shows the usual range of the trip: `#` is the average plus or minus 1 standard deviation, `.` is plus or minus 2 standard deviations. The motorbike row is much wider, and it crosses the exam start line.

## Definition

For a result $X$ with mean $\mu = E[X]$, the **variance** and the **standard deviation** measure the typical size of the distance from the mean ([6.5](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)):

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E\left[(X - \mu)^2\right], \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}
$$

**In words:** the standard deviation $\sigma$ is the typical distance of a result from its average, in the same unit as the result (minutes, taka, marks).

In daily life, we call a spread **risk** when the bad end of it matters. Two numbers describe risk well:

- the **standard deviation** $\sigma$, for the size of the swings;
- the **shortfall chance** $P(X \lt c)$ (or $P(X \gt c)$), for the chance of a result worse than a limit $c$ that you care about, such as "late for the exam" or "less than the rent".

If you know only $\mu$ and $\sigma$, **Chebyshev's inequality** ([16.2](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)) gives a safe bound for any distribution:

$$
P\left( \lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge k \sigma \right) \le \frac{1}{k^2}
$$

**In words:** at most one result in $k^2$ is $k$ or more standard deviations away from the average, whatever the shape.

**Diversification.** Take $n$ results $X_1, \dots, X_n$, each with standard deviation $\sigma$, and suppose every pair has the same correlation $\rho$ ([11.2](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md)). The variance of their average $\bar{X}$ is:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left( 1 + (n - 1) \rho \right)
$$

**In words:** if the parts are independent ($\rho = 0$), the variance of the average falls like $\frac{1}{n}$. If the parts are tied together ($\rho = 1$), the average is as risky as one part.

## Why It Is True

The variance of a sum is the sum of all the variances and covariances ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)). There are $n$ variances $\sigma^2$ and $n(n-1)$ ordered pairs $(i, j)$ with $i \ne j$, each with covariance $\rho \sigma^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + \dots + X_n) &= n \sigma^2 + n(n-1) \rho \sigma^2 && \text{(variances plus covariances)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n \sigma^2 \left( 1 + (n-1) \rho \right) && \text{(} \operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) \text{, with } a = \tfrac{1}{n} \text{)} \\
&= \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left( 1 + (n-1) \rho \right) && \text{(simplify)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now look at the limit. When $n$ grows, $\frac{1 + (n-1)\rho}{n}$ tends to $\rho$. So $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) \to \rho \sigma^2$: **the part of the risk that all sources share can never be averaged away**. Only the separate part, the fraction $1 - \rho$, disappears.

> **Note:** This is a simple model. It treats the parts as equal in size and correlation, and it uses the normal curve to turn a standard deviation into a chance. Real risks have different sizes and heavier tails ([22.13](22.13-Fat-Tails.md)). It is a way to think, not a prediction and not financial advice.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Bus or Motorbike

**Problem.** The exam starts in 40 minutes. Bus time is $\mathcal{N}(30, 5^2)$ and motorbike time is $\mathcal{N}(28, 10^2)$ (mean and variance, as in [9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)). Find the chance of being late with each. What changes if you leave 5 minutes earlier?

**Solution.**

1. Convert to standard units. Bus: $z = \frac{40 - 30}{5} = 2$. Motorbike: $z = \frac{40 - 28}{10} = 1.2$.
2. Use the standard normal tail: $P(Z \gt 2) = 0.0228$ and $P(Z \gt 1.2) = 0.1151$.
3. With 5 extra minutes the limit is 45. Bus: $z = \frac{45 - 30}{5} = 3$, so the chance is $0.00135$. Motorbike: $z = \frac{45 - 28}{10} = 1.7$, so the chance is $0.0446$.

**Answer.** Late with the bus: 2.3%. Late with the motorbike: 11.5%. After leaving 5 minutes earlier: 0.13% and 4.5%.

**Check.** The motorbike is faster on average and still 5 times more likely to make you late. Leaving earlier helps the bus much more in relative terms, because its tail drops fast. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Four Small Incomes

**Problem.** You can earn from 4 different sources (for example 4 clients or 4 small jobs). Each pays on average 10,000 taka per month with standard deviation 6,000 taka. You split your time equally, so your income is the average of the four. Find the standard deviation of your income, and the chance that it is below 5,000 taka (use the normal curve), when the four sources are (a) independent, (b) correlated with $\rho = 0.5$, (c) completely tied together. Compare with using only one source.

**Solution.**

1. One source: $\sigma = 6000$. The shortfall: $z = \frac{5000 - 10000}{6000} = -0.833$, and $P = 0.2023$.
2. (a) $\rho = 0$: $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = 6000 \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} = 3000$. Then $z = \frac{-5000}{3000} = -1.667$ and $P = 0.0478$.
3. (b) $\rho = 0.5$: $1 + 3 \times 0.5 = 2.5$, so $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = 6000 \sqrt{\frac{2.5}{4}} = 6000 \times 0.7906 = 4743$. Then $z = \frac{-5000}{4743} = -1.054$ and $P = 0.1459$.
4. (c) $\rho = 1$: $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = 6000 \sqrt{\frac{4}{4}} = 6000$, and $P = 0.2023$ again.

**Answer.** The average income is 10,000 taka in all cases. The chance of falling below 5,000 taka is 4.8% for independent sources, 14.6% for sources with correlation 0.5, and 20.2% for tied sources (the same as one source).

**Check.** Four independent sources halve the standard deviation ($\frac{1}{\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{2}$). Correlation eats most of the gain: with $\rho = 0.5$ the standard deviation falls only from 6000 to 4743. For example, four clients who all depend on the same industry are not four independent sources.

### Example 3: Reading the Job Offer Again

**Problem.** In [22.3](22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md), Offer A pays 50,000 taka for sure, and Offer B pays 30,000 taka plus a 40,000-taka bonus with probability 0.6. Find the standard deviation of B and the chance that B pays less than A.

**Solution.**

1. The bonus is $40{,}000 \times I$, where $I$ is Bernoulli with $p = 0.6$ ([7.1](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.1-Bernoulli.md)). So $\operatorname{Var}(B) = 40{,}000^2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 1.6 \times 10^9 \times 0.24 = 3.84 \times 10^8$.
2. $\sigma_B = \sqrt{3.84 \times 10^8} \approx 19{,}596$ taka.
3. B pays less than A (30,000 against 50,000) exactly when the bonus is missed: probability $0.4$.

**Answer.** B has the higher average (54,000 against 50,000) but a standard deviation of about 19,600 taka, and it pays 20,000 taka less than A in 40% of the months. A has no risk at all.

**Check.** The average gain of 4,000 taka comes with a 40% chance of a 20,000-taka loss relative to A. Whether this is worth it depends on your savings and your needs. Do not use only the average.

## Simulate It

The code checks Examples 1 and 2: how often each route is late, and how the risk of the averaged income changes with the correlation.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Two ways to reach the exam, which starts after 40 minutes (normal travel times).
bus = rng.normal(30, 5, n)
motorbike = rng.normal(28, 10, n)
print(f"Late by bus:       simulation {np.mean(bus > 40):.4f}, exact {norm.sf(40, 30, 5):.4f}")
print(f"Late by motorbike: simulation {np.mean(motorbike > 40):.4f}, exact {norm.sf(40, 28, 10):.4f}")

# Average of 4 income sources, each with mean 10,000 and SD 6,000 taka.
# A shared shock (weight rho) makes the sources move together.
sigma, k = 6000, 4
for rho in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0]:
    shared = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 1))
    own = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, k))
    sources = 10_000 + sigma * (np.sqrt(rho) * shared + np.sqrt(1 - rho) * own)
    average = sources.mean(axis=1)
    sd_exact = sigma * np.sqrt((1 + (k - 1) * rho) / k)
    print(f"rho {rho}: SD {average.std():6.0f} (exact {sd_exact:6.0f}),"
          f" P(below 5,000) {np.mean(average < 5000):.4f} (exact {norm.cdf(5000, 10_000, sd_exact):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Late by bus:       simulation 0.0228, exact 0.0228
Late by motorbike: simulation 0.1150, exact 0.1151
rho 0.0: SD   3002 (exact   3000), P(below 5,000) 0.0479 (exact 0.0478)
rho 0.5: SD   4741 (exact   4743), P(below 5,000) 0.1462 (exact 0.1459)
rho 1.0: SD   6003 (exact   6000), P(below 5,000) 0.2024 (exact 0.2023)
```

The shared shock in the code is the part of the risk that does not average out. At $\rho = 1$ the four sources are really one source.

## Common Mistakes

- **Comparing only the averages.** Two options with equal averages are not equal. Look at the spread and at the chance of the bad result you cannot accept.
- **Thinking that a high standard deviation is always bad.** Spread also means more chance of a very good result. It is the **bad end** that you must be able to survive. A student who needs 90 marks to win a scholarship may want a risky plan.
- **Assuming independence.** Sources of income, investments and risks often share a cause (the economy, the weather, one boss). The correlation decides how much diversification helps.
- **Using the standard deviation when the tail is heavy.** A normal curve says that a 5-sigma day almost never happens. In real life with heavy tails, it happens much more often ([22.13](22.13-Fat-Tails.md)).
- **Forgetting ruin.** If one bad result ends the game (no money left, a missed once-only exam), the long-run average does not matter. See [22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md) and [22.14](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the variance of an estimate tells how stable a model is. An ensemble of models reduces variance in the same way as averaging independent sources ([21.1.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)).
- **Computer science:** a server that answers in 100 ms on average but sometimes in 5 seconds feels slow. Engineers watch the tail of the delay, not only the mean ([21.3.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
- **Finance and insurance:** risk is measured with the standard deviation and with the chance of a large loss, and a portfolio mixes assets whose correlation is low ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)). This is a model, not investment advice.
- **Engineering:** a bridge or a power system is built with safety margins that cover several standard deviations of the load.
- **Everyday life:** leaving early, keeping savings for a rainy month, not putting all your study time into one topic.
- **Try this today:** for the next decision, write down not only "what will probably happen" but also "what is the worst result I could really live with, and how likely is it?" If that chance is more than a few percent, look for a safer option or a margin.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Game A: you win 100 taka or lose 100 taka, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Game B: you win 10 or lose 10, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Compare the averages and the standard deviations.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Both averages are $0$. For game A, $E[X^2] = 100^2 = 10{,}000$, so $\sigma_A = 100$. For game B, $E[X^2] = 100$, so $\sigma_B = 10$.

The averages are equal, but game A has 10 times the swings. Game A is the riskier game.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** The time to get a visa decision has mean 20 days and standard deviation 4 days. You know nothing else about its shape. What is the largest possible chance that it is 32 days or more, or 8 days or less?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The distance from the mean is 12 days, which is $k = \frac{12}{4} = 3$ standard deviations. By Chebyshev:

$$P(\lvert X - 20 \rvert \ge 12) \le \frac{1}{3^2} = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$$

So at most about 11% of the cases are that far from 20 days, for any shape. A normal curve would say about 0.27%, but you cannot assume it.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Two independent clients each pay on average 100 taka per job with standard deviation 30. Compare two plans: (a) half of the time with each client, so your pay is the sum of one job from each; (b) all the time with client 1, so your pay is twice one job. Find the mean and standard deviation of each plan.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Mean: $100 + 100 = 200$. Variance: $30^2 + 30^2 = 1800$ (independent), so $\sigma = \sqrt{1800} \approx 42.4$.

(b) Mean: $2 \times 100 = 200$. Variance: $2^2 \times 30^2 = 3600$, so $\sigma = 60$.

The means are equal. Splitting your time over two independent clients cuts the standard deviation from 60 to about 42.4, which is a factor of $\sqrt{2}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Use the normal curve for problem 3 and find the chance that your pay is below 120 taka in each plan.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Plan (a): $z = \frac{120 - 200}{42.43} = -1.886$, and $P \approx 0.0297$. Plan (b): $z = \frac{120 - 200}{60} = -1.333$, and $P \approx 0.0912$.

Splitting makes a bad month about 3 times less likely (3.0% against 9.1%).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for $n$ variables with equal variance $\sigma^2$ and equal pairwise correlation $\rho$, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}(1 + (n-1)\rho)$. Then explain why $\rho \ge -\frac{1}{n-1}$ must hold.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\operatorname{Var}(\sum_i X_i) = \sum_i \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + \sum_{i \ne j} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$. There are $n$ variance terms $\sigma^2$ and $n(n-1)$ ordered pairs, each with covariance $\rho \sigma \cdot \sigma = \rho \sigma^2$. So:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\textstyle\sum_i X_i) = n \sigma^2 + n(n-1)\rho \sigma^2 = n \sigma^2 (1 + (n-1)\rho)$$

Dividing by $n^2$ (because $\operatorname{Var}(aY) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y)$) gives $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}(1 + (n-1)\rho)$.

A variance cannot be negative, so $1 + (n-1)\rho \ge 0$, which means $\rho \ge -\frac{1}{n-1}$. For example, with $n = 3$ the bound is $-0.5$, so three variables cannot all have correlation $-0.9$ with each other.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problems 3 and 4 by simulation, with normal pay.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
client_1 = rng.normal(100, 30, n)       # pay of one client, mean 100 and SD 30
client_2 = rng.normal(100, 30, n)       # a second, independent client
split = client_1 + client_2             # half the time with each client
all_in = 2 * client_1                   # all the time with one client (pays double)

for name, pay, sd in [("split", split, np.sqrt(2) * 30), ("all-in", all_in, 60)]:
    print(f"{name:>6}: mean {pay.mean():.0f}, SD {pay.std():.2f} (exact {sd:.2f}),"
          f" P(<120) {np.mean(pay < 120):.4f} (exact {norm.cdf(120, 200, sd):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
 split: mean 200, SD 42.43 (exact 42.43), P(<120) 0.0297 (exact 0.0297)
all-in: mean 200, SD 60.03 (exact 60.00), P(<120) 0.0912 (exact 0.0912)
```

</details>

## Summary

- The average does not describe risk. Look at the standard deviation and at the chance of the bad result you care about.
- Use standard units: $z = \frac{\text{limit} - \text{mean}}{\sigma}$, then the normal tail, as a first estimate. Chebyshev gives a safe bound for any shape.
- Averaging $n$ independent sources divides the variance by $n$. With a common correlation $\rho$, the risk falls only to $\rho \sigma^2$ in the limit.
- Risks that share a cause cannot cancel each other. Before you trust a spread of risks, ask what they have in common.
- If one bad result ends the game, the average is not enough ([22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md), [22.14](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md)).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left( 1 + (n - 1) \rho \right)}
$$
