## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy)** *Uses [22.1 Base Rates](22.1-Base-Rates.md).* A condition affects 0.5% of people. A test catches 95% of the sick and wrongly flags 2% of the healthy. A person tests positive. How likely is the person to be sick?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

True positives: $0.005 \times 0.95 = 0.00475$. False positives: $0.995 \times 0.02 = 0.0199$.

$$P(\text{sick} \mid +) = \frac{0.00475}{0.00475 + 0.0199} = \frac{0.00475}{0.02465} \approx 0.193$$

About 19%: roughly 4 of every 5 positives are false alarms, although the test looks very good.

</details>

**W2. (Easy)** *Uses [22.3 Expected Value Decisions](22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md).* A raffle ticket costs 50 taka. One ticket in 2,000 wins 60,000 taka, and the others win nothing. What is the expected net gain per ticket?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The expected prize is $\frac{60000}{2000} = 30$ taka. The expected net gain:

$$30 - 50 = -20 \text{ taka}$$

You lose 20 taka per ticket on average.

</details>

**W3. (Easy)** *Uses [22.5 Gambler's Fallacy](22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md).* A fair die shows an even number 6 times in a row. What is the chance that the next roll is even? What was the chance of the 6-roll streak?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rolls are independent, so the next roll is even with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. The streak itself had chance $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^6 = \frac{1}{64} \approx 0.0156$, but it is already in the past and does not change the next roll.

</details>

**W4. (Easy)** *Uses [22.12 Compounding Small Risks](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md).* Each month there is a 2% chance of a problem with your phone, independently. What is the chance of at least one problem in 12 months?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P = 1 - 0.98^{12} = 1 - 0.7847 = 0.2153$$

About 21.5%, more than 10 times the monthly risk.

</details>

**W5. (Easy)** *Uses [22.11 Conjunction Fallacy](22.11-Conjunction-Fallacy.md).* The chance of rain tomorrow is 0.2. If it rains, the chance of a traffic jam is 0.5. What is the chance of "rain and a traffic jam"? Which is more probable: "rain", or "rain and a traffic jam"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{rain} \cap \text{jam}) = 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1$$

"Rain" (0.2) is more probable than "rain and a traffic jam" (0.1). A conjunction is never more likely than one of its parts.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium)** *Uses [22.2 Bayesian Updating in Daily Life](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md) and [22.1](22.1-Base-Rates.md).* You think 30% of online sellers of a certain product are scams. One seller has a price far below the market price, which is 6 times as likely for a scammer. The same seller has 200 good reviews, which is 4 times as likely for an honest seller (a likelihood ratio of $\frac{1}{4}$ for "scam"). Treat the two clues as independent given the type of seller. What is the chance that this seller is a scam?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds: $\frac{0.3}{0.7} = 0.4286$. Multiply by the two likelihood ratios:

$$0.4286 \times 6 \times 0.25 = 0.6429$$

$$P(\text{scam}) = \frac{0.6429}{1 + 0.6429} = 0.391$$

About 39%. The low price pushes the belief up from 30% to 72% ($0.4286 \times 6 = 2.571$, which is $\frac{2.571}{3.571} = 0.72$), and the reviews pull it back down. (Reviews can be bought, so in real life their likelihood ratio may be closer to 1.)

</details>

**C2. (Medium)** *Uses [22.6 Regression to the Mean](22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md).* In a league, batting averages have mean 35, and the correlation between a player's averages in two seasons is 0.4. A batter averaged 55 in season 1. What do you expect for season 2? What if the correlation were 0.9?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X_2 \mid X_1 = 55] = 35 + 0.4 \times (55 - 35) = 35 + 8 = 43$$

With a correlation of 0.9: $35 + 0.9 \times 20 = 53$.

With a low correlation (0.4), most of the 55 was luck, so the expected average falls to 43. With a high correlation (0.9), the average is mostly skill and stays near 53. Nothing needs to have "gone wrong" in either case.

</details>

**C3. (Medium)** *Uses [22.9 Simpson's Paradox](22.9-Simpsons-Paradox.md).* Teacher X passed 9 of 10 beginners and 30 of 90 advanced students. Teacher Y passed 80 of 90 beginners and 3 of 10 advanced students. Find the pass rates by group and overall. Which teacher would you choose for an advanced student? What are the rates for a 50-50 mix?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Beginners: X $= \frac{9}{10} = 0.900$, Y $= \frac{80}{90} = 0.889$. Advanced: X $= \frac{30}{90} = 0.333$, Y $= \frac{3}{10} = 0.300$. Overall: X $= \frac{39}{100} = 0.39$, Y $= \frac{83}{100} = 0.83$.

X is better in both groups, and Y looks far better overall, because Y teaches mostly beginners. For an advanced student, choose X (33% against 30%). With a 50-50 mix: X $= 0.5(0.900) + 0.5(0.333) = 0.617$ and Y $= 0.5(0.889) + 0.5(0.300) = 0.594$.

</details>

**C4. (Medium)** *Uses [22.3](22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md) and [22.4 Variance and Risk](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md).* Offer A pays 60,000 taka per month for sure. Offer B pays 40,000 taka or 90,000 taka per month, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Find the expected pay and the standard deviation of each. You need 45,000 taka every month for rent. Which offer would you take?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[A] = 60{,}000$ and $\sigma_A = 0$. For B: $E[B] = 0.5(40000) + 0.5(90000) = 65{,}000$. The distance from the mean is 25,000 in both cases, so $\sigma_B = 25{,}000$.

B has the higher average (by 5,000), but it pays 40,000 in half of the months, which is below the rent. So the chance of being short is 0.5 with B and 0 with A. If you have savings that cover a short month, B may be better. If you do not, A is the safer choice. The average alone does not decide.

</details>

**C5. (Medium)** *Uses [22.14 Kelly Criterion](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md).* A repeated bet wins with probability 0.55 and pays 1.5 taka for each taka staked ($b = 1.5$). Find the Kelly fraction and the growth rate per bet. Compare with half Kelly and with double Kelly.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f^{\ast} = p - \frac{q}{b} = 0.55 - \frac{0.45}{1.5} = 0.55 - 0.30 = 0.25$$

Growth rate $g(f) = 0.55 \ln(1 + 1.5 f) + 0.45 \ln(1 - f)$:

$g(0.25) = 0.55 \ln 1.375 + 0.45 \ln 0.75 = 0.55(0.31845) + 0.45(-0.28768) = 0.17515 - 0.12946 = 0.04569$.

Half Kelly ($f = 0.125$): $g = 0.55 \ln 1.1875 + 0.45 \ln 0.875 = 0.55(0.17185) + 0.45(-0.13353) = 0.09452 - 0.06009 = 0.03443$, which is 75% of the best growth.

Double Kelly ($f = 0.5$): $g = 0.55 \ln 1.75 + 0.45 \ln 0.5 = 0.55(0.55962) + 0.45(-0.69315) = 0.30779 - 0.31192 = -0.00413$, a small loss.

</details>

**C6. (Medium)** *Uses [22.15 Calibration](22.15-Calibration.md).* Your journal has 40 predictions. At "80%": 20 predictions, 14 came true. At "60%": 20 predictions, 13 came true. Find your average confidence, hit rate and Brier score. Compare with always saying 50% and always saying your hit rate.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Average confidence: $\frac{20(0.8) + 20(0.6)}{40} = 0.70$. Hit rate: $\frac{14 + 13}{40} = 0.675$.

Brier: at 80%, $14(0.2)^2 + 6(0.8)^2 = 0.56 + 3.84 = 4.40$. At 60%, $13(0.4)^2 + 7(0.6)^2 = 2.08 + 2.52 = 4.60$. Total $\frac{4.40 + 4.60}{40} = 0.225$.

Always 50% gives $0.250$. Always saying $0.675$ gives $0.675 \times 0.325 = 0.2194$. You are close to calibrated (2.5 points too confident), but the 80% group (70% hit rate) was no better than the 60% group (65%), so your "80%" does not carry much more information than your "60%". With 20 predictions per group, this is only a hint.

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard)** *Uses [22.1](22.1-Base-Rates.md) and [22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md). Includes a proof.* A condition has base rate 1%. A test has sensitivity 0.90 and false-positive rate 0.05. (a) Prove that if $k$ tests are independent given the true state and all are positive, the posterior odds equal the prior odds times $\left( \frac{s}{f} \right)^k$. (b) How many positive tests are needed to make the chance above 90%? (c) Why can this be too optimistic in real life?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Given the state, the tests are independent, so $P(+^k \mid H) = s^k$ and $P(+^k \mid H^c) = f^k$. By Bayes' theorem in odds form:

$$\frac{P(H \mid +^k)}{P(H^c \mid +^k)} = \frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)} \cdot \frac{s^k}{f^k} = \frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)} \left( \frac{s}{f} \right)^k$$

(b) The likelihood ratio of one test is $\frac{0.90}{0.05} = 18$. Prior odds: $\frac{0.01}{0.99} = 0.0101$. After 1 test: $0.0101 \times 18 = 0.182$, which is probability 0.154. After 2 tests: $0.0101 \times 324 = 3.27$, probability 0.766. After 3 tests: $0.0101 \times 5832 = 58.9$, probability 0.983. For 90% we need odds of at least 9, that is, $18^k \ge 891$, so $k \ge \frac{\ln 891}{\ln 18} = 2.35$. We need **3** positive tests.

(c) Repeating the same test often repeats the same error (a person with a certain trait may be flagged every time). Then the tests are not independent given the state, and the false-positive chances do not multiply. A second, different kind of test is worth more than a repeat of the first. This is a simple model, not medical advice.

</details>

**H2. (Hard)** *Uses [22.11 Conjunction Fallacy](22.11-Conjunction-Fallacy.md) and [22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md). Includes a proof.* A plan has 5 steps with failure chances $0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.10, 0.20$. (a) Prove that, whatever the dependence between the steps, $P(\text{plan works}) \ge 1 - \sum_i P(\text{step } i \text{ fails})$. (b) Evaluate the bound, and compare with the product of the success chances if the steps are independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Let $F_i$ be the event that step $i$ fails. The plan fails if at least one step fails, which is the union $F_1 \cup \dots \cup F_5$. By the union bound ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)), $P(F_1 \cup \dots \cup F_5) \le \sum_i P(F_i)$. So:

$$P(\text{plan works}) = 1 - P(F_1 \cup \dots \cup F_5) \ge 1 - \sum_i P(F_i)$$

No independence is needed.

(b) The sum of the failure chances is $0.05 + 0.10 + 0.15 + 0.10 + 0.20 = 0.60$, so the plan works with probability at least $1 - 0.60 = 0.40$. If the steps are independent: $0.95 \times 0.90 \times 0.85 \times 0.90 \times 0.80 = 0.5233$. So the plan works between 40% and (at most) about 80% (the weakest step), and exactly 52% if independent. The bound is a safe floor when you do not know how the steps depend on each other.

</details>

**H3. (Hard)** *Uses [22.8 Correlation vs Causation](22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md) and [22.9](22.9-Simpsons-Paradox.md).* A headline says: "Students who use the library app score 8 marks higher. The university will make all students use it." Suppose half of the students are motivated (base mark 60) and half are not (base mark 50). A motivated student uses the app with probability 0.8, and a not-motivated one with probability 0.2. The app truly adds 2 marks to anyone who uses it. (a) Find the average marks of users and non-users, and the gap. (b) What will the policy really gain? (c) Name a better way to measure the effect.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Users: the share of motivated students among them is $\frac{0.5 \times 0.8}{0.5 \times 0.8 + 0.5 \times 0.2} = 0.8$. Their base average is $0.8 \times 60 + 0.2 \times 50 = 58$, and with the app effect, $60$. Non-users: the share of motivated is $\frac{0.5 \times 0.2}{0.5} = 0.2$, so their average is $0.2 \times 60 + 0.8 \times 50 = 52$. The gap is $60 - 52 = 8$ marks, as in the headline.

(b) Of the 8 marks, only 2 come from the app. The other 6 come from motivation, which is a confounder. Forcing everyone to use the app adds about 2 marks per student, not 8.

(c) Give the app to a random half of the students (a randomized trial), or compare students of the same motivation level. Inside each motivation group, the gap is exactly 2.

</details>

**H4. (Hard)** *Uses [22.14 Kelly Criterion](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md) and [22.15](22.15-Calibration.md). Includes a proof.* A repeated even-money bet ($b = 1$) has a win probability that is either 0.5 or 0.6, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and you do not know which. You must choose one fixed fraction $f$ for all bets. (a) Prove that the average long-run growth rate over the two possibilities equals $g_{\bar{p}}(f)$ with $\bar{p} = 0.55$. (b) Find the best $f$. (c) Compare with betting the Kelly fraction for the optimistic $p = 0.6$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) For a fixed win probability $p$, the growth rate is $g_p(f) = p \ln(1 + f) + (1 - p) \ln(1 - f)$, which is a linear function of $p$. So:

$$\tfrac{1}{2} g_{0.5}(f) + \tfrac{1}{2} g_{0.6}(f) = 0.55 \ln(1 + f) + 0.45 \ln(1 - f) = g_{0.55}(f)$$

(b) The maximum of $g_{0.55}$ is at $f^{\ast} = 0.55 - 0.45 = 0.10$.

(c) The Kelly fraction for $p = 0.6$ is $0.2$. We have $g_{0.55}(0.2) = 0.55 \ln 1.2 + 0.45 \ln 0.8 = 0.10028 - 0.10041 = -0.00014$, which is zero growth on average, while $g_{0.55}(0.1) = +0.00501$. So when you are unsure about the edge, bet the Kelly size for your **average** belief, not for the optimistic one. (In real life you also learn about $p$ as the bets go on, which would let you adjust $f$.)

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. Project: how long must a journal be?** *Uses [22.15 Calibration](22.15-Calibration.md) and [22.7 Law of Small Numbers](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md).* An ideal forecaster knows the true chance $q$ of each event and reports it honestly. An overconfident forecaster reports $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-2 \ln(q / (1 - q))}}$, which doubles the log-odds. Each true chance is drawn from $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$. For journals of 20, 50, 100, 400 and 1,600 predictions, find the average Brier score of both forecasters, and the share of journals in which the overconfident forecaster scores **better** (a lower Brier score) than the honest one.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_journals = 20_000
sigmoid = lambda t: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t))

print("predictions  honest Brier  overconfident Brier  P(overconfident wins)")
for n in [20, 50, 100, 400, 1600]:
    q = rng.beta(2, 2, size=(n_journals, n))                  # the true chance of each event
    happened = rng.random((n_journals, n)) < q
    bold = sigmoid(2 * np.log(q / (1 - q)))                   # doubles the log-odds: overconfident
    brier_honest = ((q - happened) ** 2).mean(axis=1)         # honest forecaster reports q itself
    brier_bold = ((bold - happened) ** 2).mean(axis=1)
    print(f"{n:>11}  {brier_honest.mean():>12.4f}  {brier_bold.mean():>19.4f}  "
          f"{np.mean(brier_bold < brier_honest):>21.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
predictions  honest Brier  overconfident Brier  P(overconfident wins)
         20        0.1995               0.2118                  0.302
         50        0.1999               0.2122                  0.187
        100        0.2001               0.2126                  0.100
        400        0.1999               0.2122                  0.006
       1600        0.1999               0.2123                  0.000
```

On average the honest forecaster is better by about 0.0124 (the exact values are $0.2$ and $0.2124$). But in a journal of 20 predictions, the overconfident forecaster looks better 30% of the time. Only at 400 predictions is the ranking almost always right. This is why small journals and short track records (a fund, a pundit, your own record) cannot be trusted.

</details>

**S2. Project: risks with a shared cause.** *Uses [22.12 Compounding Small Risks](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md) and [22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md).* You have 20 accounts. In model A, each is breached each year with probability 0.03, independently. In model B, one year in ten is an "attack wave" in which each account is breached with probability 0.20, and in the other years each is breached with probability $\frac{0.01}{0.9}$ (so that the average is again 0.03). Simulate 2 million years of each model and find the average number of breaches, the chance of at least one breach, and the chance of 5 or more. Compare with the exact values.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_years, n_sites = 2_000_000, 20

# Model A: every site is breached with chance 0.03 each year, independently.
indep = rng.binomial(n_sites, 0.03, size=n_years)

# Model B: same 0.03 per site on average, but one year in ten is an "attack wave".
# In a wave each site is breached with chance 0.20. In the other years, with chance 0.01/0.9.
wave = rng.random(n_years) < 0.10
p_calm = 0.01 / 0.9
shared = rng.binomial(n_sites, np.where(wave, 0.20, p_calm))

exact_none = 0.1 * 0.8 ** n_sites + 0.9 * (1 - p_calm) ** n_sites
exact_many = 0.1 * binom.sf(4, n_sites, 0.20) + 0.9 * binom.sf(4, n_sites, p_calm)
rows = [("average breaches", indep.mean(), 0.6, shared.mean(), 0.6),
        ("P(at least one)", np.mean(indep > 0), 1 - 0.97 ** n_sites, np.mean(shared > 0), 1 - exact_none),
        ("P(5 or more)", np.mean(indep >= 5), binom.sf(4, n_sites, 0.03), np.mean(shared >= 5), exact_many)]
print(f"{'':<18}{'independent':>22}{'shared cause':>22}")
print(f"{'':<18}{'simulation  exact':>22}{'simulation  exact':>22}")
for name, a, ea, b, eb in rows:
    print(f"{name:<18}{a:>12.5f}{ea:>10.5f}{b:>12.5f}{eb:>10.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
                             independent          shared cause
                       simulation  exact     simulation  exact
average breaches       0.59981   0.60000     0.60143   0.60000
P(at least one)        0.45592   0.45621     0.27944   0.27908
P(5 or more)           0.00025   0.00026     0.03722   0.03704
```

Both models have the same average (0.6 breaches a year). With a shared cause, "at least one breach" becomes **less** likely (28% against 46%), but "five or more at once" becomes about 140 times more likely (3.7% against 0.026%). Sharing a cause moves risk from many small events to a few large ones. This is the same lesson as the two servers of [22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md).

</details>

## Probability Thinking Checklist

Keep this page. Before an important decision, read down the left column and answer the questions that fit.

| Ask yourself | What to do | Topic |
|---|---|---|
| How common is this in general, and among people like me? | Start from the base rate. Count 1,000 people. | [22.1](22.1-Base-Rates.md) |
| How much more likely is this clue if I am right than if I am wrong? | Multiply the odds by that ratio. Never start at 0% or 100%. | [22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md) |
| What do I get on average, after costs? | Chance times size, added up. Find the break-even chance. | [22.3](22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md) |
| How bad can it get, and can I live with it? | Look at the spread and at the chance of the bad result. Do not mix risks that share a cause. | [22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md) |
| Is this reset each time, like a coin? | If yes, "due" does not exist. A very long streak means doubt the model. | [22.5](22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md) |
| Was this result extreme? | Expect the next one to be closer to the average. Use a control group. | [22.6](22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md) |
| Out of how many cases? | The margin of error is about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. Small samples have the extreme results. | [22.7](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md) |
| What are the four stories for this link? | $X$ causes $Y$, $Y$ causes $X$, a hidden $Z$, or chance. Ask about a random experiment. | [22.8](22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md) |
| Do the two options have the same mix of cases? | Compare inside groups that exist before the choice, not groups the choice creates. | [22.9](22.9-Simpsons-Paradox.md) |
| Who is missing from what I see? | Find the failures, the dropouts, the non-answers. | [22.10](22.10-Survivorship-Bias.md) |
| Does the story have many details or steps? | Multiply the chances. More detail means less probable. | [22.11](22.11-Conjunction-Fallacy.md) |
| How many times will I face this risk? | $1 - (1 - p)^n$. Lower $p$ or lower $n$. | [22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md) |
| Could one event be 10 times the biggest I have seen? | If yes, plan for the tail. Do not trust the average. | [22.13](22.13-Fat-Tails.md) |
| How much do I stake each time? | Never everything. Less than the Kelly size, because the edge is a guess. | [22.14](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md) |
| Did I write down my number, and will I check it? | Keep a journal. Score it. Widen your ranges if they miss. | [22.15](22.15-Calibration.md) |

**The 30-second version.** Before you decide, say these five sentences: "How common is it? How strong is the clue? What is the average, and what is the worst case I can live with? How many times will I face it? What am I not seeing?"

> **Note:** These are habits for thinking, not a guarantee. Real situations break the assumptions (independence, a known chance, a stable world). A good user of probability says which assumption is weakest and keeps a margin for it.

- **3Blue1Brown**, for Bayes' theorem and the Central Limit Theorem.
- **Seeing Theory** (Brown University), for interactive visual probability.

**Three habits that keep the knowledge alive:**

1. **Simulate before you trust.** For any new formula or claim, write 10 lines of Python with `numpy` and `scipy.stats`, and compare the simulation with the exact answer, as in every file of this book.
2. **Keep a prediction journal** ([22.15](22.15-Calibration.md)). It is the only exercise that trains your own judgment and gives you a score.
3. **Teach one idea to someone.** Explain base rates, regression to the mean or compounding risk to a friend, with their own numbers. If you can do it without a formula, you own it.
