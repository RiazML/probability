# 22.3 Expected Value Decisions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.3 Expected Value Decisions

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [22.2 Bayesian Updating in Daily Life](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md). You need [6.1 Expected Value](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.1-Expected-Value.md).

> **Big idea:** To compare risky choices, multiply each possible result by its chance and add them up: the choice with the best average result wins in the long run, as long as you can survive the bad days.

---

## Why It Matters

Should you buy a lottery ticket, guess on an exam question, take a job with a bonus, or carry an umbrella? All of these are choices with uncertain results. **Expected value** gives one number for each choice, so you can compare them fairly. [Module 06](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.1-Expected-Value.md) defines it and the [Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md) says why it works. This file uses it as a tool for decisions, and also says plainly when the tool is not enough: that is the job of [22.4 Variance and Risk](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md).

## Intuition

A friend offers a game. You pay 20 taka and roll a die. If it shows 6, you get 60 taka. Otherwise you get nothing. Is it a good game?

On one roll you might win. But imagine playing 600 times. You pay $600 \times 20 = 12{,}000$ taka. You win about 100 times, so you collect about $100 \times 60 = 6{,}000$ taka. You lose about half of your money. On each roll, you lose 10 taka **on average**: you expect $\frac{60}{6} = 10$ taka back for each 20 taka paid.

That "average result per play" is the expected value. The idea for decisions is simple: **ask what you get on average, not what you get in your best dream**. A lottery ticket is sold with the best dream (the jackpot) on the poster and the average (a small fraction of the price) hidden.

## Picture

```text
                         +-- 0.6 --> 70,000 taka   (30,000 + bonus 40,000)
            +-- Offer B -+
            |            +-- 0.4 --> 30,000 taka   (base only)
  Choose ---+
            |
            +-- Offer A ------ 1.0 --> 50,000 taka   (fixed)

   EV(B) = 0.6 x 70,000 + 0.4 x 30,000 = 54,000 taka
   EV(A) = 1.0 x 50,000               = 50,000 taka
```

A decision tree: each branch has a chance and a result. Multiply along each branch and add to get the expected value of each offer (monthly pay, made-up numbers).

## Definition

Suppose an option gives the results $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ with probabilities $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$ (they add to 1). The **expected value** of the option is:

$$
E[V] = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \thinspace v_i
$$

**In words:** multiply each possible result by its chance, then add. It is the long-run average result per try.

The **expected net gain** of a bet subtracts what you pay. For a bet that wins $W$ with chance $p$ and loses $L$ with chance $1 - p$:

$$
E[\text{net}] = p \thinspace W - (1 - p) \thinspace L
$$

The **break-even probability** is the chance $p^{\ast}$ at which the expected net gain is exactly zero:

$$
p^{\ast} = \frac{L}{W + L}
$$

**In words:** a bet is worth taking (on average) only when your chance of winning is above $p^{\ast}$, which is "what I can lose" divided by "what I can win plus what I can lose".

**The decision rule:** choose the option with the largest expected value of the thing you care about, after the costs.

## Why It Is True

**Why the average matters.** If you repeat the same choice many times with independent results, the average result per try gets close to $E[V]$. This is the Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md), [17.4](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)). A rule that picks the larger $E[V]$ each time therefore wins more money, marks or time in the long run.

**Why we can add choices.** The expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, even when the parts are dependent ([6.2 Linearity of Expectation](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)). So for a day with several separate choices, you can judge each choice by itself and add the answers.

**The break-even formula.** Set the expected net gain to zero and solve for $p$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p W - (1 - p) L &= 0 && \text{(break-even)} \\
p W + p L &= L && \text{(move the } L \text{ term)} \\
p &= \frac{L}{W + L} && \text{(divide by } W + L \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For $p \gt p^{\ast}$ the expected net gain is positive, and for $p \lt p^{\ast}$ it is negative, because the expected net gain grows with $p$.

> **Warning:** The rule has limits. It assumes you can repeat the choice many times, that the money (or marks) means the same to you whether you have a lot or a little, and that you know the true chances. In real life none of these is exactly true. This is a simple model to help you think. It is not financial advice.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Lottery Ticket

**Problem.** A ticket costs 100 taka. Made-up prizes: 1 ticket in 5,000,000 wins 100,000,000 taka. 1 ticket in 50,000 wins 10,000 taka. 1 ticket in 100 wins 100 taka. Find the expected prize and the expected net gain per ticket.

**Solution.**

1. Jackpot part: $\frac{100{,}000{,}000}{5{,}000{,}000} = 20$ taka.
2. Middle prize part: $\frac{10{,}000}{50{,}000} = 0.20$ taka.
3. Small prize part: $\frac{100}{100} = 1$ taka.
4. Expected prize: $20 + 0.20 + 1 = 21.20$ taka.
5. Expected net gain: $21.20 - 100 = -78.80$ taka.

**Answer.** On average, a 100-taka ticket returns about 21 taka. You lose about 79 taka per ticket, and 94% of the "expected prize" comes from the jackpot that almost nobody wins.

**Check.** Real lotteries have different numbers, but most are built so that the expected prize is well below the price. The rest pays for costs and profit. The simulation shows how slowly the average settles because the jackpot is so rare. (Playing a game for fun is a fine reason to spend a small amount. Just call it entertainment, not an investment.)

### Example 2: Guess or Skip?

**Problem.** An exam has multiple-choice questions with 4 options. A right answer gives 1 mark. A wrong answer loses a penalty. A skipped question gives 0. Should you guess a question when you know nothing, if the penalty is $\frac{1}{4}$ mark? If it is $\frac{1}{2}$ mark? What if you can remove one option you know is wrong?

**Solution.**

1. With 4 options and a blind guess, $p = \frac{1}{4}$. Penalty $\frac{1}{4}$: $E = \frac{1}{4}(1) - \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = 0.25 - 0.1875 = +0.0625$.
2. Penalty $\frac{1}{2}$: $E = 0.25 - \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0.25 - 0.375 = -0.125$.
3. Break-even from the formula, with $W = 1$ and $L$ equal to the penalty: $p^{\ast} = \frac{L}{1 + L}$. For $L = \frac{1}{4}$, $p^{\ast} = \frac{0.25}{1.25} = 0.2$. For $L = \frac{1}{2}$, $p^{\ast} = \frac{0.5}{1.5} = \frac{1}{3}$.
4. After removing one option, $p = \frac{1}{3}$. With penalty $\frac{1}{4}$: $E = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = 0.3333 - 0.1667 = +0.1667$. With penalty $\frac{1}{2}$: $E = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0$, exactly break-even.

**Answer.** Penalty $\frac{1}{4}$: a blind guess is slightly good ($+0.0625$ marks). Penalty $\frac{1}{2}$: skip a blind guess ($-0.125$). With one option removed and penalty $\frac{1}{2}$, you are exactly at break-even, so guessing does not hurt on average but also does not help.

**Check.** The break-even $p^{\ast}$ is the answer to a good question: "how sure must I be to guess?" For a penalty of $\frac{1}{4}$ you need to be at least 20% sure, and for $\frac{1}{2}$ at least 33% sure. The simulation in Practice problem 6 confirms these averages.

### Example 3: Two Job Offers

**Problem.** Offer A pays 50,000 taka per month. Offer B pays 30,000 taka plus a bonus of 40,000 taka that you receive with probability 0.6 (otherwise 0). Which is better on average? How likely must the bonus be for B to match A?

**Solution.**

1. $E[A] = 50{,}000$.
2. $E[B] = 30{,}000 + 0.6 \times 40{,}000 = 30{,}000 + 24{,}000 = 54{,}000$.
3. Break-even bonus chance: $30{,}000 + 40{,}000 \thinspace p = 50{,}000$, so $p = \frac{20{,}000}{40{,}000} = 0.5$.

**Answer.** B is better by 4,000 taka per month on average. B matches A when the bonus chance is 50%.

**Check.** B gives only 30,000 taka in 40% of the months, which is much less than A. If you need at least 40,000 taka every month to pay the rent, the "better average" may be a bad choice for you. Expected value ignores how bad the bad outcome is. [22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md) fixes this. Also, ask how you know the 0.6: a number from the company is itself uncertain ([22.15](22.15-Calibration.md)).

## Simulate It

One million players each buy 1,000 tickets of the lottery in Example 1. We find the average profit per ticket and how many players end up ahead.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
price = 100
prizes = np.array([100_000_000, 10_000, 100])          # taka
probs = np.array([1 / 5_000_000, 1 / 50_000, 1 / 100])  # chance per ticket, prizes are exclusive
expected_prize = np.sum(prizes * probs)
print(f"Expected prize per ticket: {expected_prize:.2f} taka, expected profit {expected_prize - price:.2f}")

# 1,000,000 players each buy 1,000 tickets. Count how many tickets win each prize.
n_players, n_tickets = 1_000_000, 1_000
wins = rng.multinomial(n_tickets, np.append(probs, 1 - probs.sum()), size=n_players)[:, :3]
profit = wins @ prizes - price * n_tickets

print(f"Simulated average profit per ticket: {profit.mean() / n_tickets:.2f} taka")
print(f"Share of players ahead after 1,000 tickets: {np.mean(profit > 0):.5f}")
print(f"Typical (median) player: {np.median(profit):,.0f} taka")
```

Output:

```text
Expected prize per ticket: 21.20 taka, expected profit -78.80
Simulated average profit per ticket: -78.70 taka
Share of players ahead after 1,000 tickets: 0.00020
Typical (median) player: -99,000 taka
```

The average is close to the exact $-78.80$, but only because a million players together win the jackpot about 200 times. The typical player is much worse than the average: about 99,000 taka lost out of 100,000 spent. Only 2 players in 10,000 end up ahead. The average is pulled up by a few huge wins, which is why "expected value" and "what usually happens" can be very different.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the best case instead of the average.** "I could win 100 million" is true and useless. Multiply it by its chance (here 1 in 5 million) first.
- **Forgetting to subtract the cost.** The expected prize is not the expected gain. Always subtract the price, the time or the effort.
- **Treating one bet like many bets.** The long-run average needs many repeats. A single chance, such as a once-in-life decision, may need more care about the worst result.
- **Counting money as equally valuable at all levels.** Losing 10,000 taka hurts more than gaining 10,000 taka helps when you have little. If the choice could ruin you, do not use only the average ([22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md), [22.14](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md)).
- **Trusting the probabilities too much.** "60%" might really be 40%. Test how the decision changes if the number is a bit lower, and use the break-even $p^{\ast}$ to see how much room you have.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a decision model picks the action with the largest expected reward. This is the core of reinforcement learning ([21.1.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)). Cost-sensitive classifiers move the cut-off to the break-even $p^{\ast}$.
- **Computer science:** a randomized algorithm is judged by its expected running time, and a cache is judged by the expected cost of a miss ([21.3](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/README.md)).
- **Games and finance:** the house edge is a negative expected value ([21.10](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)). Insurance has a negative average for you and is still useful, because it removes a huge loss ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Weather and travel:** carry an umbrella if the chance of rain times the cost of being wet is more than the cost of carrying it ([21.11](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)).
- **Everyday life:** exam guessing, job offers, whether to leave early to avoid traffic, whether a repair is worth its price.
- **Try this today:** for the next small decision with a risk, write the two or three outcomes with a chance and a value for each, and multiply. Find the break-even chance $p^{\ast}$ and ask: "Am I more sure than that?"

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You pay 20 taka to roll a die. You win 60 taka if it shows 6, and nothing otherwise. Find the expected net gain per roll.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The expected prize is $\frac{1}{6} \times 60 = 10$ taka. The net gain is:

$$E[\text{net}] = 10 - 20 = -10 \text{ taka}$$

You lose 10 taka per roll on average.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Carrying an umbrella costs you 10 units of trouble. Getting wet costs you 50 units. At what chance of rain should you carry it? What do you do if the forecast says 30%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Carrying wastes 10 units when it stays dry, and saves 50 units when it rains. Carry it when the expected loss from being wet is more than the cost of carrying:

$$p \times 50 \gt 10 \implies p \gt \frac{10}{50} = 0.2$$

At 30% you carry the umbrella.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A phone repair plan costs 600 taka per year. Made-up numbers: a phone breaks in a year with chance 2%, and the repair costs 20,000 taka. Compare the expected cost with and without the plan. Why might a person still buy the plan?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Without the plan: $0.02 \times 20{,}000 = 400$ taka expected cost. With the plan: 600 taka for sure. The plan costs 200 taka more per year on average.

A person may still buy it because a 20,000-taka bill could be very hard to pay at once, while 600 taka is easy. The plan trades a small sure loss for protection against a big loss. This is the idea of risk in [22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md). A person who could easily pay 20,000 taka from savings does better, on average, without the plan.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A quiz game offers either a sure 450 taka, or a coin flip for 1,000 taka (heads) or nothing (tails). Find the expected value of the flip. What sure amount would make the choice exactly equal on average?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[\text{flip}] = 0.5 \times 1000 + 0.5 \times 0 = 500 \text{ taka}$$

A sure amount of 500 taka would make the two options equal on average. The sure 450 is 50 taka lower than the average of the flip. Many people still take the sure 450 when the money matters a lot to them. That is a reasonable choice, and the gap of 50 taka is the price they pay for certainty.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A bet wins $W$ with probability $p$ and loses $L$ with probability $1 - p$, where $W \gt 0$ and $L \gt 0$. Show that the expected net gain is positive exactly when $p \gt \frac{L}{W + L}$. Then find the break-even $p$ for a bet that pays 2 taka for each 1 taka risked.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The expected net gain is $pW - (1 - p)L = p(W + L) - L$. Since $W + L \gt 0$, this is positive exactly when $p(W + L) \gt L$, that is, when $p \gt \frac{L}{W + L}$.

For a bet that pays 2 taka for each 1 taka risked, $W = 2$ and $L = 1$:

$$p^{\ast} = \frac{1}{2 + 1} = \frac{1}{3}$$

You need to win more than one time in three.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2 by simulation: the average score of a blind guess for (4 options, penalty $\frac{1}{4}$), (4 options, penalty $\frac{1}{2}$), and (3 options, penalty $\frac{1}{4}$).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_guesses = 1_000_000
for n_options, penalty in [(4, 0.25), (4, 0.5), (3, 0.25)]:
    # One guess among n_options choices: +1 mark if right, minus the penalty if wrong.
    is_right = rng.random(n_guesses) < 1 / n_options
    score = np.where(is_right, 1.0, -penalty)
    exact = 1 / n_options - (1 - 1 / n_options) * penalty
    print(f"{n_options} options, penalty {penalty}: simulation {score.mean():+.4f}, exact {exact:+.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
4 options, penalty 0.25: simulation +0.0625, exact +0.0625
4 options, penalty 0.5: simulation -0.1248, exact -0.1250
3 options, penalty 0.25: simulation +0.1670, exact +0.1667
```

</details>

## Summary

- The expected value of a choice is the sum of (chance times result). It is the long-run average per try.
- Compare choices by expected net gain: subtract the cost, and use the same units for every option.
- The break-even chance $p^{\ast} = \frac{L}{W + L}$ tells you how sure you must be before a bet is worth taking.
- The average can be very different from what usually happens, when a few rare results are huge.
- Use expected value when you can repeat the choice and survive the bad days. Otherwise also look at risk ([22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md)).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[V] = \sum_{i} p_i \thinspace v_i}
$$
