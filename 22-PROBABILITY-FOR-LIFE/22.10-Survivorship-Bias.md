# 22.10 Survivorship Bias

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [22.9 Simpson's Paradox](22.9-Simpsons-Paradox.md). You need [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md) and [4.3 Law of Total Probability](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md).

> **Big idea:** If you only see the cases that passed a filter (the winners, the survivors, the ones who answered), what you see is not what the whole group looked like, so always ask what was removed.

---

## Why It Matters

Books about successful founders, lists of the best funds, stories of old people who smoked and lived to 95: we only hear from those who "made it". The ones who failed, closed, or died are not in the story. Any average computed from the visible cases is an average over the **survivors**, not over everyone. This is **survivorship bias**. It is a conditional probability problem: we see $P(\cdot \mid \text{survived})$ and we wrongly read it as $P(\cdot)$. This file shows how large the effect can be, how to correct it with Bayes, and how to find the missing cases.

## Intuition

A well-known story from World War II (told about the statistician Abraham Wald, details vary in the retelling): the army studied the bomber planes that came back from missions. They showed many bullet holes on the wings and the body, and few on the engine. The first idea was to add armor where the holes were. The right idea was the opposite: put armor on the engine.

Why? The planes were hit all over, at random. A plane that was hit in the engine often did not come back, so it was never examined. The engine has few holes on returned planes **because** engine hits are deadly, not because engines are rarely hit. The holes you see show you where a plane can be hit and still fly home.

The same logic works in many places. A fund list with a great average does not show the funds that closed. A group of "perfect" forecasters may contain only the lucky ones. A shop's reviews come only from customers who did not give up and leave.

## Picture

```text
   WHAT HAPPENED                THE FILTER                 WHAT YOU SEE

   1,000 funds              [ closed after a year      178 funds still open
   true average               worse than -10% ]        average return 9.3%
   return: 5.0%       ----------------------------->   "great!"

   The 822 closed funds are not in the database, and they are the ones
   with the worst years.
```

The filter removes the bad cases, so the cases that remain look better than the whole group.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a result we care about, and let $S$ be the event "the case is still visible" (survived, answered, was published). **Survivorship bias** is the difference between the average over the visible cases and the average over all cases:

$$
E[X \mid S] \ne E[X]
$$

**In words:** the average of what you can see is not the average of what happened, whenever being visible depends on the result.

For a yes-or-no category $A$ (for example "the plane was hit in the engine"), Bayes' theorem ([4.4](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)) gives the share you see:

$$
P(A \mid S) = \frac{P(S \mid A) \thinspace P(A)}{P(S)}
$$

**In words:** the share of category $A$ among the survivors equals its true share, multiplied by how likely $A$-cases are to survive, and divided by the overall survival rate. A category that is deadly ($P(S \mid A)$ small) looks rare among survivors.

## Why It Is True

By the law of total probability ([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)), $P(S) = \sum_j P(S \mid A_j) P(A_j)$ for categories $A_1, \dots, A_k$. Then the shares among the survivors are:

$$
P(A_i \mid S) = \frac{P(S \mid A_i) \thinspace P(A_i)}{\sum_j P(S \mid A_j) \thinspace P(A_j)}
$$

When all categories are equally likely, $P(A_i) = \frac{1}{k}$, and the factors $\frac{1}{k}$ cancel:

$$
P(A_i \mid S) = \frac{s_i}{s_1 + \dots + s_k}, \qquad s_i = P(S \mid A_i)
$$

So category $i$ is **under-represented** among survivors exactly when its survival chance $s_i$ is below the average survival chance. The area with the fewest holes is the area where a hit is the most dangerous.

**For numbers.** If $X$ is a normal result with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and a case survives only when $X \gt c$, the average of the survivors is:

$$
E[X \mid X \gt c] = \mu + \sigma \thinspace \frac{\varphi(a)}{1 - \Phi(a)}, \qquad a = \frac{c - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density and $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF ([9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)). **In words:** the survivors' average is the true average plus a positive amount, which is larger when the filter is stricter.

**How to fix it.** There is no trick on the visible data alone. You must find the missing cases (the closed funds, the planes that did not return), or count how many started, or model the filter, as in the Bayes formula above.

> **Note:** All the numbers here are made up to show the idea. The fund model is only a toy (independent yearly returns and a fixed closing rule). It is not investment advice, and it does not describe any real fund.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Fund Database

**Problem.** Each fund has a yearly return that is normal with mean 5% and standard deviation 15%, independent from year to year. A fund closes after any year with a return below -10%. A database lists only funds that are still open after 10 years. What do the listed funds seem to earn per year, and what share of funds is listed?

**Solution.**

1. Standardize the closing limit: $a = \frac{-0.10 - 0.05}{0.15} = -1$. So a fund survives a given year with probability $1 - \Phi(-1) = 0.8413$.
2. Survival for all 10 years: $0.8413^{10} = 0.1777$.
3. A listed fund has had 10 years with return above $-0.10$. By independence, each of its yearly returns has the average:

$$E[R \mid R \gt -0.10] = 0.05 + 0.15 \times \frac{\varphi(-1)}{1 - \Phi(-1)} = 0.05 + 0.15 \times \frac{0.2420}{0.8413} = 0.05 + 0.0431 = 0.0931$$

**Answer.** About 18% of the funds are listed, and they seem to earn 9.3% a year. The true average of the whole group is 5%.

**Check.** The simulation gives $0.1784$ listed and $0.0934$ average. The listed funds are not better managed. They are the ones that did not have a very bad year, and by construction none of them did. The bias is 4.3 points a year, which is almost as big as the true return.

### Example 2: The Perfect Forecaster

**Problem.** 1,024 people each predict the result of a fair coin flip each round, by guessing. A person who is wrong is out. After 10 rounds, how many perfect forecasters do we expect? What is the chance that there is at least one?

**Solution.**

1. A person is right in all 10 rounds with probability $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{10} = \frac{1}{1024}$.
2. The expected number of perfect forecasters: $1024 \times \frac{1}{1024} = 1$.
3. The chance that nobody is perfect: $\left(1 - \frac{1}{1024}\right)^{1024} \approx 0.3677$. So the chance of at least one is $1 - 0.3677 = 0.6323$.

**Answer.** We expect 1 perfect forecaster, and there is at least one with probability 63%.

**Check.** The simulation gives $1.000$ and $0.6317$. A person with 10 correct predictions in a row looks like a genius, but if 1,024 people guess, one such "genius" is typical. Before you follow an expert, ask: **how many people made predictions?** (A stock tip newsletter, a sports tipster, a star fund manager.)

### Example 3: Where to Put the Armor

**Problem.** Each returning bomber had exactly one hit. A hit lands in the engine, body, wings or tail, each with the true chance $\frac{1}{4}$. A plane hit in the engine returns with probability 0.4. A plane hit anywhere else returns with probability 0.9. What share of the holes on returned planes is in the engine? (Made-up numbers.)

**Solution.**

1. The overall return probability: $P(S) = \frac{1}{4}(0.4) + \frac{3}{4}(0.9) = 0.1 + 0.675 = 0.775$.
2. Engine share among returned planes: $P(\text{engine} \mid S) = \frac{0.4 \times 0.25}{0.775} = \frac{0.1}{0.775} = 0.129$.
3. Each other area: $\frac{0.9 \times 0.25}{0.775} = \frac{0.225}{0.775} = 0.290$.

**Answer.** Only 12.9% of the holes on returned planes are in the engine, against 29.0% for each other area. The true share is 25% for every area.

**Check.** The shares add up: $0.129 + 3 \times 0.290 = 1.000$. The area with the fewest visible holes (the engine) is the most dangerous one, so it needs the armor. The simulation agrees.

## Simulate It

The code runs the three examples: the fund database, the 1,024 forecasters, and the bomber planes.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Funds with yearly return N(5%, 15%). A fund closes after any year below -10%, and
#    disappears from the database. Look only at funds still open after 10 years.
returns = rng.normal(0.05, 0.15, size=(200_000, 10))
alive = (returns > -0.10).all(axis=1)
a = (-0.10 - 0.05) / 0.15
print(f"Share of funds still open: simulation {alive.mean():.4f}, exact {norm.sf(a) ** 10:.4f}")
print(f"Average yearly return, all funds:      {returns.mean():.4f}  (true 0.0500)")
print(f"Average yearly return, survivors only: {returns[alive].mean():.4f}  "
      f"(exact {0.05 + 0.15 * norm.pdf(a) / norm.sf(a):.4f})")

# 2. 1,024 forecasters guess a coin flip each round and drop out at their first mistake.
perfect = rng.binomial(1024, 1 / 1024, size=1_000_000)       # who is right 10 times in a row
print(f"Perfect forecasters: average {perfect.mean():.3f}, P(at least one) {np.mean(perfect > 0):.4f} "
      f"(exact {1 - (1 - 1 / 1024) ** 1024:.4f})")

# 3. Planes get one hit in a random area (engine, body, wings, tail). An engine hit is
#    survived 40% of the time, any other hit 90% of the time. We only see returned planes.
area = rng.integers(0, 4, size=2_000_000)
returned = rng.random(2_000_000) < np.where(area == 0, 0.4, 0.9)
share = np.bincount(area[returned], minlength=4) / returned.sum()
print(f"Holes seen on returned planes: engine {share[0]:.3f}, other areas {share[1:].mean():.3f}")
print(f"Exact: engine {0.1 / 0.775:.3f}, other areas {0.225 / 0.775:.3f}   (true: 0.250 each)")
```

Output:

```text
Share of funds still open: simulation 0.1784, exact 0.1777
Average yearly return, all funds:      0.0501  (true 0.0500)
Average yearly return, survivors only: 0.0934  (exact 0.0931)
Perfect forecasters: average 1.000, P(at least one) 0.6317 (exact 0.6323)
Holes seen on returned planes: engine 0.129, other areas 0.290
Exact: engine 0.129, other areas 0.290   (true: 0.250 each)
```

In all three cases, the visible group looks different from the real group, with no change in the real group.

## Common Mistakes

- **Learning only from winners.** "All successful people did X" says nothing, unless many who did X failed too. Ask: "How many people did X and are not in the story?"
- **Believing the visible average.** An average over survivors is too high when survival depends on the result (funds, startups, athletes) and too low in cases where the filter removes the high ones.
- **Thinking that more survivors fix the bias.** A bigger sample of survivors has the same bias. You need the missing cases ([22.7](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md) says more data removes noise, not bias).
- **Not asking who is missing.** Online reviews, survey answers and exit interviews only include the people who stayed and chose to answer. Think about who left without a word.
- **Taking a lucky streak for skill.** Among enough people, some will have a perfect record by luck (Example 2, and [22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a loan model trained only on customers who were approved never sees how the rejected ones would have repaid. This is selection bias, and it makes the model's estimates too optimistic. Recommender systems have the same problem: they see only the items they showed ([21.1.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** a team that studies only the bugs that users report misses the bugs that made users leave. Benchmarks that publish only the runs that finished hide the runs that crashed.
- **Finance:** databases of funds and stocks drop the ones that closed, so past averages look better than they were. This is a simple model, not investment advice ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **History and design:** old buildings seem to be built better, because the badly built ones fell down long ago.
- **Everyday life:** "My grandfather smoked every day and lived to 95." The people who did not live that long cannot tell their story. Success stories, rich-founder books, and "this phone never broke for me".
- **Try this today:** when you see a list of winners, write one question: "How many started, and where are the others?" Look for the group that is missing, and think about what the average would be with them.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A magazine interviews 10 successful founders. All of them took big risks. Why is this not enough to say that big risks lead to success?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The magazine saw only the successes. Many founders who also took big risks may have failed and were never interviewed. To judge the effect of risk, we need the share of risk-takers who succeed, and the share of cautious founders who succeed, among **all** who started. The 10 interviews tell us neither.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** 2,048 forecasters guess coin flips. A wrong guess means they are out. How many are expected to be right 11 rounds in a row?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each has chance $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{11} = \frac{1}{2048}$. The expected number is $2048 \times \frac{1}{2048} = 1$. One "perfect" forecaster is normal, with no skill at all.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A plane gets one hit, equally likely in area X, area Y or area Z. After a hit in X, the plane returns with probability 0.5. After a hit in Y or Z, it returns with probability 0.9. Among returned planes, what share of the holes is in each area? Where should the armor go?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the formula with equal prior chances, the shares are proportional to the return probabilities $0.5, 0.9, 0.9$, whose sum is $2.3$:

$$P(X \mid S) = \frac{0.5}{2.3} \approx 0.217, \qquad P(Y \mid S) = P(Z \mid S) = \frac{0.9}{2.3} \approx 0.391$$

Area X has the fewest holes among returned planes (21.7% against 33.3% true), so it is the most dangerous, and the armor should go there.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A fund closes after any year with a negative return. Yearly returns are normal with mean 8% and standard deviation 10%. Find the average yearly return of the funds that survive a given year. (Use $\varphi(-0.8) = 0.2897$ and $1 - \Phi(-0.8) = 0.7881$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $a = \frac{0 - 0.08}{0.10} = -0.8$.

$$E[R \mid R \gt 0] = 0.08 + 0.10 \times \frac{0.2897}{0.7881} = 0.08 + 0.0368 = 0.1168$$

The survivors seem to earn 11.7% instead of 8%. About 79% of the funds survive each year.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** There are $k$ equally likely categories, with survival chances $s_1, \dots, s_k$. Prove that $P(A_i \mid S) \lt \frac{1}{k}$ exactly when $s_i$ is smaller than the average $\bar{s}$ of the survival chances.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From the formula, $P(A_i \mid S) = \frac{s_i}{s_1 + \dots + s_k} = \frac{s_i}{k \bar{s}}$, because the sum equals $k \bar{s}$. Then:

$$\frac{s_i}{k \bar{s}} \lt \frac{1}{k} \iff s_i \lt \bar{s}$$

(we multiplied by $k \bar{s} \gt 0$). So a category that is less likely to survive than average is under-represented among survivors, and one that is more likely to survive is over-represented. The shares among survivors always add to 1, so some categories must be over-represented as soon as others are under-represented.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000
survive = np.array([0.5, 0.9, 0.9])                  # survival chance after a hit in area X, Y, Z
area = rng.integers(0, 3, size=n)                    # each area is hit equally often
returned = rng.random(n) < survive[area]
share = np.bincount(area[returned], minlength=3) / returned.sum()
exact = survive / survive.sum()
for name, s, e in zip("XYZ", share, exact):
    print(f"Area {name}: holes on returned planes {s:.4f}, exact {e:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Area X: holes on returned planes 0.2176, exact 0.2174
Area Y: holes on returned planes 0.3920, exact 0.3913
Area Z: holes on returned planes 0.3904, exact 0.3913
```

</details>

## Summary

- We only see the cases that passed a filter, so visible averages are averages over survivors: $E[X \mid S]$, not $E[X]$.
- Among survivors, a deadly category looks rare: $P(A \mid S) \propto P(S \mid A) P(A)$.
- A strict filter creates a big bias even when nothing real differs. Survivors of a coin-guessing game look like experts.
- More survivors do not fix it. You need the missing cases, or the number who started.
- Ask every time: "Who is missing, and why?"

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A \mid S) = \frac{P(S \mid A) \thinspace P(A)}{P(S)}}
$$
