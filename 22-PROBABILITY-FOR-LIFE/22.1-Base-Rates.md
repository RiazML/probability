# 22.1 Base Rates

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.1 Base Rates

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 21: Applications of Probability](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/README.md). You need [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md).

> **Big idea:** Before you look at any evidence, ask "how common is this in general?", because a clue that sounds strong can still point to a very unlikely answer when the thing you look for is rare.

---

## Why It Matters

Many bad decisions start with a clue that feels strong. A test is "95% accurate". A stranger "looks like a thief". A job candidate "has the perfect resume". The clue is real, but we forget to ask how common the answer is before the clue. That missing number is the **base rate**. This file turns [Bayes' theorem](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md) into one daily habit. The proofs are in Module 04. Here we only use them. You meet the same idea again in [22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md), where we update the base rate step by step.

## Intuition

Rina takes a rapid test for an infection. The test comes back positive. She panics. Then her doctor friend says: "Let us count people, not percentages."

Take 10,000 people from Rina's city. Suppose 1% of them carry the infection. That is 100 people. The test catches 90% of the sick, so 90 of them test positive. The other 9,900 people are healthy, but the test wrongly flags 5% of them, so 495 healthy people also test positive.

Now count all positives: 90 + 495 = 585. Only 90 of these 585 people are really sick. That is about 15%, not 95%. Rina is not "probably sick". She is "worth a second test".

The test did not get worse. The **healthy group is just much bigger**, so even a small error rate on healthy people creates a lot of false alarms. (All numbers in this file are made up to show the idea. They are not the numbers of any real test.)

## Picture

```text
                           10,000 people
                                 |
              +------------------+------------------+
              |                                     |
        100 sick (1%)                         9,900 healthy (99%)
              |                                     |
        +-----+------+                       +------+-------+
        |            |                       |              |
   90 test +    10 test -               495 test +    9,405 test -
        |                                     |
        +----------------+--------------------+
                         |
                585 positives in total
                only 90 of them are sick: 90 / 585 = 15.4%
```

The 10,000 people split first by the base rate (sick or healthy), and only then by the test result. The false alarms (495) come from the big healthy group.

## Definition

The **base rate** of an event is how often it happens in a general group, before we know anything special about the person or case in front of us. We write it $\pi = P(H)$, where $H$ is the thing we look for (for example "this person is sick").

Call the test result "positive" and write $+$. Let $s = P(+ \mid H)$ be the **sensitivity** (the chance that the test catches a real case). Let $f = P(+ \mid H^c)$ be the **false-positive rate** (the chance that the test flags a person who does not have it). Then the chance that a person with a positive result really has $H$ is:

$$
P(H \mid +) = \frac{\pi \thinspace s}{\pi \thinspace s + (1 - \pi) \thinspace f}
$$

**In words:** the share of true positives among all positives, where the true positives are "base rate times sensitivity" and the false positives are "(1 minus base rate) times false-positive rate".

This is exactly [Bayes' theorem](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md) with the law of total probability in the bottom. The number $P(H \mid +)$ is called the **positive predictive value** (PPV).

The **natural frequency** method is the same formula, written as counting. Take 10,000 people (or 1,000, or 100,000), count how many fall in each box, and divide.

## Why It Is True

Start from the definition of conditional probability ([4.1](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.1-Conditional-Probability.md)) and split the positives into two groups, the sick and the healthy ([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H \mid +) &= \frac{P(H \cap +)}{P(+)} && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \frac{P(+ \mid H) \thinspace P(H)}{P(+ \mid H) \thinspace P(H) + P(+ \mid H^c) \thinspace P(H^c)} && \text{(multiplication rule, total probability)} \\
&= \frac{s \thinspace \pi}{s \thinspace \pi + f \thinspace (1 - \pi)} && \text{(rename the symbols)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Why does the base rate matter so much? Divide the top and the bottom by $\pi s$:

$$
P(H \mid +) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi} \cdot \frac{f}{s}}
$$

**In words:** the answer depends on two ratios: how many healthy people there are for each sick person, $\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}$, and how many false alarms there are for each true catch, $\frac{f}{s}$. When the first ratio is huge (a rare condition), even a small second ratio gives many false alarms.

This also shows that, for one fixed test, $P(H \mid +)$ **grows when $\pi$ grows**, because $\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}$ shrinks. The same test is weak evidence in a low-risk group and strong evidence in a high-risk group.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Rapid Test

**Problem.** The base rate is $\pi = 0.01$, the sensitivity is $s = 0.90$ and the false-positive rate is $f = 0.05$. Rina tests positive. What is the chance she is sick? And if she tests negative, what is the chance she is healthy?

**Solution.**

1. True positives: $\pi s = 0.01 \times 0.90 = 0.009$.
2. False positives: $(1 - \pi) f = 0.99 \times 0.05 = 0.0495$.
3. Add them to get all positives: $0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585$.
4. Divide: $P(H \mid +) = \frac{0.009}{0.0585} \approx 0.1538$.
5. For the negative test: healthy and negative is $0.99 \times 0.95 = 0.9405$. Sick and negative is $0.01 \times 0.10 = 0.001$. So $P(H^c \mid -) = \frac{0.9405}{0.9405 + 0.001} = \frac{0.9405}{0.9415} \approx 0.9989$.

**Answer.** After a positive test, the chance of being sick is about 15%. After a negative test, the chance of being healthy is about 99.9%.

**Check.** In the 10,000-person picture: 90 sick among 585 positives is $0.1538$, and 9,405 healthy among 9,415 negatives is $0.9989$. The simulation below gives the same numbers.

### Example 2: A Fraud Alert

**Problem.** A bank checks card payments. Made-up numbers: 1 payment in 2,000 is fraud ($\pi = 0.0005$). The detector flags 98% of fraud payments and wrongly flags 1% of good payments. A payment gets flagged. How likely is it to be fraud?

**Solution.**

1. True alerts: $0.0005 \times 0.98 = 0.00049$.
2. False alerts: $0.9995 \times 0.01 = 0.009995$.
3. Total alerts: $0.00049 + 0.009995 = 0.010485$.
4. $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{alert}) = \frac{0.00049}{0.010485} \approx 0.0467$.

**Answer.** About 4.7%. So about 19 out of every 20 alerts are false alarms.

**Check.** Count 1,000,000 payments: 500 are fraud (490 flagged), 999,500 are good (9,995 flagged). So 490 out of 10,485 alerts are real, which is 4.67%. This does not mean the detector is useless: the share of fraud among flagged payments went from 0.05% to 4.7%, almost 100 times higher. It means the bank should send a cheap message ("Was this you?") and not block the card.

### Example 3: The Same Positive, Two People

**Problem.** Use the test of Example 1. Aman has no symptoms and was tested as part of a random check. Bela has a fever and was in contact with a sick person, so doctors think her base rate is 30%. Both test positive. Compare them.

**Solution.**

1. Aman: $\pi = 0.01$, so $P(H \mid +) \approx 0.154$ (Example 1).
2. Bela: true positives $0.30 \times 0.90 = 0.27$. False positives $0.70 \times 0.05 = 0.035$. Total $0.305$.
3. $P(H \mid +) = \frac{0.27}{0.305} \approx 0.885$.

**Answer.** The same positive result means about 15% for Aman and about 89% for Bela.

**Check.** This is the result "the PPV grows when $\pi$ grows" of the proof. The right base rate is the one for people **like you** (same symptoms, same exposure), not the one for the whole country. Choosing that group is a judgment call, so a different reasonable group may give a different base rate.

## Simulate It

This code runs the same test on groups with four different base rates, and finds the share of sick people among those who test positive.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 2_000_000
sensitivity, false_positive = 0.90, 0.05   # P(+ | sick), P(+ | healthy)

# The same test, used on groups with different base rates.
for base_rate in [0.001, 0.01, 0.10, 0.50]:
    is_sick = rng.random(n_people) < base_rate
    p_positive = np.where(is_sick, sensitivity, false_positive)
    is_positive = rng.random(n_people) < p_positive
    estimate = is_sick[is_positive].mean()            # P(sick | positive)
    exact = base_rate * sensitivity / (base_rate * sensitivity + (1 - base_rate) * false_positive)
    print(f"base rate {base_rate:5.3f}: simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
base rate 0.001: simulation 0.0176, exact 0.0177
base rate 0.010: simulation 0.1520, exact 0.1538
base rate 0.100: simulation 0.6664, exact 0.6667
base rate 0.500: simulation 0.9473, exact 0.9474
```

The test never changes, but the meaning of a positive result goes from under 2% to almost 95%. Only the base rate moved.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading "95% accurate" as "95% sure I am sick".** Accuracy numbers describe $P(+ \mid H)$ and $P(- \mid H^c)$. What you want is $P(H \mid +)$, which is a different number ([4.11.2](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.2-Medical-Testing.md)). Always ask: "out of 1,000 people with a positive result, how many are really sick?"
- **Using the wrong group for the base rate.** The rate for the whole country, for your age group, and for people with your symptoms can be very different. Pick the group that matches you, and say which one you picked.
- **Throwing away the clue.** Base rates are a starting point, not the end. A strong clue can beat a small base rate, as Bela's case shows. Do not say "it is rare, so it cannot be me".
- **Using old base rates.** Rates change with time and place. A rate from another country or from ten years ago can mislead you.
- **Forgetting that the errors may be linked.** The formula assumes the test errors for one person are independent of who the person is. If the test fails more often for people with some trait, the numbers change. This is a simple model, not a replacement for a doctor.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a classifier trained on data with 0.1% positives and judged only by "accuracy" can look excellent by always saying "negative". Precision (the PPV) depends on the base rate in the real data ([21.1.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md)).
- **Computer science and security:** intrusion alarms, spam filters and fraud detectors have the same arithmetic. A rare attack means most alarms are false, so alerts are ranked and checked in steps ([21.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md)).
- **Medicine:** screening and diagnostic tests are read with the base rate of the group tested ([21.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)). This file is not medical advice. Ask a doctor about your own result.
- **Law:** a rare match in evidence does not mean guilt unless the base rate of "innocent but matching" is counted ([4.11.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.3-Prosecutors-Fallacy.md)).
- **Everyday life:** "He is quiet and loves books, so he must be a librarian": there are many more farmers than librarians, so even a good fit may point to the farmer.
- **Try this today:** the next time you read "a test is X% accurate" or "this app detects Y", write the two numbers "how many people like me have it?" and "how many of the people with a positive result would really have it?" Use 1,000 people and count.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A condition affects 2% of people. A test finds 90% of the sick and wrongly flags 10% of the healthy. Out of 1,000 people, how many test positive, and how many of those are sick? (Made-up numbers.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Sick: $1000 \times 0.02 = 20$, and $20 \times 0.90 = 18$ of them test positive. Healthy: $980$, and $980 \times 0.10 = 98$ of them test positive. Total positives: $18 + 98 = 116$.

$$P(H \mid +) = \frac{18}{116} \approx 0.155$$

Only about 15.5% of the positives are sick.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Of all your emails, 80% are spam. A filter marks 95% of spam as spam, and wrongly marks 2% of good emails as spam. An email is marked as spam. How likely is it really spam?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Spam and marked: $0.80 \times 0.95 = 0.76$. Good and marked: $0.20 \times 0.02 = 0.004$.

$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{marked}) = \frac{0.76}{0.76 + 0.004} = \frac{0.76}{0.764} \approx 0.9948$$

Here the base rate is high, so a "spam" mark is reliable. Same filter, different base rate, different meaning.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In a town there are 20 farmers for every librarian. Suppose 40% of librarians and 10% of farmers fit the description "quiet and loves books". Meet someone with this description. What is the chance this person is a librarian? (Made-up numbers.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take 1 librarian and 20 farmers. Librarians who fit: $1 \times 0.40 = 0.4$. Farmers who fit: $20 \times 0.10 = 2$.

$$P(\text{librarian} \mid \text{fit}) = \frac{0.4}{0.4 + 2} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$$

The description is a good fit for a librarian, but there are so many more farmers that the person is probably a farmer.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Use the test of Example 1 ($\pi = 0.01$, $s = 0.90$, $f = 0.05$). Aman tests negative. Find the chance he is actually sick.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Sick and negative: $0.01 \times 0.10 = 0.001$. Healthy and negative: $0.99 \times 0.95 = 0.9405$.

$$P(H \mid -) = \frac{0.001}{0.001 + 0.9405} = \frac{0.001}{0.9415} \approx 0.00106$$

About 1 in 940. A negative result is very reassuring here because the condition is rare.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for a fixed test ($s$ and $f$ fixed, $s \gt 0$, $f \gt 0$), the PPV $P(H \mid +)$ is an increasing function of the base rate $\pi$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From "Why It Is True":

$$P(H \mid +) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi} \cdot \frac{f}{s}}$$

The ratio $\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi} - 1$ decreases when $\pi$ increases, for $0 \lt \pi \le 1$. The factor $\frac{f}{s}$ is a fixed positive number. So the denominator decreases when $\pi$ increases, and the fraction $\frac{1}{\text{denominator}}$ increases. At $\pi = 1$ the PPV is $1$, and when $\pi \to 0$ the PPV goes to $0$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2 by simulation: 4 million payments, fraud probability $0.0005$, detector flags 98% of fraud and 1% of good payments.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 4_000_000
is_fraud = rng.random(n) < 0.0005                    # 1 in 2,000 transactions
p_alert = np.where(is_fraud, 0.98, 0.01)             # detector catches 98%, flags 1% of good ones
is_alert = rng.random(n) < p_alert
print(f"Simulation: P(fraud | alert) = {is_fraud[is_alert].mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {0.0005 * 0.98 / (0.0005 * 0.98 + 0.9995 * 0.01):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: P(fraud | alert) = 0.0463
Exact:      0.0467
```

</details>

## Summary

- The **base rate** is how common something is before you see the clue. Ask for it first.
- A positive result is a mix of true positives (base rate times sensitivity) and false positives ((1 minus base rate) times false-positive rate).
- For a rare condition, most positives can be false alarms even when the test is "95% accurate".
- The same test means different things for different groups, so use the base rate of people like you.
- Counting 1,000 people is the fastest way to avoid mistakes.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(H \mid +) = \frac{\pi \thinspace s}{\pi \thinspace s + (1 - \pi) \thinspace f}}
$$
