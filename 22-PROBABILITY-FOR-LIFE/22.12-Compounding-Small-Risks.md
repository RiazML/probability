# 22.12 Compounding Small Risks

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.12 Compounding Small Risks

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [22.11 Conjunction Fallacy](22.11-Conjunction-Fallacy.md). You need [3.6 Union Bound](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md) and [4.7 Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md).

> **Big idea:** A small risk that you take again and again is not small any more: the chance that it happens at least once in $n$ tries is $1 - (1 - p)^n$, which gets close to 1.

---

## Why It Matters

"Only a 1% chance." That sounds safe, until you meet it every day. A tiny risk per use becomes a big risk over a year, a career or a lifetime. This is why a weak password reused on many sites, a small chance of a data-center failure each night, a shortcut that is "almost always fine" and a lottery played for decades all behave very differently from a single try. [Module 03](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md) gives the union bound and [Module 04](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md) gives independence. Here we turn them into one habit: count how many times you will meet the risk.

## Intuition

Rina takes a shortcut across a busy road on her way to class. Each time, there is a 1% chance (made-up number) of a close call that spoils her day. One crossing is safe enough. But she does it every school day.

The chance of **no** close call in one crossing is $0.99$. For two crossings in a row it is $0.99 \times 0.99$. For 365 crossings it is $0.99^{365} = 0.0255$. So the chance of **at least one** close call in a year is $1 - 0.0255 = 0.9745$: more than 97%.

Notice the trick: do not add up the "at least once" chances. First compute the chance that nothing happens in all tries, which is easy to multiply, and then take 1 minus it. Also notice the speed. For a 1% risk, you reach a 50% chance after only 69 tries. Time works against you, because each try has the same small chance, and the tries add up.

## Picture

```text
   tries n     chance of at least one bad event, if each try has p = 1%

       1         1.0%  |#
      10         9.6%  |#####
      30        26.0%  |#############
      69        50.0%  |#########################
     100        63.4%  |################################
     365        97.4%  |#################################################
                       0%                                            100%
```

Each bar is $1 - 0.99^n$. The chance rises almost in a straight line at first (about $np$), then it bends and goes toward 100%.

## Definition

Suppose a bad event happens with probability $p$ in each try, and the tries are **independent** ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)). The chance that it happens **at least once** in $n$ tries is:

$$
P(\text{at least once}) = 1 - (1 - p)^n
$$

**In words:** one minus the chance that all $n$ tries are safe.

The number of tries needed to reach a chance $q$ (for example $q = 0.5$) is the smallest $n$ with $1 - (1 - p)^n \ge q$:

$$
n \ge \frac{\ln(1 - q)}{\ln(1 - p)} \qquad \text{and for } q = \tfrac{1}{2}, \quad n \approx \frac{0.693}{p}
$$

**In words:** to have an even chance of at least one event, you need about $\frac{0.7}{p}$ tries: 70 tries for a 1% risk, 700 tries for a 0.1% risk.

Two quick bounds, valid for all $p$ and $n$, say how close the formula is to simple guesses:

$$
1 - e^{-np} \le 1 - (1 - p)^n \le \min(1, \thinspace np)
$$

**In words:** the chance is at most the expected number of events, $np$ (the union bound), and at least $1 - e^{-np}$. For small $np$ they are almost the same.

## Why It Is True

**The formula.** The complement rule ([3.3](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.3-Consequences-of-the-Axioms.md)) gives $P(\text{at least once}) = 1 - P(\text{never})$. "Never" means that all $n$ tries are safe. By independence, the multiplication rule gives $P(\text{never}) = (1 - p)^n$.

**The upper bound.** Let $A_i$ be the event "try $i$ goes bad". By the union bound ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)):

$$
P(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) = np
$$

This needs **no** independence. Of course, a probability is also at most 1.

**The lower bound.** For all $x$, $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ (the line $1 - x$ is a tangent of the curve $e^{-x}$ at $x = 0$, and the curve lies above it). Put $x = p$ and raise both sides, which are non-negative, to the power $n$:

$$
(1 - p)^n \le e^{-np} \implies 1 - (1 - p)^n \ge 1 - e^{-np}
$$

**Time to an even chance.** Solve $(1 - p)^n \le 1 - q$ by taking logarithms. Since $\ln(1 - p) \lt 0$, the inequality flips: $n \ge \frac{\ln(1 - q)}{\ln(1 - p)}$. For $q = \frac{1}{2}$ the numerator is $-\ln 2 = -0.693$, and $\ln(1 - p) \approx -p$ for small $p$, so $n \approx \frac{0.693}{p}$.

**Dependence.** The formula needs independent tries. If the tries share a cause (one power cut breaks both servers), the chance of "at least one" can be **lower** than the formula, but the chance of "all at once" is **higher**. Example 3 gives numbers.

> **Note:** Risks that are not independent, not constant over time, or not known exactly need more care. The numbers in this file are made up to show the idea. They are not safety or financial advice.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Daily 1% Risk

**Problem.** A bad event has chance $p = 0.01$ each day, independently. Find the chance of at least one event in 30 days and in 365 days. How many days until the chance is 50%? And what is the expected number of bad days in a year?

**Solution.**

1. 30 days: $0.99^{30} = 0.7397$, so $P = 1 - 0.7397 = 0.2603$.
2. 365 days: $0.99^{365} = 0.0255$, so $P = 1 - 0.0255 = 0.9745$.
3. For 50%: $n \ge \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln 0.99} = \frac{-0.6931}{-0.01005} = 68.97$, so 69 days. (Check with the shortcut: $\frac{0.693}{0.01} = 69.3$.)
4. The expected number of bad days in a year is $np = 365 \times 0.01 = 3.65$.

**Answer.** 26% in a month, 97.4% in a year, and 69 days to reach an even chance. We expect 3.65 bad days a year.

**Check.** The upper bound $np$ for 30 days is $0.30$, above the true $0.26$. For 365 days, $np = 3.65$ is not a probability at all (more than 1), so the bound is useless there, and the exact formula must be used. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Passwords on Many Sites

**Problem.** You use the same password on 20 sites. Made-up numbers: each site is breached in a given year with probability 0.03, independently. Once the password leaks from one site, the others are at risk too. What is the chance that at least one site is breached in one year? In five years?

**Solution.**

1. One year, 20 site-years: $0.97^{20} = 0.5438$, so $P = 1 - 0.5438 = 0.4562$.
2. Five years, 100 site-years: $0.97^{100} = 0.0476$, so $P = 1 - 0.0476 = 0.9524$.

**Answer.** About 46% in one year, and about 95% in five years.

**Check.** Each single site looks safe (3%). The number of places where the password can leak is what makes the risk large. Using a different password for each site cuts the damage of one breach to one account: the chance of a breach is the same, but its **effect** is small. A password manager makes this easy.

### Example 3: Two Servers

**Problem.** Two servers each fail on a given night with probability 0.1. Compare "at least one fails" and "both fail" when (a) the failures are independent and (b) both fail together because of one shared cause (for example, one power line).

**Solution.**

1. (a) Independent: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - 0.9^2 = 0.19$ and $P(\text{both}) = 0.1^2 = 0.01$.
2. (b) Shared cause: the two servers fail together or not at all, so $P(\text{at least one}) = P(\text{both}) = 0.1$.

**Answer.** Independent: 19% and 1%. Shared cause: 10% and 10%.

**Check.** Dependence makes "at least one" less likely (0.10 against 0.19), but makes "everything fails" ten times more likely (0.10 against 0.01). Two servers in the same place do not give the safety of two independent servers. The same is true of putting all your savings with one company, or all your plans on one bus route.

## Simulate It

The code checks the formula for the daily 1% risk, the time to the first event, the password example, and the two servers.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_sims = 1_000_000

def at_least_once(p, n):
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** n

# 1. A 1% risk every day, for 1 day, 30 days, 1 year.
for days in [1, 30, 365]:
    hits = rng.binomial(days, 0.01, size=n_sims)             # number of bad days
    print(f"{days:>3} days at 1%: simulation {np.mean(hits > 0):.4f}, exact {at_least_once(0.01, days):.4f}")

# 2. Waiting time until the first bad day: median is about 0.693 / p.
wait = rng.geometric(0.01, size=n_sims)
print(f"Median wait for the first bad day: {np.median(wait):.0f} days (exact 69)")

# 3. 20 accounts, each breached with chance 0.03 per year. One year, then five years.
for years in [1, 5]:
    breaches = rng.binomial(20 * years, 0.03, size=n_sims)
    print(f"{years} year(s): P(at least one breach) {np.mean(breaches > 0):.4f}, "
          f"exact {at_least_once(0.03, 20 * years):.4f}")

# 4. Two servers that each fail with chance 0.1: independent, or one shared cause.
a, b = rng.random(n_sims) < 0.1, rng.random(n_sims) < 0.1
shared = rng.random(n_sims) < 0.1                              # both servers fail together
print(f"Independent: P(any fails) {np.mean(a | b):.4f}, P(both fail) {np.mean(a & b):.4f}")
print(f"Shared cause: P(any fails) {np.mean(shared):.4f}, P(both fail) {np.mean(shared):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
  1 days at 1%: simulation 0.0101, exact 0.0100
 30 days at 1%: simulation 0.2599, exact 0.2603
365 days at 1%: simulation 0.9746, exact 0.9745
Median wait for the first bad day: 69 days (exact 69)
1 year(s): P(at least one breach) 0.4567, exact 0.4562
5 year(s): P(at least one breach) 0.9527, exact 0.9524
Independent: P(any fails) 0.1902, P(both fail) 0.0101
Shared cause: P(any fails) 0.0998, P(both fail) 0.0998
```

The risk per try is the same in every line. Only the number of tries (or the way the risks are linked) changes the answer.

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding probabilities that cannot be added.** For 365 days of 1%, the sum is 365%, which is not a probability. The union bound $np$ is only an upper bound, and it is useful only when $np$ is small.
- **Judging a risk per try instead of per life.** A risk of 0.1% per trip sounds nothing. Over 500 trips it is 39%. Always ask: "How many times will I be exposed?"
- **Assuming that the tries are independent.** Risks often share a cause (one weak password, one power line, one bad habit). Then "at least one" may be smaller and "all at once" larger than the formula suggests (Example 3).
- **Believing that survival so far makes the next try safer.** After 100 safe days, the chance of a bad event tomorrow is still 1% ([22.5](22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md)). The chance of a bad event within the next 100 days is also still 63%.
- **Confusing the chance of at least one with the number of events.** The expected number of events in a year can be 1 (20 tries at 5%), while the chance of at least one is only 64% (Practice problem 6). Some years have none and some have several.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a model that makes a small error on 1% of the steps of a long task (a code agent, a long chain of reasoning) will make at least one error in most long tasks. This is why reliability per step must be very high ([21.1.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)).
- **Computer science and security:** a hash function with a tiny collision chance per pair still collides for many items, because there are many pairs ([21.3.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.2-Hashing-and-Bloom-Filters.md)). Brute-force attacks and password reuse follow the same arithmetic ([21.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md)).
- **Statistics:** run 20 tests at the 5% level and the chance of at least one false alarm is $1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64$ (64%) ([21.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Engineering and health:** a part with a small failure chance per use fails sooner or later if it is used often ([21.7](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)). This is a model, not a safety rule.
- **Everyday life:** road safety, habits, repeated small bets, using a risky shortcut every day. Money works the same way: a small chance of a big loss, taken many times, ends in ruin ([4.11.5](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md), [22.14](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md)).
- **Try this today:** pick one small risk that you take often. Write $p$ per time and $n$ per year, and compute $1 - (1 - p)^n$. If the answer is high, lower $p$ (a helmet, a backup, a separate password) or lower $n$.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A backup fails with chance $\frac{1}{50}$ each time. You make 10 backups. What is the chance that at least one of them fails?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P = 1 - \left(\frac{49}{50}\right)^{10} = 1 - 0.98^{10} = 1 - 0.8171 = 0.1829$$

About 18%.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** An event has chance 0.1% each day. What is the chance of at least one in 30 days? Compare with the quick guess $np$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P = 1 - 0.999^{30} = 1 - 0.9704 = 0.0296$$

The guess is $np = 30 \times 0.001 = 0.03$. They agree well because $np$ is small.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Each try succeeds with chance $0.02$. How many independent tries do you need for at least a 90% chance of one success?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need $(0.98)^n \le 0.10$:

$$n \ge \frac{\ln 0.10}{\ln 0.98} = \frac{-2.3026}{-0.020203} = 113.97$$

So $n = 114$ tries. (The rough rule for 50% gives $\frac{0.693}{0.02} = 35$ tries. To reach 90% you need about 3.3 times as many.)

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A person has a 5% chance each year of a serious problem (independent across years). Find the chance of at least one problem in 20 years. What yearly chance of a problem would be needed to have only a 10% chance in 20 years?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P = 1 - 0.95^{20} = 1 - 0.3585 = 0.6415$$

For a 10% chance in 20 years we need $(1 - p)^{20} = 0.90$, so $1 - p = 0.90^{1/20} = 0.99475$ and $p = 0.00525$. The yearly risk must be about 0.5%, ten times smaller.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $1 - e^{-np} \le 1 - (1-p)^n \le \min(1, np)$ for $0 \le p \le 1$ and $n \ge 1$. Use the inequality $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ for the left side and induction (or the union bound) for the right side.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Left side: for $0 \le p \le 1$, $0 \le 1 - p \le e^{-p}$. Raising to the power $n$ keeps the inequality: $(1 - p)^n \le e^{-np}$. So $1 - (1 - p)^n \ge 1 - e^{-np}$.

Right side by induction: $(1 - p)^n \ge 1 - np$ is true for $n = 1$ (equality). If it holds for $n$, then

$$(1 - p)^{n+1} = (1 - p)(1 - p)^n \ge (1 - p)(1 - np) = 1 - (n+1)p + np^2 \ge 1 - (n+1)p$$

so it holds for $n + 1$. Therefore $1 - (1 - p)^n \le np$. Also $1 - (1 - p)^n \le 1$ since $(1 - p)^n \ge 0$. (The union bound gives the same result without independence.)

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** A 5% chance of a serious problem each year, over 20 years. Simulate the chance of at least one problem, the average number of problems, and the chance of 2 or more.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 1_000_000

# A 5% chance of a serious problem each year, independent across years, over 20 years.
bad_years = rng.binomial(20, 0.05, size=n_people)
print(f"P(at least one bad year in 20): simulation {np.mean(bad_years > 0):.4f}, "
      f"exact {1 - 0.95 ** 20:.4f}")
print(f"Average number of bad years: {bad_years.mean():.3f} (exact {20 * 0.05:.3f})")
print(f"P(2 or more bad years): simulation {np.mean(bad_years >= 2):.4f}, "
      f"exact {binom.sf(1, 20, 0.05):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text

Average number of bad years: 1.000 (exact 1.000)
P(2 or more bad years): simulation 0.2642, exact 0.2642
```

On average there is 1 bad year in 20, yet 36% of people have none and 26% have two or more.

</details>

## Summary

- A risk repeated $n$ times has the chance $1 - (1 - p)^n$ of happening at least once. Compute "never" first, then take 1 minus it.
- Even chance after about $\frac{0.7}{p}$ tries: 70 tries for 1%, 700 for 0.1%.
- For small $np$, the chance is about $np$. For large $np$ it goes to 1, and the sum $np$ is no longer a probability.
- Risks that share a cause are not independent. Redundancy only helps when the parts fail for different reasons.
- Lower either the risk per try, or the number of tries.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(\text{at least once in } n \text{ tries}) = 1 - (1 - p)^n}
$$
