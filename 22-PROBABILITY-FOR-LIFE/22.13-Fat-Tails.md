# 22.13 Fat Tails

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [22.12 Compounding Small Risks](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md). You need [9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md), which defines the Pareto law and proves its properties. Here we use them to make decisions.

> **Big idea:** For some quantities (wealth, losses, waiting times, followers) one huge event can outweigh all the normal ones, so your "worst case so far" is not the worst case, and the average is a poor guide.

---

## Why It Matters

Heights, exam marks and daily temperatures stay close to their average. Money, deaths in a disaster, the size of a file or the number of followers do not. In these cases a single value can be hundreds of times the typical one, and it can be larger than everything else added together. Planning with the average, or with a normal curve, works well for the first kind of quantity and fails badly for the second. [9.13](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md) explains the mathematics of tails. This file shows three things you can do with it: how likely a "10 times bigger" event is, how fast the worst case grows when you collect more data, and how much of the total comes from the rare big events.

## Intuition

Take 1,000 adults and add up their heights. The tallest person adds a tiny part of the total. Remove them, and nothing changes. Now take 1,000 people and add up their wealth. The richest person may hold a large part of the total, and removing them changes the total a lot.

The first quantity has a **light tail**: the chance of a very big value falls so fast that very big values never matter. The second has a **fat tail** (also called a heavy tail): the chance falls slowly, so very big values keep appearing, and they matter.

Here is the danger in daily life. You collect data for a year. The worst day in your data cost you 10,000 taka. You think "the worst case is about 10,000". For a light-tailed quantity, that is nearly true. For a fat-tailed quantity, it is not: with 10 times more data, the worst case may be several times larger. The worst event is usually still in the future.

## Definition

A quantity $X$ has a **fat tail** (or **power-law tail**) if the chance of exceeding $x$ falls like a power of $x$, and not like an exponential. The simplest model is the **Pareto** law with minimum value $x_m \gt 0$ and **tail index** $\alpha \gt 0$ ([9.13](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)):

$$
P(X \gt x) = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha}, \qquad x \ge x_m
$$

**In words:** each time the threshold $x$ is multiplied by 10, the chance of exceeding it is divided by only $10^{\alpha}$. For $\alpha = 2$, a value 10 times bigger is only 100 times rarer, not millions of times as for a normal curve. A smaller $\alpha$ means a fatter tail.

Three facts about a Pareto quantity matter for decisions:

1. **The mean and the spread may not exist.** The mean is finite only for $\alpha \gt 1$, and the variance only for $\alpha \gt 2$. Then the sample average and the sample standard deviation do not settle down as data grows.
2. **The worst case grows fast.** The median of the largest of $m$ independent values is about $x_m \left( \frac{m}{\ln 2} \right)^{1/\alpha}$.
3. **Rare events carry the total.** The share of the total mean that comes from values above $x$ is $\left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha - 1}$ (for $\alpha \gt 1$).

## Why It Is True

**The mean.** With density $f(x) = \alpha x_m^{\alpha} x^{-\alpha - 1}$ for $x \ge x_m$:

$$
E[X] = \int_{x_m}^{\infty} x \cdot \alpha x_m^{\alpha} x^{-\alpha - 1} \thinspace dx = \alpha x_m^{\alpha} \int_{x_m}^{\infty} x^{-\alpha} \thinspace dx
$$

The integral of $x^{-\alpha}$ to infinity is finite only if $\alpha \gt 1$, and then it equals $\frac{x_m^{1 - \alpha}}{\alpha - 1}$. So $E[X] = \frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1}$ for $\alpha \gt 1$, and the mean is infinite for $\alpha \le 1$. The same argument with $x^2$ in place of $x$ shows the variance needs $\alpha \gt 2$.

**The worst case.** The largest of $m$ independent values is at most $x$ with probability $\left( 1 - P(X \gt x) \right)^m$. The median $x^{\ast}$ solves $\left( 1 - P(X \gt x^{\ast}) \right)^m = \frac{1}{2}$. For large $m$, $\left( 1 - s \right)^m \approx e^{-ms}$, so $P(X \gt x^{\ast}) \approx \frac{\ln 2}{m}$. For a Pareto law, $\left( \frac{x_m}{x^{\ast}} \right)^{\alpha} = \frac{\ln 2}{m}$, which gives:

$$
x^{\ast} = x_m \left( \frac{m}{\ln 2} \right)^{1/\alpha}
$$

For an exponential law with mean 1, $e^{-x^{\ast}} = \frac{\ln 2}{m}$, so $x^{\ast} = \ln \frac{m}{\ln 2}$, which grows only like $\ln m$.

**The share of the rare events.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X \cdot I_{X \gt x}] &= \int_{x}^{\infty} t \cdot \alpha x_m^{\alpha} t^{-\alpha - 1} \thinspace dt = \frac{\alpha x_m^{\alpha} x^{1 - \alpha}}{\alpha - 1} && \text{(same integral, from } x \text{)} \\
\frac{E[X \cdot I_{X \gt x}]}{E[X]} &= \frac{\alpha x_m^{\alpha} x^{1 - \alpha} / (\alpha - 1)}{\alpha x_m / (\alpha - 1)} = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha - 1} && \text{(divide by the mean)}
\end{aligned}
$$

If we look at the top fraction $q$ of the values, the threshold is the point where $\left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha} = q$, so the share is $q^{(\alpha - 1)/\alpha}$. For $\alpha = 1.5$ and $q = 0.001$: $0.001^{1/3} = 0.1$. The top 0.1% of values make 10% of the total mean.

> **Warning:** Real data are not exactly Pareto. This is a model to give you a feeling for the size of the effect, not a way to predict a specific event. The tail index is also hard to estimate, because the data that decide it (the rare big events) are exactly the data you do not have. Treat all the numbers here as made-up illustrations, and not as advice about money or safety.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: How Likely Is "10 Times the Average"?

**Problem.** Two daily quantities both have mean 100 (for example, taka lost per day). One is exponential with mean 100 (light tail). The other is Pareto with $x_m = 50$ and $\alpha = 2$ (fat tail), which has mean $\frac{2 \times 50}{2 - 1} = 100$. Find the chance of a day above 1,000, which is 10 times the mean. A third quantity is normal with mean 100 and standard deviation 15.

**Solution.**

1. Exponential: $P(X \gt 1000) = e^{-1000/100} = e^{-10} = 0.0000454$, about 1 day in 22,000.
2. Pareto: $P(X \gt 1000) = \left( \frac{50}{1000} \right)^2 = 0.05^2 = 0.0025$, 1 day in 400.
3. Normal: the distance from the mean is $\frac{1000 - 100}{15} = 60$ standard deviations. The chance is smaller than $10^{-700}$, which is zero for every practical purpose.
4. One more step for the Pareto: a day above 10,000 has chance $\left( \frac{50}{10000} \right)^2 = 0.000025$, 1 day in 40,000.

**Answer.** Normal: never. Exponential: once in 22,000 days. Pareto: once in 400 days. For the Pareto, a day 100 times the mean is only 100 times rarer than a day 10 times the mean.

**Check.** The simulation gives $0.002500$ and $0.000045$. With a normal model, a "1,000-taka day" is impossible, so you would plan no buffer for it. With the Pareto, you should expect one such day every two years (400 working days), and you should have a plan for it.

### Example 2: The Worst Case So Far

**Problem.** You record the largest loss in each of $m$ days. Find the typical (median) value of the largest loss for $m = 100$ and for $m = 1000$, when a single day is (a) exponential with mean 1, (b) Pareto with $x_m = 1$ and $\alpha = 2$, (c) Pareto with $x_m = 1$ and $\alpha = 1$. How does the worst case change when you have 10 times more data?

**Solution.**

1. (a) $x^{\ast} = \ln \frac{m}{\ln 2}$. For $m = 100$: $\ln 144.27 = 4.97$. For $m = 1000$: $\ln 1442.7 = 7.27$. Ratio: $\frac{7.27}{4.97} = 1.46$.
2. (b) $x^{\ast} = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\ln 2}}$. For $m = 100$: $\sqrt{144.27} = 12.0$. For $m = 1000$: $\sqrt{1442.7} = 38.0$. Ratio: $\sqrt{10} = 3.16$.
3. (c) $x^{\ast} = \frac{m}{\ln 2}$: 144.3 and 1442.7. Ratio: 10.

**Answer.** With 10 times more data, the typical worst loss grows by a factor of 1.5 (exponential), 3.2 ($\alpha = 2$) and 10 ($\alpha = 1$).

**Check.** The simulation gives 5.0 and 7.3, then 12.0 and 38.0, then 144.9 and 1448.5. For a light tail, more data tells you the worst case. For a fat tail, more data keeps showing you worse cases. The right question is not "what was the worst day?" but "how fast does the worst day grow with more days?".

### Example 3: Where Does the Total Come From?

**Problem.** Daily losses are Pareto with $x_m = 1$ (in thousands of taka) and $\alpha = 1.5$. What share of the total expected loss comes from the 0.1% worst days? Compare with an exponential law, where the top 0.1% of days come from the same fraction of the days.

**Solution.**

1. The top 0.1% has the threshold $x$ with $x^{-1.5} = 0.001$, so $x = 0.001^{-1/1.5} = 1000^{2/3} = 100$ (that is, 100,000 taka).
2. Share of the mean: $\left( \frac{1}{100} \right)^{0.5} = 0.1$.
3. Exponential with mean 1: the threshold is $x = \ln 1000 = 6.908$. The part of the mean above $x$ is $E[X \cdot I_{X \gt x}] = (x + 1) e^{-x} = 7.908 \times 0.001 = 0.00791$.

**Answer.** For the Pareto, the worst 0.1% of the days make 10% of the expected total loss. For the exponential, the same 0.1% of days make only 0.8%.

**Check.** The simulation gives $0.097$ for the Pareto and $0.0079$ for the exponential. If you try to understand your losses from the 999 normal days of 1,000, you miss 10% of the cost in the first case and almost nothing in the second. In a fat-tailed world, "typical days" are not where most of the risk is.

## Simulate It

The code checks the exceedance chances of Example 1, the worst-case growth of Example 2, the share of the rare days of Example 3, and the share of the 10 biggest values among 1,000 (the Picture).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def pareto(alpha, size, x_min=1.0):
    return x_min * (1 - rng.random(size)) ** (-1 / alpha)         # inverse-CDF sampling

# 1. Chance of a value above 10 times the mean (mean 100 in both cases).
n = 20_000_000
print(f"Pareto(50, 2): P(X > 1000) simulation {np.mean(pareto(2, n, 50) > 1000):.6f}, exact {0.05 ** 2:.6f}")
print(f"Exp(mean 100): P(X > 1000) simulation {np.mean(rng.exponential(100, n) > 1000):.6f}, exact {np.exp(-10):.6f}")

# 2. Median of the largest of m values ("the worst year so far"), for m = 100 and m = 1000.
for name, draw, exact in [("Exp(1)", lambda s: rng.exponential(1, s), lambda m: np.log(m / np.log(2))),
                          ("Pareto(1, 2)", lambda s: pareto(2, s), lambda m: np.sqrt(m / np.log(2))),
                          ("Pareto(1, 1)", lambda s: pareto(1, s), lambda m: m / np.log(2))]:
    sims = [np.median(draw((20_000, m)).max(axis=1)) for m in (100, 1000)]
    print(f"{name:>12}: simulation {sims[0]:6.1f}, {sims[1]:7.1f}; exact {exact(100):6.1f}, {exact(1000):7.1f}")

# 3. Share of the total that comes from the top 0.1% of 2,000,000 days.
def top_share(x):
    return np.sort(x)[-int(0.001 * len(x)):].sum() / x.sum()
heavy = np.median([top_share(pareto(1.5, 2_000_000)) for _ in range(9)])
print(f"Top 0.1% share, Pareto(1, 1.5): {heavy:.3f} (exact 0.100)")
print(f"Top 0.1% share, Exp(1):         {top_share(rng.exponential(1, 2_000_000)):.4f} (exact 0.0079)")

# 4. Share of the total made by the 10 biggest of 1,000 values (median of 20,000 runs).
for name, values in [("Exp(1)", rng.exponential(1, (20_000, 1000))), ("Pareto(1, 1.5)", pareto(1.5, (20_000, 1000))),
                     ("Pareto(1, 1.1)", pareto(1.1, (20_000, 1000)))]:
    s = np.sort(values, axis=1)
    print(f"{name:>14}: top 10 of 1000 make {np.median(s[:, -10:].sum(axis=1) / s.sum(axis=1)):.3f} of the total")
```

Output:

```text
Pareto(50, 2): P(X > 1000) simulation 0.002500, exact 0.002500
Exp(mean 100): P(X > 1000) simulation 0.000045, exact 0.000045
      Exp(1): simulation    5.0,     7.3; exact    5.0,     7.3
Pareto(1, 2): simulation   12.0,    38.0; exact   12.0,    38.0
Pareto(1, 1): simulation  144.9,  1448.5; exact  144.3,  1442.7
Top 0.1% share, Pareto(1, 1.5): 0.097 (exact 0.100)
Top 0.1% share, Exp(1):         0.0079 (exact 0.0079)
        Exp(1): top 10 of 1000 make 0.055 of the total
Pareto(1, 1.5): top 10 of 1000 make 0.171 of the total
Pareto(1, 1.1): top 10 of 1000 make 0.371 of the total
```

For the Pareto laws, the exact numbers match closely and the largest values dominate. The simulation of the heavy tail with $\alpha = 1.5$ is itself noisy (we used the median of 9 runs), which is another symptom of a fat tail: even the simulation needs a lot of data.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using a normal curve for everything.** A normal model says that a 6-standard-deviation event never happens. For wealth, losses and waiting times, such events do happen. Check how the largest values in your data compare with the typical ones.
- **Planning for the worst case so far.** With a fat tail, the worst day in your data is not the worst day that will happen. Keep a margin and ask what happens if the next one is 3 times bigger.
- **Trusting the average and the standard deviation.** For $\alpha \le 2$ the standard deviation does not exist, and for $\alpha \le 1$ the mean does not exist. The sample values keep jumping when you add data ([17.9 When the CLT Fails](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).
- **Thinking that fat tails mean "anything can happen".** The tail is still a precise law. A power law tells you how fast the chances fall, and you can plan with it: limit your exposure to any single event, buy cover, spread your risk across sources that do not fail together ([22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md)).
- **Mixing up rare and harmless.** A rare event can be rare and still be the main source of the total cost. Judge a risk by chance times size, and not by the chance alone ([22.3](22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** word frequencies, the sizes of web pages and the number of links follow power laws, and a few huge values can dominate training data and gradients. Robust losses and gradient clipping protect against them ([21.1.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)).
- **Computer science:** job running times and file sizes have heavy tails, so one huge job can block a queue, and averages of server delay are misleading ([21.3.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
- **Finance and insurance:** big losses and market falls are far more frequent than a normal curve says, and a few days can decide the result of many years ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Disasters and health:** floods, earthquakes and epidemics have fat tails in size. Preparation is planned for events much bigger than any in the records.
- **Everyday life:** income and followers, the time you wait for a delayed project, one huge expense that dominates a year.
- **Try this today:** for one quantity you plan around (a bill, a delay, a number of requests), write the typical value and the biggest one you have ever seen. If the biggest is more than 10 times the typical, do not plan with the average. Ask what you would do if the next one were 3 times bigger than the biggest.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A Pareto quantity has $x_m = 1$ and $\alpha = 2$. Find $P(X \gt 10)$ and $P(X \gt 100)$. How many times rarer is a value above 100 than a value above 10?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \gt 10) = 10^{-2} = 0.01, \qquad P(X \gt 100) = 100^{-2} = 0.0001$$

A value above 100 is 100 times rarer than a value above 10. A tenfold bigger event is only 100 times rarer. For a normal curve, the same step would make the chance far smaller (millions of times rarer or more).

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Which of these are more likely to have a fat tail: adult height, personal wealth, followers of social media accounts, marks in a class exam, size of files on a computer, daily temperature, population of cities, loss caused by an earthquake?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Likely fat-tailed: personal wealth, followers, file sizes, populations of cities, earthquake losses. Likely light-tailed: adult height, marks in a class exam (they have a maximum), daily temperature. A fast check: is there a natural limit near the average, or can one value be 100 times the typical one?

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Daily losses are Pareto with $x_m = 1$ and $\alpha = 3$. What share of the total expected loss comes from the top 1% of days? Compare with $\alpha = 1.5$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the share $q^{(\alpha - 1)/\alpha}$ with $q = 0.01$.

For $\alpha = 3$: $0.01^{2/3} = e^{(2/3)\ln 0.01} = e^{-3.070} = 0.0464$, about 4.6%.

For $\alpha = 1.5$: $0.01^{1/3} = e^{-1.535} = 0.2154$, about 21.5%.

The fatter tail ($\alpha = 1.5$) makes the top 1% of days responsible for more than a fifth of the total.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A light-tailed quantity (exponential with mean 1) and a fat-tailed quantity (Pareto with $x_m = 1$, $\alpha = 2$) are recorded for $m = 100$ days and then for $m = 10{,}000$ days. By how much does the typical worst value grow in each case?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Exponential: $x^{\ast} = \ln \frac{m}{\ln 2}$, so the growth is an addition of $\ln 100 = 4.61$ (from about 4.97 to 9.57). The factor is $\frac{9.57}{4.97} = 1.9$.

Pareto with $\alpha = 2$: $x^{\ast} = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\ln 2}}$, so the growth is a factor $\sqrt{100} = 10$ (from 12.0 to 120.1).

A hundred times more data only doubles the worst case for the light tail, and makes it ten times larger for the fat tail.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for a Pareto law with $\alpha \gt 1$, the share of the mean coming from values above $x$ is $\left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha - 1}$. Then explain why this share is at least as large as the chance $P(X \gt x)$ itself.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The density is $f(t) = \alpha x_m^{\alpha} t^{-\alpha - 1}$, so:

$$E[X \cdot I_{X \gt x}] = \int_x^{\infty} \alpha x_m^{\alpha} t^{-\alpha} \thinspace dt = \frac{\alpha x_m^{\alpha} x^{1 - \alpha}}{\alpha - 1}$$

With $E[X] = \frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1}$, the ratio is $x_m^{\alpha - 1} x^{1 - \alpha} = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha - 1}$.

The chance itself is $P(X \gt x) = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha}$. Since $\frac{x_m}{x} \le 1$ and $\alpha - 1 \lt \alpha$, we have $\left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha - 1} \ge \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha}$. The values above $x$ are more than their share of the days, because they are larger than the average. The ratio of the two is $\frac{x}{x_m}$, so the bigger the threshold, the more each rare day counts.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Both a light-tailed quantity (exponential, mean 3) and a fat-tailed quantity (Pareto with $x_m = 1$, $\alpha = 1.5$, mean 3) have the same mean. Average $n$ values, for $n = 1000$ and $n = 100{,}000$, in 500 runs each. Find the range that contains 80% of the sample means.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs = 500
for n in [1_000, 100_000]:
    # Each run averages n values. Both distributions have mean 3.
    heavy = ((1 - rng.random((n_runs, n))) ** (-1 / 1.5)).mean(axis=1)      # Pareto(1, 1.5), mean 3
    light = rng.exponential(3, (n_runs, n)).mean(axis=1)                      # Exp, mean 3
    h_lo, h_hi = np.percentile(heavy, [10, 90])
    l_lo, l_hi = np.percentile(light, [10, 90])
    print(f"n={n:>7}: 80% of means in [{l_lo:.2f}, {l_hi:.2f}] (light), [{h_lo:.2f}, {h_hi:.2f}] (heavy)")
```

Output:

```text
n=   1000: 80% of means in [2.89, 3.13] (light), [2.58, 3.31] (heavy)
n= 100000: 80% of means in [2.99, 3.01] (light), [2.91, 3.09] (heavy)
```

With 100,000 values the light-tailed average is known to within 0.01, and the fat-tailed average only to within about 0.09. The sample mean of a fat tail also tends to be below the true mean (most runs miss the rare giant values).

</details>

## Summary

- A fat tail means that $P(X \gt x)$ falls like a power: a value 10 times bigger is only $10^{\alpha}$ times rarer.
- The worst case grows with the amount of data: like $m^{1/\alpha}$ for a Pareto, and only like $\ln m$ for a light tail. The worst case so far is not the worst case.
- A few rare events can make a large share of the total, so averages, standard deviations and "typical days" mislead.
- Use the tail to plan: limit your exposure to a single event, keep margins, and avoid sources of risk that fail together.
- Real tails are hard to measure. Treat every number as a rough guide.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X \gt x) = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha}, \qquad \text{worst of } m \text{ values} \approx x_m \left( \frac{m}{\ln 2} \right)^{1/\alpha}}
$$
