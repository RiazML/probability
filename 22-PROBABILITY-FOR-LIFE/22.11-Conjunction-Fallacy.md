# 22.11 Conjunction Fallacy

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.11 Conjunction Fallacy

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [22.10 Survivorship Bias](22.10-Survivorship-Bias.md). You need [3.3 Consequences of the Axioms](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.3-Consequences-of-the-Axioms.md) and [4.2 Multiplication Rule and Chain Rule](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md).

> **Big idea:** "A and B" can never be more likely than "A" alone, so adding details to a story makes it more believable but never more probable.

---

## Why It Matters

A story with many details feels real. "The server crashed because a power cut hit the data centre while a backup was running" sounds more likely than "the server crashed". But the second sentence includes the first story and many others, so it is at least as likely. The mistake of rating "A and B" as more likely than "A" is the **conjunction fallacy**. It leads to bad forecasts, bad plans (every step "sounds likely" and the whole plan is not), and bad arguments (a detailed excuse feels more probable than a plain one). The rule behind it is one line of Module 03. This file shows how to catch the fallacy in daily life and how to price a long chain of steps.

## Intuition

Meet Rafi. He is 26, studied computer science, loves puzzles, and spends many weekends working on open-source projects. Which statement is more probable?

(a) Rafi works as a bank clerk.

(b) Rafi works as a bank clerk and writes code for an open-source project at weekends.

Many people pick (b), because it fits the description. But think of all the people who are like Rafi. Among them, the bank clerks form one group. The bank clerks who also write open-source code form a smaller part of that same group. Group (b) is **inside** group (a). It cannot be bigger.

The fallacy happens because we judge by **fit**, not by counting. "Does this story match the picture I have?" is a different question from "How often does it happen?". Psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman found this effect in the 1980s with a similar problem about a woman named Linda. Many people, including well-trained ones, chose the "more detailed" option.

## Picture

```text
   +--------------------------------------------------------------+
   |  A: Rafi is a bank clerk                  2 in 100 people    |
   |                                                              |
   |        +------------------------------------------+          |
   |        |  A and B: bank clerk and also writes     |          |
   |        |  open-source code      6 in 1,000 people |          |
   |        +------------------------------------------+          |
   +--------------------------------------------------------------+

   The inner box is a part of the outer box, so it can never be bigger.
```

Adding a detail ($B$) can only cut the group down. Every extra detail takes a piece away.

## Definition

For any two events $A$ and $B$, the **conjunction** is the event that both $A$ and $B$ happen, written $A \cap B$. Then:

$$
P(A \cap B) \le P(A) \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(A \cap B) \le P(B)
$$

**In words:** both things together are never more likely than either one alone. The **conjunction fallacy** is the belief that $P(A \cap B) \gt P(A)$.

Because $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B \mid A)$ ([4.2](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)), a story built from steps $E_1, E_2, \dots, E_n$ has the probability:

$$
P(E_1 \cap E_2 \cap \dots \cap E_n) = P(E_1) \thinspace P(E_2 \mid E_1) \thinspace P(E_3 \mid E_1 \cap E_2) \cdots P(E_n \mid E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_{n-1})
$$

**In words:** multiply the chance of each step, given that all the earlier steps happened. Every factor is at most 1, so each new step can only make the total smaller.

## Why It Is True

The event $A$ can be split into the part inside $B$ and the part outside $B$. These two parts do not overlap, so by the addition rule for disjoint events ([3.4](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.4-Addition-Rule.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A) &= P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B^c) && \text{(split } A \text{ into two disjoint parts)} \\
&\ge P(A \cap B) && \text{(because } P(A \cap B^c) \ge 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same argument with the roles of $A$ and $B$ swapped gives $P(A \cap B) \le P(B)$. The two sides are equal only when $P(A \cap B^c) = 0$, that is, when $A$ almost never happens without $B$.

For a chain of steps, apply the rule again and again. $P(E_1 \cap E_2 \cap E_3) \le P(E_1 \cap E_2) \le P(E_1)$. So the probability of the whole story is at most the probability of its **least likely** step, and usually much smaller.

**Also true for "or".** The union is the opposite: $P(A \cup B) \ge \max(P(A), P(B))$. "A or B" is always at least as likely as "A". So a vague statement ("something goes wrong") is more probable than a specific one ("the disk fails on Monday"), which is often a good reason to prefer broad predictions when you are unsure.

> **Note:** This is not a rule against detail. A detailed story **can** be true and useful. The rule says that it is less probable than its simpler part, so you should not pay a higher price (in belief or in plans) for a detailed story than for a simple one.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Rafi, with Numbers

**Problem.** Among 10,000 people with Rafi's profile, 2% are bank clerks. Among the clerks, 30% write open-source code at weekends, and among the others, 10% do. (Made-up numbers.) Find $P(A)$ for "bank clerk" and $P(A \cap B)$ for "bank clerk and writes code".

**Solution.**

1. $P(A) = 0.02$, which is 200 people.
2. $P(B \mid A) = 0.30$.
3. $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A) = 0.02 \times 0.30 = 0.006$, which is 60 people.

**Answer.** $P(A) = 0.02$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.006$. Statement (a) is 3.3 times as likely as statement (b).

**Check.** Even if every bank clerk wrote code ($P(B \mid A) = 1$), we would get $P(A \cap B) = P(A) = 0.02$, a tie. It can never be above. The simulation below gives $0.0199$ and $0.0060$.

### Example 2: A Five-Step Plan

**Problem.** You plan to launch a small app with 5 steps: finish the design, build it, pass testing, get the store approval, get 100 users in the first month. You think each step has a good chance given that the earlier steps worked: $0.95$, $0.90$, $0.85$, $0.90$ and $0.80$. What is the chance that the whole plan works?

**Solution.**

1. Multiply along the chain: $0.95 \times 0.90 = 0.855$.
2. $0.855 \times 0.85 = 0.72675$.
3. $0.72675 \times 0.90 = 0.654075$.
4. $0.654075 \times 0.80 = 0.52326$.

**Answer.** About 52%. Each step looks very likely, but the whole plan is a coin flip.

**Check.** The answer is lower than the weakest step (0.80), as the rule says. If you also expect each step to take the time you planned, the chance of finishing on time is lower still. This is one reason why projects are often late ([22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

### Example 3: The Excuse and the Plain Fact

**Problem.** Tomorrow, the chance of heavy rain is 0.4. If it rains, the chance of a traffic jam is 0.5. If it rains and there is a jam, the chance that you miss your exam is 0.1 (you have a margin). Compare "heavy rain, a jam, and you miss the exam" with "you miss the exam". Say that the exam is also missed for other reasons (a late alarm, a sick day) with total chance 0.01, in cases that do not match the rain story.

**Solution.**

1. The detailed story: $P(\text{rain} \cap \text{jam} \cap \text{miss}) = 0.4 \times 0.5 \times 0.1 = 0.02$.
2. The plain event "miss the exam" contains this story and the other reasons: $P(\text{miss}) = 0.02 + 0.01 = 0.03$.

**Answer.** The detailed story has probability 0.02, and the plain event has probability 0.03. The plain event is more probable, as it must be.

**Check.** A detailed excuse ("it rained, there was a jam, and ...") feels more believable than "I missed it", but it can only cover part of the cases. When you plan for risks, list the **broad** risk ("I might miss the exam") and its many causes, not only the one story you can picture.

## Simulate It

The code checks three things: the Rafi numbers, the five-step plan, and a test on 100,000 random worlds to see whether $P(A \cap B)$ ever beats $P(A)$ or $P(B)$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000

# 1. People like Rafi. A: bank clerk (2%). B: writes open-source code (30% of clerks, 10% of others).
is_clerk = rng.random(n) < 0.02
codes = rng.random(n) < np.where(is_clerk, 0.30, 0.10)
print(f"P(A) = {is_clerk.mean():.4f}   (exact 0.0200)")
print(f"P(A and B) = {(is_clerk & codes).mean():.4f}   (exact 0.0060)")

# 2. A five-step plan. Each step succeeds with the given chance, if the steps before it worked.
steps = np.array([0.95, 0.90, 0.85, 0.90, 0.80])
works = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
for p in steps:
    works &= rng.random(n) < p                       # a step counts only if all earlier steps worked
print(f"Plan works: simulation {works.mean():.4f}, exact {steps.prod():.4f}")

# 3. A random world: 100,000 random joint tables for two events. Is P(A and B) <= P(A) always?
table = rng.dirichlet([1, 1, 1, 1], size=100_000)    # P(A,B), P(A,not B), P(not A,B), P(not A,not B)
p_a, p_b, p_ab = table[:, 0] + table[:, 1], table[:, 0] + table[:, 2], table[:, 0]
print(f"Tables with P(A and B) > P(A) or > P(B): {np.sum((p_ab > p_a) | (p_ab > p_b))}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A) = 0.0199   (exact 0.0200)
P(A and B) = 0.0060   (exact 0.0060)
Plan works: simulation 0.5226, exact 0.5233
Tables with P(A and B) > P(A) or > P(B): 0
```

In 100,000 random worlds, the conjunction was never more probable than one of its parts. This is not luck: the rule is a theorem.

## Common Mistakes

- **Choosing by fit.** "Which story matches the person?" is not "Which is more probable?". Count the groups, or ask "is one statement a part of the other?".
- **Believing a plan because every step looks likely.** Multiply the steps. Five steps at 90% each give 59%. Ask which steps could fail together, and add a margin.
- **Rejecting broad statements.** "It will rain somewhere in the country this week" is very probable, "it will rain in my street on Tuesday at noon" is not. A broad claim is more likely to be true, and a precise claim is more useful if it is true. Do not mix up the two.
- **Thinking that independence is needed.** The rule $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$ holds for **any** two events. Independence is needed only to write $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$.
- **Forgetting that the steps can be linked.** If success in step 2 makes step 3 more likely, use the conditional chances $P(E_3 \mid E_1 \cap E_2)$, not the raw ones. If a single cause can break many steps, the total can be lower than the product suggests.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a language model that writes a long, detailed answer assigns it a low probability, because each word multiplies in a factor below 1. Longer sequences always have lower probability ([21.1.3](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.3-Language-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** a system with 5 services in a chain that must all work is less reliable than each service ([21.7](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)). A request that needs 3 cache hits in a row has a lower hit chance than each hit.
- **Law and news:** a detailed account ("the suspect, wearing a red coat, entered at 9, and left by the back door") is less probable than its parts. Each added detail must be checked, not admired.
- **Forecasting:** experts who build rich scenarios often give them higher chances than the sum of the parts allows. Break a big forecast into steps and multiply ([22.15](22.15-Calibration.md)).
- **Everyday life:** plans for travel, exams and projects. Excuses. Advertising claims with several parts.
- **Try this today:** for a plan with steps, write the chance of each step given the earlier ones, and multiply. If the answer is below what you need, find the weakest step or add a margin for it.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $P(A) = 0.3$ and $P(B \mid A) = 0.4$. Find $P(A \cap B)$. Can $P(A \cap B)$ be larger than $0.3$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(A \cap B) = 0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12$$

No. $P(A \cap B) \le P(A) = 0.3$ always. Here the conditional probability $P(B \mid A) = 0.4$ is at most 1, so the product can only be smaller than $P(A)$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Which is more probable: "you pass the exam", or "you pass the exam and score above 80"? Explain in one sentence.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"You pass the exam" is at least as probable. Every case in which you pass and score above 80 is also a case in which you pass, so the second event is a part of the first.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A plan has 4 steps. Given that earlier steps worked, the chances are $0.90$, $0.80$, $0.95$ and $0.70$. Find the chance that the whole plan works, and compare it with the weakest step.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$0.90 \times 0.80 \times 0.95 \times 0.70 = 0.72 \times 0.95 \times 0.70 = 0.684 \times 0.70 = 0.4788$$

About 48%, which is lower than the weakest step (0.70).

</details>

**4. (Medium)** If $P(A) = 0.7$ and $P(B) = 0.6$, what are the smallest and the largest possible values of $P(A \cap B)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The largest value is $\min(0.7, 0.6) = 0.6$ (when $B$ is contained in $A$). For the smallest value, use $P(A \cup B) \le 1$:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) \ge 0.7 + 0.6 - 1 = 0.3$$

So $0.3 \le P(A \cap B) \le 0.6$. Even in the worst case, the two events must overlap by at least 30%.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $P(E_1 \cap E_2 \cap \dots \cap E_n) \le \min_i P(E_i)$ for any events, and that it equals the product of the $P(E_i)$ when the events are independent. Why is the product, for $n$ independent events with probability $p \lt 1$ each, smaller than $p$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The intersection of all the events is a subset of each $E_i$, so by monotonicity (the same argument as in "Why It Is True") $P(E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n) \le P(E_i)$ for every $i$, and so it is at most the smallest of them.

For independent events, the multiplication rule gives $P(E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n) = P(E_1) \cdots P(E_n)$ ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)).

For $n$ events with the same $p \lt 1$, the product is $p^n$. Since $p^n = p \cdot p^{n-1}$ and $p^{n-1} \lt 1$ when $n \ge 2$, we get $p^n \lt p$. It also goes to 0 when $n$ grows.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000
steps = [0.90, 0.80, 0.95, 0.70]          # chance of each step, given that the earlier steps worked
works = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
for p in steps:
    works &= rng.random(n) < p
print(f"Whole plan works: simulation {works.mean():.4f}, exact {np.prod(steps):.4f}")
print(f"Weakest single step: {min(steps):.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
Whole plan works: simulation 0.4790, exact 0.4788
Weakest single step: 0.70
```

</details>

## Summary

- $P(A \cap B) \le \min(P(A), P(B))$ for any events: details can only take probability away.
- A story with many details feels more likely and is less likely. Judge by counting, not by fit.
- A chain of steps has the probability $P(E_1) P(E_2 \mid E_1) \cdots$, which is below its weakest step.
- "A or B" is always at least as likely as "A": broad claims are safer than precise claims.
- When you plan, multiply the steps and keep a margin for the weak ones.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B \mid A) \le P(A)}
$$
