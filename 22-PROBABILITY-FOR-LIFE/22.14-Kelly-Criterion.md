# 22.14 Kelly Criterion

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.14 Kelly Criterion

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [22.13 Fat Tails](22.13-Fat-Tails.md). You need [16.3 Jensen's Inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md) and [17.4 Strong Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md).

> **Big idea:** When you repeat a favorable bet and can bet any part of your money, the best long-run growth comes from betting a fixed fraction $f^{\ast} = p - \frac{1 - p}{b}$ of your wealth, never all of it, and betting too much is worse than betting too little.

---

## Why It Matters

If a bet has a good edge, how much of your money should you put on it? "All of it" has the highest average, but it ends in ruin with high probability. "A tiny amount" is safe but wastes the edge. The **Kelly criterion** gives the size that grows your money fastest in the long run. It is the clearest example of a rule that [Jensen's inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md) explains: with repeated bets, wealth is multiplied each time, so the log of the wealth is what grows steadily. The same thinking applies to any repeated risk: how much time, money or effort to put at stake each time.

> **Warning:** This file is about probability, not about investing. Real markets give no fixed $p$ and no fixed $b$, and estimates of your edge are always uncertain (Example 3). Nothing here is financial advice.

## Intuition

You have 1,000 taka. A friend offers a bet many times. You win with probability 0.6, and a win pays the same as the stake (even money). A loss costs the stake. The edge is good: on each taka staked you gain $0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2$ taka on average.

Bet everything each time? One loss and you are at zero, forever. After 100 bets, the chance that you have not lost even once is $0.6^{100} = 6.5 \times 10^{-23}$. Yet the **average** wealth is huge: it is multiplied by 1.2 each bet, so $1.2^{100} = 8.3 \times 10^{7}$ times. A few unbelievably lucky versions of you hold all of that average.

Bet a small fixed fraction, say 20% of your current money each time. After a win you have 1.2 times as much. After a loss you have 0.8 times as much. A loss never ruins you, and your stakes shrink when you lose and grow when you win. This is the Kelly bet: the fraction that makes your money grow fastest, in the typical case.

## Picture

```text
  bet size f   growth per bet x 1000      (p = 0.6, even-money bets)
    0.00      +0.0                    |
    0.05      +8.8                    |#########
    0.10     +15.0                    |###############
    0.15     +18.8                    |###################
    0.20     +20.1                    |####################  <- Kelly
    0.25     +18.8                    |###################
    0.30     +14.7                    |###############
    0.35      +7.7                    |########
    0.40      -2.4                  ##|
    0.45     -16.2    ################|
                 (1 # = 0.001 growth per bet; left of | = losses)
```

Growth rises to a peak at the Kelly fraction, then falls. Betting twice the Kelly size gives no growth at all, and betting more than that loses money, even though every single bet has a positive average.

## Definition

You bet a fraction $f$ of your current wealth ($0 \le f \lt 1$) on a bet that pays $b$ taka for each 1 taka staked if you win (we say the **odds are $b$ to 1**), and loses the stake otherwise. You win with probability $p$, and $q = 1 - p$. After one bet, your wealth is multiplied by:

$$
X =
\begin{cases}
1 + b f & \text{with probability } p \\
1 - f & \text{with probability } q
\end{cases}
$$

The **growth rate** is the average of the log of this multiplier:

$$
g(f) = E[\ln X] = p \ln(1 + bf) + q \ln(1 - f)
$$

**In words:** $g(f)$ is the typical growth of the log-wealth per bet. After $n$ bets your wealth is about $e^{n g(f)}$ times the start.

The **Kelly fraction** is the $f$ that makes $g(f)$ largest:

$$
f^{\ast} = \frac{bp - q}{b} = p - \frac{q}{b}
$$

**In words:** the edge ($bp - q$, your average gain per taka staked) divided by the odds $b$. If $bp \le q$, there is no edge, and the best choice is $f^{\ast} = 0$: do not bet.

## Why It Is True

**Why the log.** After $n$ bets, the wealth is $W_n = W_0 X_1 X_2 \cdots X_n$, a product of independent multipliers. Taking the log turns the product into a sum:

$$
\frac{1}{n} \ln \frac{W_n}{W_0} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln X_i \to E[\ln X] = g(f)
$$

The arrow is the Strong Law of Large Numbers ([17.4](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)), and it holds with probability 1. **In words:** in the long run, wealth grows (or shrinks) like $e^{n g(f)}$. Growth is governed by $E[\ln X]$ and not by $E[X]$. The two differ because $\ln$ is concave: $E[\ln X] \lt \ln E[X]$ ([16.3 Jensen](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)). Betting everything maximizes $E[X]$, but it makes $E[\ln X]$ equal to $-\infty$.

**Finding the best $f$.** Differentiate $g(f)$ and set the derivative to zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g'(f) &= \frac{p b}{1 + bf} - \frac{q}{1 - f} = 0 && \text{(derivative of the two logs)} \\
p b (1 - f) &= q (1 + bf) && \text{(cross-multiply)} \\
pb - pbf &= q + qbf && \text{(expand)} \\
pb - q &= bf (p + q) = bf && \text{(collect, and } p + q = 1 \text{)} \\
f^{\ast} &= \frac{bp - q}{b} && \text{(solve)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**It is a maximum.** The second derivative is $g''(f) = -\frac{p b^2}{(1 + bf)^2} - \frac{q}{(1 - f)^2} \lt 0$, so $g$ is concave, and the point where $g' = 0$ is the unique maximum.

**Why not more.** Since $g$ is concave with $g(0) = 0$, and a concave function that rises and then falls comes back to zero, there is a bet size above which $g(f) \lt 0$: your wealth shrinks to zero in the long run, even though each bet has positive average gain. For small edges, the zero point is close to $2 f^{\ast}$. **Betting twice the Kelly fraction gives about zero growth.**

**Half Kelly.** Near the top, $g$ looks like a parabola, so for small edges $g(\frac{1}{2} f^{\ast}) \approx \frac{3}{4} g(f^{\ast})$. Half the stake gives about 75% of the growth, with much smaller swings (Practice problem 4).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The 60-40 Bet

**Problem.** You win with probability $p = 0.6$ and get even money ($b = 1$). Find the Kelly fraction and the growth rate. How does the growth rate change for $f = 0.1$, $f = 0.3$ and $f = 0.4$? What wealth would you have after 100 bets, typically?

**Solution.**

1. $f^{\ast} = p - \frac{q}{b} = 0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2$.
2. Growth at $f = 0.2$: $g = 0.6 \ln 1.2 + 0.4 \ln 0.8 = 0.6 (0.18232) + 0.4 (-0.22314) = 0.10939 - 0.08926 = 0.02014$.
3. $f = 0.1$: $g = 0.6 \ln 1.1 + 0.4 \ln 0.9 = 0.6 (0.09531) + 0.4 (-0.10536) = 0.05719 - 0.04214 = 0.01504$. This is $\frac{0.01504}{0.02014} = 0.75$ of the best growth.
4. $f = 0.3$: $g = 0.6 \ln 1.3 + 0.4 \ln 0.7 = 0.6 (0.26236) + 0.4 (-0.35667) = 0.15742 - 0.14267 = 0.01475$.
5. $f = 0.4$: $g = 0.6 \ln 1.4 + 0.4 \ln 0.6 = 0.6 (0.33647) + 0.4 (-0.51083) = 0.20188 - 0.20433 = -0.00245$.
6. After 100 bets with $f = 0.2$, the typical wealth is $e^{100 \times 0.02014} = e^{2.014} = 7.49$ times the start.

**Answer.** $f^{\ast} = 0.2$, with growth 2.0% per bet. $f = 0.1$ gives 75% of that growth, $f = 0.3$ gives 73%, and $f = 0.4$ gives a small loss. A typical result after 100 bets with Kelly is about 7.5 times the start.

**Check.** The mean wealth with $f = 0.2$ is $(1 + 0.2 \times 0.2)^{100} = 1.04^{100} = 50.5$ times the start, far above the typical 7.5, because a few lucky people have a huge amount. Betting everything has a mean of $1.2^{100} = 8.3 \times 10^{7}$ times the start, and the chance of anything left is $0.6^{100}$. The simulation below confirms the growth rates.

### Example 2: Different Odds

**Problem.** Find the Kelly fraction for (a) $p = 0.4$ and $b = 2$, (b) $p = 0.5$ and $b = 2$, (c) $p = 0.3$ and $b = 2$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The edge is $bp - q = 2(0.4) - 0.6 = 0.2$. $f^{\ast} = \frac{0.2}{2} = 0.1$. Check with the other form: $0.4 - \frac{0.6}{2} = 0.4 - 0.3 = 0.1$.
2. (b) The edge is $2(0.5) - 0.5 = 0.5$. $f^{\ast} = \frac{0.5}{2} = 0.25$.
3. (c) The edge is $2(0.3) - 0.7 = -0.1$, which is negative. The formula gives $f^{\ast} = -0.05$, and since we cannot bet a negative amount, $f^{\ast} = 0$.

**Answer.** (a) 10% of wealth, (b) 25%, (c) do not bet.

**Check.** In case (a) you win only 40% of the time, but a win pays twice the stake, so each taka staked earns $0.4 \times 2 - 0.6 = 0.2$ on average. The growth rate is $0.4 \ln 1.2 + 0.6 \ln 0.9 = 0.07293 - 0.06322 = 0.00971$ per bet. In case (c), every bet loses on average, and the best amount to bet is zero.

### Example 3: When Your Edge Is Smaller Than You Think

**Problem.** You believe that $p = 0.6$ (so you bet the Kelly fraction $f = 0.2$ at even money), but the truth is $p = 0.55$. Find the growth rate you really get, and compare with betting the Kelly fraction for the true $p$.

**Solution.**

1. The true Kelly fraction is $0.55 - 0.45 = 0.10$.
2. Growth with your bet $f = 0.2$ and the true $p$: $g = 0.55 \ln 1.2 + 0.45 \ln 0.8 = 0.55 (0.18232) + 0.45 (-0.22314) = 0.10028 - 0.10041 = -0.00014$.
3. Growth with $f = 0.1$: $g = 0.55 \ln 1.1 + 0.45 \ln 0.9 = 0.55 (0.09531) + 0.45 (-0.10536) = 0.05242 - 0.04741 = +0.00501$.

**Answer.** Your "Kelly" bet has a growth rate of $-0.00014$ per bet: zero, slightly negative. Betting half as much (the real Kelly) grows at $+0.00501$ per bet.

**Check.** You overestimated the edge by 5 points ($0.6$ against $0.55$), and your bet became twice the right size, which is the zero-growth point. This is why people who use the Kelly idea in practice bet a **fraction** of it (half or less): the true edge is never known, and overbetting costs far more than underbetting. The simulation in Practice problem 6 gives the same numbers.

## Simulate It

We simulate 200,000 players, each making 1,000 bets with $p = 0.6$ at even money, with different fractions $f$. For each fraction, we find the typical growth rate of the log-wealth, the median wealth, and the share of players below their start.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p, b, n_bets, n_players = 0.6, 1.0, 1000, 200_000
print(f"Kelly fraction f* = p - (1 - p)/b = {p - (1 - p) / b:.2f}")
print("  f     growth/bet (simulation, exact)   median wealth   ended below start")

for f in [0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.60]:
    # Each bet: wealth is multiplied by (1 + b f) with chance p, and by (1 - f) otherwise.
    wins = rng.binomial(n_bets, p, size=n_players)
    log_wealth = wins * np.log(1 + b * f) + (n_bets - wins) * np.log(1 - f)
    exact = p * np.log(1 + b * f) + (1 - p) * np.log(1 - f)
    print(f"{f:.2f}     {np.median(log_wealth) / n_bets:+.4f}  {exact:+.4f}"
          f"              x {np.exp(np.median(log_wealth)):9.3g}        {np.mean(log_wealth < 0):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
Kelly fraction f* = p - (1 - p)/b = 0.20
  f     growth/bet (simulation, exact)   median wealth   ended below start
0.05     +0.0088  +0.0088              x  6.35e+03        0.000
0.10     +0.0150  +0.0150              x  3.41e+06        0.000
0.20     +0.0201  +0.0201              x  5.56e+08        0.001
0.30     +0.0147  +0.0147              x  2.54e+06        0.065
0.40     -0.0024  -0.0024              x    0.0866        0.562
0.60     -0.0845  -0.0845              x  1.98e-37        1.000
```

The growth rate peaks at $f = 0.2$ and matches the formula. With $f = 0.4$ (twice Kelly), the median player ends with less than the start. With $f = 0.6$ the median wealth is $10^{-37}$ of the start, which means ruin, although every bet has a positive average gain. (The wealth numbers are the typical values after 1,000 bets, which is a very long run of bets. They show the shape of the effect.)

## Common Mistakes

- **Betting to maximize the average.** The average is maximized by betting everything, which makes ruin almost certain. For repeated bets, maximize the growth of the log of the wealth.
- **Using Kelly with an edge you have guessed.** The formula needs the true $p$ and $b$. Real edges are small and uncertain, and overestimating them makes the bet too big (Example 3). Use a fraction of Kelly, and doubt your own estimate.
- **Betting when there is no edge.** If $bp \le q$, the formula says $f^{\ast} = 0$. No staking plan can turn a game with a negative average into a winning one ([21.10](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).
- **Forgetting that Kelly has big swings.** Even at the best fraction, wealth can fall by half before it recovers, because the stakes follow the wealth. Many people cannot live with these swings, and a smaller fraction gives a smoother path ([22.4](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md)).
- **Treating it as a rule only for gamblers.** The idea that "repeated risks multiply" is general: a risk that you can take many times must be small compared with what you can lose ([22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the log loss in classification is the same quantity as the log growth rate: maximizing the expected log of the probability you assigned to what happened ([21.1.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)). A well calibrated model is the best "bettor" ([22.15](22.15-Calibration.md)).
- **Computer science and information theory:** in a repeated bet with fixed odds, the best growth rate you can reach is linked to how much you know. For a horse race, the extra growth that private information gives equals its mutual information, measured in bits. This is a result of information theory, outside this book.
- **Finance and insurance:** portfolio and betting sizes are often discussed in terms of Kelly or fractions of it. Insurers keep reserves so that no single bad year ruins them ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)). This is a model, not advice.
- **Sport and games:** in a game with an edge (card counting, or a skilled player in a game with a fixed payout), stake size decides whether skill turns into growth ([21.10](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).
- **Everyday life:** how much of your savings, time or reputation to risk on one repeated venture. A good rule is: size it so that a long bad streak (see [22.5](22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md) and [22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)) does not end the game.
- **Try this today:** for any risk you can take again and again, write the chance of a win, the payoff ratio $b$ and compute $p - \frac{q}{b}$. If it is zero or negative, do not stake anything. If positive, stake **less than half** of that, because you do not know $p$ exactly.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You win with probability 0.55 and get even money. What is the Kelly fraction?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f^{\ast} = p - \frac{q}{b} = 0.55 - \frac{0.45}{1} = 0.10$$

Bet 10% of your wealth each time.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You win with probability 0.4 and get even money. What does the Kelly formula say?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f^{\ast} = 0.4 - 0.6 = -0.2$$

A negative value means that the bet has a negative average. The best bet size is 0: do not bet. (Betting a negative amount would mean taking the other side, which is a different bet.)

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You win with probability 0.5 and a win pays 3 taka for each taka staked ($b = 3$). Find the Kelly fraction and the growth rate per bet.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f^{\ast} = 0.5 - \frac{0.5}{3} = 0.5 - 0.1667 = 0.3333$$

With $f = \frac{1}{3}$: a win multiplies wealth by $1 + 3 \times \frac{1}{3} = 2$, a loss by $1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.

$$g = 0.5 \ln 2 + 0.5 \ln \tfrac{2}{3} = 0.5 \ln \tfrac{4}{3} = 0.5 \times 0.28768 = 0.14384$$

So wealth grows by about 14% (in log terms) per bet.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For $p = 0.6$ and $b = 1$, compare the growth rates at $f = 0.1$, $f = 0.2$ and $f = 0.4$. What fraction of the best growth do you get with half Kelly? What happens with double Kelly?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From Example 1: $g(0.1) = 0.01504$, $g(0.2) = 0.02014$, $g(0.4) = -0.00245$.

Half Kelly gives $\frac{0.01504}{0.02014} = 0.747$, about 75% of the best growth. Double Kelly gives $-0.00245$, a small loss: zero growth, with all the risk. Being too big by a factor 2 costs much more than being too small by a factor 2, and this is the reason why people bet a fraction of Kelly.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $g(f) = p \ln(1 + bf) + q \ln(1 - f)$ is concave on $0 \le f \lt 1$, and that its maximum is at $f^{\ast} = p - \frac{q}{b}$ when $bp \gt q$. What is the maximum when $b = 1$ and $p = \frac{1}{2} + \varepsilon$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$g'(f) = \frac{pb}{1 + bf} - \frac{q}{1 - f}, \qquad g''(f) = -\frac{p b^2}{(1 + bf)^2} - \frac{q}{(1 - f)^2}$$

Both terms of $g''$ are negative for $0 \le f \lt 1$, so $g$ is concave. Setting $g' = 0$ gives $f^{\ast} = p - \frac{q}{b}$ (the steps are in "Why It Is True"). Since $g'(0) = pb - q \gt 0$ when there is an edge, the maximum is at a positive $f$, and by concavity it is the global one.

For $b = 1$ and $p = \frac{1}{2} + \varepsilon$: $f^{\ast} = 2\varepsilon$, then $1 + f^{\ast} = 1 + 2\varepsilon$ and $1 - f^{\ast} = 1 - 2\varepsilon$, and

$$g(f^{\ast}) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2} + \varepsilon\right) \ln(1 + 2\varepsilon) + \left(\tfrac{1}{2} - \varepsilon\right) \ln(1 - 2\varepsilon) \approx 2\varepsilon^2$$

using $\ln(1 \pm x) \approx \pm x - \frac{x^2}{2}$. For $\varepsilon = 0.1$ (the case $p = 0.6$) this gives $0.02$, which matches the exact $0.02014$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 3 by simulation: the true $p$ is $0.55$, and you bet $f = 0.2$ (Kelly for a believed $p = 0.6$) or $f = 0.1$ (Kelly for the true $p$), for 1,000 bets.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p_true, b, n_bets, n_players = 0.55, 1.0, 1000, 200_000
for f, label in [(0.20, "Kelly for the believed p = 0.6"), (0.10, "Kelly for the true p = 0.55")]:
    wins = rng.binomial(n_bets, p_true, size=n_players)
    log_wealth = wins * np.log(1 + b * f) + (n_bets - wins) * np.log(1 - f)
    exact = p_true * np.log(1 + b * f) + (1 - p_true) * np.log(1 - f)
    print(f"f = {f:.2f} ({label}):")
    print(f"   growth per bet {np.median(log_wealth) / n_bets:+.5f} (exact {exact:+.5f}), "
          f"median wealth x{np.exp(np.median(log_wealth)):.3g}")
```

```text

   growth per bet -0.00014 (exact -0.00014), median wealth x0.871
f = 0.10 (Kelly for the true p = 0.55):
   growth per bet +0.00501 (exact +0.00501), median wealth x150
```

</details>

## Summary

- For repeated bets where you can choose the stake, long-run growth is $E[\ln X]$ per bet, not $E[X]$.
- The best fixed fraction is $f^{\ast} = p - \frac{q}{b}$, which is the edge divided by the odds. With no edge, bet nothing.
- Betting everything gives the best average and almost sure ruin. Betting twice Kelly gives no growth.
- Betting less than Kelly keeps most of the growth (half Kelly: about 75%) with smaller swings, so it is the safe side.
- Your estimate of the edge is uncertain, so use a fraction of the Kelly size.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f^{\ast} = p - \frac{1 - p}{b}}
$$
