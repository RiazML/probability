> **Big idea:** When you say "I am 70% sure", you should be right about 70% of the time, and the only way to find out if you are is to write your predictions down, score them, and learn from the score.

---

## Why It Matters

Every plan, promise and decision uses a hidden probability: "I will finish by Friday" (90%?), "this candidate will do well" (70%?), "the exam will have this topic" (60%?). If your "90%" really means 70%, you will make plans that fail a quarter of the time more often than you expect. Most people are **overconfident**: their stated certainty is higher than their hit rate. The good news is that calibration is a skill you can train, and it is the only part of probability in this book that you can measure on yourself. This file shows how to keep score, how many predictions you need before the score means anything, and how to fix the most common error (intervals that are too narrow).

## Intuition

Rina keeps a prediction journal for a month. Each time she makes a prediction, she writes a probability and a date: "My team will ship on Friday: 90%". "The bus will be late tomorrow: 70%". At the end of the month she has 20 predictions with their results.

She groups them by the probability she gave. Among the 8 predictions where she said 90%, only 6 came true (75%). Among the 8 where she said 70%, 5 came true (62.5%). Among 4 predictions at 50%, 2 came true. Her average confidence was 74%, and her hit rate was 65%. She is overconfident by about 9 points.

Is that proof? Not yet: 8 predictions is a small sample, and even a perfectly calibrated person would show a result like this sometimes ([22.7](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md)). What the journal does is to turn a vague feeling ("I am usually right") into a number that gets more reliable with every month.

## Picture

```text
  You said    Predictions    Came true    Hit rate
   90%            8              6          75.0%  |###############     |
   70%            8              5          62.5%  |############        |
   50%            4              2          50.0%  |##########          |
                                                    0%              100%

  Average confidence 74%, real hit rate 65%: overconfident by 9 points.
```

A calibrated person has a hit rate equal to the stated confidence in every row. Here the 90% row should be 90%, the 70% row should be 70%, and so on.

## Definition

You are **calibrated** if, among all the predictions where you gave the probability $p$, the fraction that came true is about $p$ ([21.1.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)). To measure it, group your predictions by the stated probability and compare each group's **hit rate** with the stated value.

You are **overconfident** if the hit rates are below the stated confidence at the high end (and above it at the low end), and **underconfident** if it is the other way round.

Calibration is not enough: a person who says "50%" about everything is calibrated if half of the things happen, but the predictions carry no information. To score both calibration and sharpness, use the **Brier score** of your predictions $p_1, \dots, p_n$ with outcomes $y_i \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$ ([21.11](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)):

$$
\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)^2
$$

**In words:** the average squared miss. It is 0 for perfect predictions and 0.25 for always saying 50%. Lower is better.

For a **range prediction** (for example "the bus takes between 20 and 40 minutes, 90% sure"), calibration means that about 90% of your 90% ranges contain the true value. The **hit rate** of your ranges is the number to check.

## Why It Is True

**Honesty is the best policy.** Suppose the true chance of an event is $q$, and you report $r$. Your expected squared miss is $q (1 - r)^2 + (1 - q) r^2$. Expanding and collecting:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q (1 - r)^2 + (1 - q) r^2 &= q - 2qr + q r^2 + r^2 - q r^2 && \text{(expand)} \\
&= q - 2qr + r^2 && \text{(the } q r^2 \text{ terms cancel)} \\
&= q (1 - q) + (r - q)^2 && \text{(complete the square)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**In words:** the expected score is the unavoidable noise $q(1-q)$ plus the square of your report's error $(r - q)^2$. It is smallest when $r = q$. Since you pay for every distance between what you say and what you believe, there is no gain in exaggerating. This is why the Brier score is called a **proper** score ([21.11](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)).

**How many predictions do you need?** Among $n$ predictions at "90%" the number of hits is $\text{Bin}(n, 0.9)$ if you are calibrated. Its standard deviation, as a hit rate, is $\sqrt{\frac{0.9 \times 0.1}{n}}$ ([22.7](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md)). To notice that your true rate is 80%, the gap of 10 points must be larger than the noise. With the cut-off "flag me if the hits are lower than 95% of calibrated people would get", the chance of noticing grows slowly: Example 2 gives numbers.

**Ranges that are too narrow.** If a quantity has errors with spread $\sigma$ (normal), a correct 90% range is the best guess $\pm 1.645 \sigma$. If you feel a range of $\pm 1.645 \cdot c \sigma$ is "90%", with $c \lt 1$, the real hit rate is:

$$
P\left( \lvert Z \rvert \lt 1.645 \thinspace c \right) = 2 \Phi(1.645 \thinspace c) - 1
$$

where $Z$ is standard normal and $\Phi$ is its CDF. **In words:** if your ranges are only 60% as wide as they should be ($c = 0.6$), they contain the truth $2\Phi(0.987) - 1 = 0.676$ of the time, not 0.90. The fix is to multiply your first-guess range by $\frac{1}{c}$, in this case 1.67.

> **Note:** A journal measures **you**, on the kind of questions you wrote down. Being calibrated on weather and exams does not mean being calibrated on money or relationships. Keep the journal for each area you care about. The numbers in this file are made up.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Scoring a Month of Predictions

**Problem.** Rina's journal has 20 predictions. At "90%": 8 predictions, 6 came true. At "70%": 8 predictions, 5 came true. At "50%": 4 predictions, 2 came true. Find her average confidence, her hit rate, and her Brier score. Compare with simple strategies.

**Solution.**

1. Average confidence: $\frac{8(0.9) + 8(0.7) + 4(0.5)}{20} = \frac{7.2 + 5.6 + 2.0}{20} = \frac{14.8}{20} = 0.74$.
2. Hit rate: $\frac{6 + 5 + 2}{20} = \frac{13}{20} = 0.65$.
3. Squared misses. At 90%: 6 hits cost $(0.9 - 1)^2 = 0.01$ each and 2 misses cost $(0.9 - 0)^2 = 0.81$ each, so $6(0.01) + 2(0.81) = 0.06 + 1.62 = 1.68$. At 70%: $5(0.09) + 3(0.49) = 0.45 + 1.47 = 1.92$. At 50%: $4 \times 0.25 = 1.00$.
4. Brier score: $\frac{1.68 + 1.92 + 1.00}{20} = \frac{4.60}{20} = 0.230$.
5. Baselines: always saying 50% gives $0.250$. Always saying the hit rate $0.65$ gives $0.65 \times 0.35 = 0.2275$.

**Answer.** Confidence 74%, hit rate 65%, Brier 0.230.

**Check.** Rina is a little better than "always 50%" (0.250), but not better than just saying 65% every time (0.2275). With only 20 predictions, the difference between 0.230 and 0.2275 is noise. What the numbers do say is that her high-confidence predictions are not much more reliable than her low-confidence ones. The Picture shows the same.

### Example 2: How Many Predictions Do You Need?

**Problem.** You say "90%" for each of $n$ predictions, but the real hit rate is 80%. You test yourself with this rule: "flag as overconfident if the number of hits is so low that a perfectly calibrated person would get that few or fewer at most 5% of the time". How likely are you to be flagged for $n = 20, 50, 100, 200$?

**Solution.**

1. For each $n$, find the largest number of hits $c$ such that $P(\text{Bin}(n, 0.9) \le c) \le 0.05$. These are $c = 15, 40, 84, 172$ (the chances of a false alarm for a calibrated person are 4.3%, 2.5%, 4.0% and 4.3%).
2. The chance of being flagged when the true rate is 0.8 is $P(\text{Bin}(n, 0.8) \le c)$.
3. The binomial sums give: $n = 20$: $0.370$. $n = 50$: $0.556$. $n = 100$: $0.871$. $n = 200$: $0.989$.

**Answer.** With 20 predictions, you notice a 10-point overconfidence only 37% of the time. With 100 predictions, 87%. With 200, 99%.

**Check.** The simulation below gives the same numbers. Moral: do not trust a calibration check from 10 or 20 predictions, but a journal of 100 predictions in each confidence zone already tells a lot. Keep going for months.

### Example 3: Ranges That Are Too Narrow

**Problem.** You give 90% ranges for 1,000 unknown quantities (distances, prices, waiting times), and your ranges are only 0.6 times as wide as they should be. How often does the truth fall inside? How wide should you make a range to hit 90%?

**Solution.**

1. A correct 90% range is $\pm 1.645$ standard deviations. Yours is $\pm 1.645 \times 0.6 = \pm 0.987$.
2. $P(\lvert Z \rvert \lt 0.987) = 2 \Phi(0.987) - 1 = 2 (0.8382) - 1 = 0.6764$.
3. To correct it, multiply the width by $\frac{1}{0.6} = 1.667$.

**Answer.** Only about 68% of your "90%" ranges contain the truth (so one in three misses, not one in ten). Widen your gut-feeling ranges by about 1.7 times.

**Check.** The simulation gives $0.6764$ before and $0.9003$ after widening. This kind of overconfidence is common: when we are asked for a range, we think about the typical case and forget the surprises. A good habit is to ask: "what would have to happen for the answer to be outside this range?", and to imagine a few such cases before you fix the range.

## Simulate It

The code scores the journal of Example 1, finds how often a person with 80% real accuracy is flagged (Example 2), and checks the hit rate of too-narrow ranges (Example 3).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom, norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. A journal of 20 predictions: stated confidence, number made, number that came true.
conf = np.array([0.9, 0.7, 0.5]); made = np.array([8, 8, 4]); true = np.array([6, 5, 2])
hits = true.sum() / made.sum()
brier = np.sum(true * (1 - conf) ** 2 + (made - true) * conf ** 2) / made.sum()
print(f"Average confidence {np.sum(conf * made) / made.sum():.2f}, hit rate {hits:.2f}, Brier {brier:.3f}")
print(f"Brier of always saying 50%: 0.250; of always saying {hits:.2f}: {hits * (1 - hits):.4f}")

# 2. You say "90%" but you are right only 80% of the time. After n such predictions, do we notice?
for n in [20, 50, 100, 200]:
    cutoff = binom.ppf(0.05, n, 0.9) - 1                      # flag if hits <= cutoff (false alarm <= 5%)
    flagged = rng.binomial(n, 0.8, size=1_000_000) <= cutoff
    print(f"n={n:>3}: flagged as overconfident: simulation {flagged.mean():.3f}, "
          f"exact {binom.cdf(cutoff, n, 0.8):.3f}")

# 3. "90% intervals" that are only 0.6 times as wide as they should be.
truth = rng.normal(size=1_000_000)                           # errors in units of the true spread
print(f"Hit rate of your 90% intervals: simulation {np.mean(np.abs(truth) < 1.645 * 0.6):.4f}, "
      f"exact {2 * norm.cdf(1.645 * 0.6) - 1:.4f}")
print(f"After widening by 1/0.6: {np.mean(np.abs(truth) < 1.645):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Average confidence 0.74, hit rate 0.65, Brier 0.230
Brier of always saying 50%: 0.250; of always saying 0.65: 0.2275
n= 20: flagged as overconfident: simulation 0.370, exact 0.370
n= 50: flagged as overconfident: simulation 0.556, exact 0.556
n=100: flagged as overconfident: simulation 0.871, exact 0.871
n=200: flagged as overconfident: simulation 0.989, exact 0.989
Hit rate of your 90% intervals: simulation 0.6764, exact 0.6764
After widening by 1/0.6: 0.9003
```

A 10-point overconfidence is easy to miss with a short journal and impossible to miss with a long one. And ranges that feel like 90% can hide a hit rate of two thirds.

## Common Mistakes

- **Judging from a handful of predictions.** Fifteen predictions cannot tell a calibrated person from an overconfident one (Example 2). Collect many, and look at the pattern over months.
- **Writing only the predictions you remember.** If you record the exciting ones and forget the boring ones, the journal is itself a survivorship bias ([22.10](22.10-Survivorship-Bias.md)). Write down every prediction at the time you make it, before the result.
- **Vague predictions.** "It will go well" cannot be scored. Write a clear statement with a date, "the report will be sent by Friday 5 pm", so that anyone can say if it came true.
- **Using 0% and 100%.** If you say 100% and are wrong once, the Brier score cannot hide it. With the log loss ([21.1.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)), a 100% claim that fails costs infinity. Use 1% and 99%, and keep the extremes for logic and facts.
- **Changing the story after the result.** "I knew it" (hindsight) and "it was almost right" make you feel calibrated without being so. Compare the number you wrote with the outcome, and nothing else.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a classifier with a "90%" label should be right 90% of the time. Overconfident networks are corrected with one number, the temperature, on held-out data ([21.1.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)). The same scores are used to check language models.
- **Computer science:** estimates of time for a task ("two days") are intervals in disguise. Teams that record their past estimates and the real times can calibrate their ranges, exactly as in Example 3 ([22.11](22.11-Conjunction-Fallacy.md) explains why plans with many steps are too optimistic).
- **Weather and forecasting:** weather services are scored by the Brier score and reliability diagrams, and some public forecasting contests are scored with the same kind of proper scores ([21.11](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)).
- **Medicine and engineering:** a risk score is useful only if it is calibrated, because decisions are made from the number ([21.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)).
- **Everyday life:** deadlines, exam results, the chance that a plan works, the chance that a friend will come. The better calibrated you are, the better you can use the tools of this module: base rates ([22.1](22.1-Base-Rates.md)), updating ([22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md)), expected value ([22.3](22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md)) and the Kelly size ([22.14](22.14-Kelly-Criterion.md)). All of them need honest probabilities as input.
- **Try this today:** open a note on your phone. Write 3 predictions about the next week, each with a clear statement, a probability and a date. When the date comes, write "yes" or "no". After 100 predictions, group them by probability (50 to 60, 70 to 80, 90 or more) and compare with the hit rates.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Of 10 predictions at "80%", 6 came true. What is the hit rate, and how big is the usual noise of a hit rate with 10 predictions if you were truly calibrated?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The hit rate is $\frac{6}{10} = 0.6$, which is 20 points below the stated 80%. If you were calibrated, the standard deviation of the hit rate would be $\sqrt{\frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{10}} = 0.126$. So the miss is about 1.6 standard deviations: suggestive, but not strong evidence. You need more predictions.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You say "80%" about an event. Find your squared miss (Brier loss) if it happens, and if it does not.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If it happens: $(0.8 - 1)^2 = 0.04$. If it does not: $(0.8 - 0)^2 = 0.64$. The loss for a miss is 16 times the loss for a hit, which is why very confident wrong predictions are expensive.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Five predictions: (90%, happened), (80%, happened), (70%, did not), (60%, happened), (30%, did not). Find the Brier score.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Squared misses: $(0.9 - 1)^2 = 0.01$, $(0.8 - 1)^2 = 0.04$, $(0.7 - 0)^2 = 0.49$, $(0.6 - 1)^2 = 0.16$, $(0.3 - 0)^2 = 0.09$.

$$\text{Brier} = \frac{0.01 + 0.04 + 0.49 + 0.16 + 0.09}{5} = \frac{0.79}{5} = 0.158$$

This is better than the 0.25 of always saying 50%.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Your "80% ranges" are only half as wide as they should be. Use the normal curve, with $z = 1.2816$ for a correct 80% range, to find how often the truth is inside your ranges.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Your half-width is $1.2816 \times 0.5 = 0.6408$ standard deviations.

$$P(\lvert Z \rvert \lt 0.6408) = 2 \Phi(0.6408) - 1 = 2 (0.7392) - 1 = 0.4783$$

The truth is inside only 48% of the time, instead of 80%.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that if the true chance of an event is $q$, the expected squared miss of a report $r$ is $q(1-q) + (r - q)^2$, so that the best report is $r = q$. Explain what this says about exaggerating your confidence.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The expected squared miss is $q(1 - r)^2 + (1 - q) r^2$. Expand: $q - 2qr + qr^2 + r^2 - qr^2 = q - 2qr + r^2$. Now add and subtract $q^2$:

$$q - 2qr + r^2 = (q - q^2) + (q^2 - 2qr + r^2) = q(1 - q) + (r - q)^2$$

The first part does not depend on your report. The second part is zero only when $r = q$, and positive otherwise. So any exaggeration (say $r = 0.95$ when you believe $q = 0.8$) adds a cost of $(0.15)^2 = 0.0225$ to your expected score. You do not gain by sounding more certain.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 4 by simulation, and also check that ranges with the right width have a hit rate of 80%.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
z80 = norm.ppf(0.90)                                   # an 80% interval is the mean +/- 1.2816 spreads
errors = rng.normal(size=2_000_000)                    # real errors, in units of the true spread
for width in [1.0, 0.5]:
    hit = np.mean(np.abs(errors) < z80 * width)
    print(f"Intervals {width:.1f} times the right width: hit rate {hit:.4f}, "
          f"exact {2 * norm.cdf(z80 * width) - 1:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Intervals 1.0 times the right width: hit rate 0.8001, exact 0.8000
Intervals 0.5 times the right width: hit rate 0.4785, exact 0.4783
```

</details>

## Summary

- You are calibrated when your stated chance $p$ comes true about a fraction $p$ of the time. Most people are overconfident, especially at 90% and above, and with ranges.
- Keep a journal: a clear statement, a probability, a date, and the result. Review it in groups by probability, and with the Brier score.
- The Brier score rewards honest reports: the expected score is $q(1-q) + (r - q)^2$.
- You need about 100 predictions to notice a 10-point overconfidence. A small journal is mostly noise.
- If your ranges miss too often, widen them: ranges that feel like 90% are often only 70% ranges.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)^2}
$$
