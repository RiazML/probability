# 22.5 Gambler's Fallacy

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.5 Gambler's Fallacy

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [22.4 Variance and Risk](22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md). You need [4.7 Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md).

> **Big idea:** A fair coin, die or lottery machine has no memory, so a streak never makes the opposite result "due"; the average evens out by dilution, not by correction.

---

## Why It Matters

After five heads, many people feel that tails must come soon. After a long dry spell, "my luck must turn". This feeling is the **gambler's fallacy**: the false belief that independent random trials "balance themselves" in the short run. It costs money at casinos and lotteries, and it spoils many daily judgments: a captain who "must win the toss now", a family that "must have a boy next", a bus that "must come soon". [Module 04](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md) proves that independent trials do not remember. This file shows how to spot the fallacy, when the feeling is correct (cards, not coins), and what the Law of Large Numbers really says.

## Intuition

A cricket captain has lost the toss 5 times in a row. His team says, "Now we are due a win." Is that true?

The coin used for the toss has no memory. It does not know about the five losses. The chance of winning the next toss is still $\frac{1}{2}$. What is rare is the whole sequence of six losses: that has chance $\frac{1}{64}$, about 1.6%. But we already know the first five. Only the last toss is still open, and it is a fair toss.

Why does the feeling of "due" appear? Because we know that in the long run about half of the tosses are wins. So we think the coin must "pay back". The coin does not pay back. It just keeps tossing, and the early losses become a smaller and smaller part of the total as more tosses come. That is **dilution**. (Streaks are also much more common than people expect. Over a season of 100 tosses, a run of 5 equal results is almost certain.)

## Picture

```text
   flips so far:   T   H   H   H   H   H   ?   <- next flip
                       |   |   |   |   |   |
                       +---+---+---+---+   |
                       the coin does not   |
                       remember these      v
                                     H : 50%     T : 50%

   Wrong thinking:  "5 heads, so tails is due"      (the coin has memory)
   Right thinking:  "5 heads, and the next flip is still 50-50"
```

The past flips do not change the next one. The probability of the next flip given the past is the same as without the past.

## Definition

Trials are **independent** if the result of one does not change the chances of the others. For flips $X_1, X_2, \dots$ of a fair coin, this means:

$$
P(X_{n+1} = \text{H} \mid X_1 = \text{H}, \dots, X_n = \text{H}) = P(X_{n+1} = \text{H}) = \frac{1}{2}
$$

**In words:** knowing that the first $n$ flips were all heads does not change the chance that the next flip is heads.

The **gambler's fallacy** is the belief that after a streak the opposite result becomes more likely, although the trials are independent. Its opposite is the **hot-hand belief**: that a streak makes the same result more likely. For a fair coin both are wrong. They can be right only if the trials are not independent, for example if a player's form or the weather really changes the chances.

The **Law of Large Numbers** ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) says that the fraction of heads after $n$ flips gets close to $\frac{1}{2}$. It does **not** say that the number of heads minus the number of tails gets close to 0.

## Why It Is True

**No memory.** For independent flips, the multiplication rule gives $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$ ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)). So for the event "first five are heads" and the event "sixth is heads":

$$
P(X_6 = \text{H} \mid \text{first five are H}) = \frac{(\frac{1}{2})^6}{(\frac{1}{2})^5} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

The same idea gives the **memoryless** waiting times: if you wait for the first head, the number of extra flips you still need does not depend on how many tails you have already seen ([7.3 Geometric](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md), [9.3 Exponential](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)).

**Dilution, not correction.** Let $D_n$ be the number of heads minus the number of tails after $n$ flips. Suppose after the first $n_0$ flips we have $D_{n_0} = d$ (a lead for heads). For the next $m$ flips, each flip adds $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, so the later change $\Delta$ has $E[\Delta] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\Delta) = m$. Then:

$$
E[D_{n_0 + m}] = d + E[\Delta] = d, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(D_{n_0 + m}) = m
$$

**In words:** the lead $d$ is expected to stay, not to disappear. The typical size of the random change is $\sqrt{m}$, which can be much larger than $d$.

The share of heads is $\frac{n + D_n}{2n}$. Since $D_n$ stays of the size $\sqrt{n}$ and $n$ grows faster, the term $\frac{D_n}{2n}$ goes to 0. So the fraction goes to $\frac{1}{2}$ even though no flip "fixes" the early lead. The lead is just spread over more and more flips.

**Where memory is real.** If you draw from a **finite** pile without putting the items back, the pile changes after each draw. Then a streak does change the next chance (Example 2). The fallacy is not "the past never matters", it is "independent trials remember".

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Captain Who Lost Five Tosses

**Problem.** A fair coin is used for the toss. The captain has lost 5 tosses in a row. (a) What is the chance he wins the next toss? (b) What is the chance of a loss streak of 5 in the first place? (c) Over a season of 100 tosses, how likely is a run of at least 5 equal results (wins or losses)?

**Solution.**

1. (a) By independence, $P(\text{win next}) = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. (b) $P(5 \text{ losses in a row}) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^5 = \frac{1}{32} \approx 0.031$.
3. (c) Track the length of the current run of equal flips: it grows by one with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ and resets to 1 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. A calculation over 100 flips (done by the code below) gives $0.9717$.

**Answer.** (a) $\frac{1}{2}$. (b) About 3.1%. (c) About 97%.

**Check.** A given run of 5 is rare, but with 100 tosses there are many places for a run to start, so a run somewhere is almost certain. A "rare" streak is not a sign that the coin or the luck is broken.

### Example 2: Cards Have Memory, Coins Do Not

**Problem.** A deck has 26 red and 26 black cards. You draw cards one by one without putting them back, and the first 10 cards are all red. What is the chance that the next card is red? Compare with the chance of heads after 10 heads.

**Solution.**

1. After 10 red cards are removed, the deck has $52 - 10 = 42$ cards. Of these, $26 - 10 = 16$ are red and $26$ are black.
2. $P(\text{next is red}) = \frac{16}{42} \approx 0.381$.
3. For a fair coin, after 10 heads the next flip is heads with probability $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Answer.** For the deck, 38.1% (less than the 50% at the start): black cards **are** more likely now. For the coin, 50%.

**Check.** The deck loses red cards when they are drawn, so it "remembers". This is the idea behind counting cards. A coin, a die or a lottery machine that returns every ball to the same state before each draw does not remember. Ask yourself: **is the situation reset before each trial?**

### Example 3: When a Streak Means Something Else

**Problem.** A friend flips a coin 10 times and gets 10 heads. Before the flips, you thought there was a 1% chance that the coin is a trick coin with heads on both sides, and a 99% chance that it is fair. (Made-up numbers.) What do you believe now?

**Solution.**

1. Chance of 10 heads with a trick coin: $1$. With a fair coin: $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{10} = \frac{1}{1024} \approx 0.000977$.
2. Joint chances: trick coin and 10 heads: $0.01 \times 1 = 0.01$. Fair coin and 10 heads: $0.99 \times 0.000977 = 0.000967$.
3. By Bayes ([22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md)):

$$
P(\text{trick} \mid 10 \text{ heads}) = \frac{0.01}{0.01 + 0.000967} \approx 0.912
$$

**Answer.** About 91% that the coin is a trick coin. The right reaction to a very long streak is to suspect that the trials are **not** fair, not to expect a reversal.

**Check.** This is the opposite mistake from the gambler's fallacy: believing that a streak "must end" when the evidence says the rules are not what you thought. Five heads in a row is weak evidence (it happens with a fair coin 1 time in 32), but 10 or 20 in a row is strong evidence.

## Simulate It

The code checks three things: the next flip after 5 heads, the chance of a run of 5 equal flips in 100 flips (against the exact value from a short chain), and the "dilution, not correction" idea.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Coins have no memory: look at every flip that follows 5 heads in a row.
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=5_000_000)
after_streak = flips[:-5] + flips[1:-4] + flips[2:-3] + flips[3:-2] + flips[4:-1] == 5
print(f"After 5 heads, P(next is heads): {flips[5:][after_streak].mean():.4f}  (exact 0.5000)")

# 2. Streaks are common: P(5 equal flips in a row somewhere in 100 flips).
coins = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(200_000, 100))
same = coins[:, 1:] == coins[:, :-1]                         # flip equals the one before it
five_equal = same[:, :-3] & same[:, 1:-2] & same[:, 2:-1] & same[:, 3:]
state = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0, 0, 0]); hit = 0.0              # exact: chain on the current run length
for _ in range(99):
    hit += state[4] * 0.5
    state = np.array([0.0, state[1:5].sum() * 0.5, state[1] * 0.5, state[2] * 0.5, state[3] * 0.5])
print(f"P(run of 5+ in 100 flips): simulation {five_equal.any(axis=1).mean():.4f}, exact {hit:.4f}")

# 3. No correction, only dilution: start 15 heads and 5 tails (10 more heads than tails).
heads_later = rng.binomial(10_000, 0.5, size=200_000)
excess = 10 + 2 * heads_later - 10_000                          # heads minus tails, final
fraction = (15 + heads_later) / 10_020
print(f"Average excess of heads after 10,000 more flips: {excess.mean():.2f} (exact 10)")
print(f"Average share of heads: {fraction.mean():.5f} (exact {(10_020 + 10) / (2 * 10_020):.5f})")
```

Output:

```text
After 5 heads, P(next is heads): 0.5007  (exact 0.5000)
P(run of 5+ in 100 flips): simulation 0.9726, exact 0.9717
Average excess of heads after 10,000 more flips: 10.08 (exact 10)
Average share of heads: 0.50050 (exact 0.50050)
```

After 5 heads the next flip is still a fair flip. A run of 5 somewhere in 100 flips is almost certain (97%). And the early lead of 10 heads is **not** paid back: it is still 10 on average after 10,000 more flips. The share of heads gets close to $\frac{1}{2}$ only because the total number of flips is larger.

## Common Mistakes

- **"It has not happened for a long time, so it is due."** For independent trials, the chance in the next trial is the same as always. A number that has not appeared in a lottery for 30 draws is no more likely to appear next time.
- **"The Law of Large Numbers says it will balance out."** It says the **fraction** gets close to the true probability. The **difference** in counts can keep growing like $\sqrt{n}$.
- **Applying the fallacy to non-independent cases.** Drawing cards without replacement, or the weather on consecutive days, has memory. Check whether the trials are reset ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)).
- **Forgetting that streaks are normal.** A run of 5 or 6 in a long series is not strange. Do not look for a cause in every streak.
- **Overreacting the other way.** A very long streak (10 or more) can be real evidence that the coin, machine or process is not what you thought ([22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** when data are shuffled for training, samples are treated as independent draws. Not shuffling lets "streaks" of one class mislead a model, which is a real memory effect. Random seeds make runs repeatable but each draw still has no memory ([19.2](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.2-Pseudo-Random-Numbers-and-Seeds.md)).
- **Computer science:** a good random number generator must pass tests for runs and streaks. If a generator "corrected" itself after a streak, it would be easy to predict, and that would be a security hole ([21.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md)).
- **Games and gambling:** roulette, dice and lottery have no memory. The casino's edge does not depend on the last ten spins ([21.10](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).
- **Sport:** the toss of a coin does not owe a team anything. A batter's "good form" is a different question: it may change the chances, and then it needs data.
- **Everyday life:** a family with four girls has a chance of about $\frac{1}{2}$ of a girl next time. A bus that is late again is not "sure to come now". A dry spell does not make rain due.
- **Try this today:** the next time you think "it must happen soon", ask: "Is the situation reset every time, like a coin, or does it really change, like a deck of cards?" If it is reset, say "the chance is the same as always".

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A fair die shows 6 four times in a row. What is the chance of a 6 on the next roll?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rolls are independent, so the past does not matter:

$$P(\text{6 on the next roll}) = \frac{1}{6}$$

The chance of four 6s in a row was $\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^4 = \frac{1}{1296}$, but that is already done.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A family has 4 girls and no boys. Assuming each child is a girl or a boy with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, independently, what is the chance the fifth child is a girl?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is $\frac{1}{2}$. The first four children do not change the fifth (a simple model: in real data the chances are very close to one half but not exactly equal, and a few families may have a real tendency).

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A lottery draws 6 numbers out of 49 each week, with all balls returned between draws. Number 13 has not appeared in the last 30 draws. What is the chance that it appears next week? What was the chance that a given number is absent for 30 draws in a row?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each draw is a new start, so the chance for number 13 is the same as for any number: $\frac{6}{49} \approx 0.1224$.

The chance that a given number is absent in one draw is $\frac{43}{49}$, so for 30 independent draws:

$$\left(\frac{43}{49}\right)^{30} \approx 0.0199$$

That is about 2% for one chosen number. With 49 numbers, it is not strange that one of them has a long absence. That does not make it "due".

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A bag has 5 red and 5 blue marbles. You draw 3 marbles without putting them back, and all 3 are red. What is the chance that the next one is red?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

After 3 red marbles are removed: 2 red and 5 blue remain, 7 in total.

$$P(\text{next is red}) = \frac{2}{7} \approx 0.286$$

This is below $\frac{1}{2}$, because the bag has memory (no replacement).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $D_m$ be the number of heads minus the number of tails in $m$ fair flips. Show that the **share** $\frac{D_m}{m}$ goes to 0 in probability, even though $E[\lvert D_m \rvert]$ grows. Use Chebyshev's inequality.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each flip adds $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, so $E[D_m] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(D_m) = m$. Then $\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{D_m}{m}\right) = \frac{m}{m^2} = \frac{1}{m}$. By Chebyshev, for any $\varepsilon \gt 0$:

$$P\left( \left\lvert \frac{D_m}{m} \right\rvert \ge \varepsilon \right) \le \frac{1}{m \varepsilon^2} \to 0$$

So the share goes to 0. But the typical size of $D_m$ is $\sqrt{m}$, which grows, because $\operatorname{Var}(D_m) = m$. The counts do not balance, only the fractions do.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Estimate the chance of a run of 6 or more equal flips in 200 fair flips, and compare it with the exact value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_seasons, n_flips, run_length = 100_000, 200, 6

# One season = 200 fair flips. Look for 6 equal flips in a row (heads or tails).
coins = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(n_seasons, n_flips))
same = (coins[:, 1:] == coins[:, :-1])                        # flip equals the previous flip
found = np.zeros(n_seasons, dtype=bool)
for start in range(n_flips - run_length + 1):                 # 5 equal steps = 6 equal flips
    found |= same[:, start:start + run_length - 1].all(axis=1)

state = np.zeros(run_length); state[1] = 1.0; hit = 0.0       # exact: chain on the current run length
for _ in range(n_flips - 1):
    hit += state[run_length - 1] * 0.5
    state = np.concatenate(([0.0], [state[1:].sum() * 0.5], state[1:run_length - 1] * 0.5))
print(f"P(run of 6+ in 200 flips): simulation {found.mean():.4f}, exact {hit:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(run of 6+ in 200 flips): simulation 0.9664, exact 0.9653
```

So in 200 tosses, a run of 6 equal results is about 97% likely.

</details>

## Summary

- Independent trials have no memory. After any streak, the next trial has the same chances as before.
- The Law of Large Numbers works by dilution: the fraction goes to the true probability, but the difference in counts does not shrink.
- Streaks are common in long series. A run of 5 in 100 flips has chance 97%.
- Memory is real when the pile changes (cards without replacement) or when the process itself changes. Ask: "is it reset each time?"
- A very long streak is a reason to doubt the model (maybe the coin is not fair), not a reason to expect a reversal.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X_{n+1} = \text{H} \mid X_1 = \dots = X_n = \text{H}) = P(X_{n+1} = \text{H})}
$$
