# 22.8 Correlation vs Causation

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.8 Correlation vs Causation

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [22.7 Law of Small Numbers](22.7-Law-of-Small-Numbers.md). You need [11.2 Correlation Coefficient](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) and [4.9 Conditional Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.9-Conditional-Independence.md).

> **Big idea:** Two things that move together are not necessarily linked by cause and effect, because a third thing may drive both, so ask "what else could explain this?" before you believe a headline.

---

## Why It Matters

"People who drink green tea live longer." "Children with more books at home get better marks." "Cities with more police have more crime." Each sentence describes a **correlation**: two things that tend to go up and down together. Each invites a cause: green tea, books, police. But a correlation alone does not say which way the cause goes, or whether there is a cause at all. [Module 11](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) defines correlation and shows what it measures. This file shows the four common reasons why two things are correlated, and how a fair experiment tells them apart.

## Intuition

In many places, ice-cream sales and swimming accidents rise and fall together over the year. If you looked only at the numbers, you might say: "Ice cream makes people drown", or "Stop selling ice cream to save lives." Of course not. Hot weather makes people buy ice cream, and it also makes more people go swimming. The weather is a **hidden common cause**, called a **confounder**.

Now try a harder case. Students who join a tutoring program have lower marks than those who do not. Does tutoring hurt? Not necessarily. Weak students are much more likely to join, so the tutored group starts lower. Here the confounder (weakness) hides a real benefit.

There are four main reasons why $X$ and $Y$ are correlated. Keep this list in your head: (A) $X$ causes $Y$; (B) $Y$ causes $X$ (reverse cause); (C) a third thing $Z$ causes both; (D) it is a coincidence, which is common when you look at many pairs in a small data set.

## Picture

```text
   A. X causes Y        B. Y causes X        C. A hidden Z drives both
                                                       Z
     X -------> Y         X <------- Y                / \
                                                     v   v
   more sleep,          stress,                      X   Y
   better marks         poor sleep
                                              hot weather: ice cream (X),
   D. Coincidence: X and Y have no link,      swimming accidents (Y)
      the numbers just happened to agree.
```

Only picture A means "change $X$ and $Y$ will change". In B, C and D, changing $X$ does nothing to $Y$.

## Definition

The **correlation** $\rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$ ([11.2](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md)) measures how well a straight line describes the way $X$ and $Y$ move together. It is a number from $-1$ to $1$. It is about **seeing** the data. It says nothing about what would happen if we **changed** $X$.

A **causal effect** answers a different question: "If I set $X$ to a new value, what happens to $Y$?" Write this as $P(Y \mid \text{do}(X = x))$, where "do" means we force the value of $X$, which is different from $P(Y \mid X = x)$, where we only see that $X = x$.

A **confounder** is a variable $Z$ that influences both $X$ and $Y$. When we look only at $X$ and $Y$, the confounder creates a correlation that is not causal:

$$
\rho_{XY} \ne 0 \quad \text{even though} \quad X \perp Y \mid Z
$$

**In words:** $X$ and $Y$ can be correlated overall and still be independent once $Z$ is held fixed ([4.9](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.9-Conditional-Independence.md)). If you compare cases with the same value of $Z$, the link disappears.

A **randomized experiment** sets $X$ by a coin flip (or a random number), so $X$ cannot depend on $Z$ or on anything else. Then any difference in $Y$ between the groups is a causal effect.

## Why It Is True

Take a simple model: $Z$ has variance $\sigma_Z^2$, and $X = aZ + \varepsilon_1$ and $Y = bZ + \varepsilon_2$, where the noise terms $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$ and $Z$ are independent with variances $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$. There is **no** arrow from $X$ to $Y$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) &= \operatorname{Cov}(aZ + \varepsilon_1, bZ + \varepsilon_2) = ab \thinspace \sigma_Z^2 && \text{(only } Z \text{ is shared)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= a^2 \sigma_Z^2 + s_1^2, \quad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = b^2 \sigma_Z^2 + s_2^2 && \text{(independent terms)} \\
\rho_{XY} &= \frac{ab \thinspace \sigma_Z^2}{\sqrt{(a^2 \sigma_Z^2 + s_1^2)(b^2 \sigma_Z^2 + s_2^2)}} && \text{(definition)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For $a = b = 1$ and all variances equal to 1, this is $\rho = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \times 2}} = \frac{1}{2}$: a clear correlation with no cause between $X$ and $Y$.

Now hold $Z$ fixed. Given $Z = z$, we have $X = az + \varepsilon_1$ and $Y = bz + \varepsilon_2$, and the two noise terms are independent. So $X$ and $Y$ are independent given $Z$, and the correlation among cases with the same $z$ is 0. This is the idea of **controlling for** a confounder.

Finally, a randomized experiment replaces $X = aZ + \varepsilon_1$ by a value chosen by a coin, independent of $Z$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ in this model, which is the truth: $X$ has no effect on $Y$. If $X$ did have an effect, the experiment would show it, because no hidden $Z$ can line up with the coin.

**Coincidence (D).** If you check many pairs in a small data set, some will look related by luck. For $m$ unrelated variables there are $\binom{m}{2}$ pairs, and each has a small chance of a large $\lvert r \rvert$. The chance that at least one is large grows quickly with the number of pairs ([22.12](22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

> **Warning:** Controlling for $Z$ works only for the confounders you know and measure. A hidden one can still fool you. Only randomization protects against all of them.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Correlation From a Hidden Cause

**Problem.** Let $Z$ (heat) have variance 1. Ice-cream sales $X = Z + \varepsilon_1$ and swimming accidents $Y = Z + \varepsilon_2$, with noise variance 1 each, all independent. Find the correlation of $X$ and $Y$. Then find it among days with the same heat, and in a trial where $X$ is set by a coin.

**Solution.**

1. $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Var}(Z) = 1$. $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 1 + 1 = 2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 2$.
2. $\rho = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \times 2}} = 0.5$.
3. For days with the same heat $Z = z$: $X = z + \varepsilon_1$ and $Y = z + \varepsilon_2$ are independent, so the correlation is 0.
4. If a coin sets $X$ (independent of $Z$), then $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Z + \varepsilon_2) = 0$, so the correlation is 0.

**Answer.** 0.5 overall, 0 for days of equal heat, and 0 when $X$ is set at random.

**Check.** The simulation gives $0.5006$, $-0.0002$ and $-0.0009$. The correlation is real, but selling less ice cream would not prevent any accident.

### Example 2: Tutoring Seems to Hurt

**Problem.** A class has two equal halves. Weak students have a base mark of 50 and strong students 70. Tutoring truly adds 5 marks to anyone. A weak student joins tutoring with probability 0.8, and a strong student with probability 0.2. (Made-up numbers.) Compare the average marks of tutored and non-tutored students. Then compare inside each group.

**Solution.**

1. Tutored: share of weak students among them $= \frac{0.5 \times 0.8}{0.5 \times 0.8 + 0.5 \times 0.2} = \frac{0.4}{0.5} = 0.8$. Average base mark $= 0.8 \times 50 + 0.2 \times 70 = 54$. With the 5 marks added: $59$.
2. Not tutored: share of weak $= \frac{0.5 \times 0.2}{0.5 \times 0.2 + 0.5 \times 0.8} = \frac{0.1}{0.5} = 0.2$. Average mark $= 0.2 \times 50 + 0.8 \times 70 = 66$.
3. The overall gap: $59 - 66 = -7$.
4. Inside the weak group: tutored $55$, not tutored $50$, gap $+5$. Inside the strong group: $75$ and $70$, gap $+5$.

**Answer.** Overall, tutored students score 7 marks lower, although tutoring adds 5 marks to everyone. Inside each group, the gap is $+5$.

**Check.** The strength of the student is a confounder: it affects who is tutored and the marks. If you must compare groups that were not randomized, compare people who are alike on the confounders. With a coin deciding who is tutored, the overall gap is $+5$ (Practice problem 4 and 6).

### Example 3: A Correlation Hunt in Noise

**Problem.** You record 10 unrelated numbers for 12 days, for example your sleep, coffee, steps, screen time, rain, bus delay, and so on. The truth is that none of them depends on the others. You look at all $\binom{10}{2} = 45$ pairs. How likely is it that at least one pair shows $\lvert r \rvert \gt 0.6$?

**Solution.**

1. For one pair of unrelated variables and 12 days, the statistic $t = \frac{r \sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}}$ follows a $t$ distribution with 10 degrees of freedom (here only used as a fact; see [9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)). For $r = 0.6$: $t = \frac{0.6 \times 3.162}{0.8} = 2.372$.
2. The chance of $\lvert r \rvert \gt 0.6$ for one pair is $2 \times P(T_{10} \gt 2.372) = 0.0392$.
3. If the 45 pairs were independent, the chance of at least one hit would be $1 - (1 - 0.0392)^{45} = 1 - 0.165 = 0.835$. (They are not exactly independent, so this is only close.)

**Answer.** About 83% to 84%. Looking at 45 pairs in a tiny data set, you will most likely "find" a correlation of 0.6 or more, in pure noise.

**Check.** The simulation gives $0.8416$. A correlation found by searching is not evidence. Test it on new data, or decide what to test before you look.

## Simulate It

The code runs three checks: a hidden common cause, the tutoring trap, and a correlation hunt in pure noise.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import t as t_dist

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# 1. Hidden cause Z (hot weather). X and Y both follow Z, and X does NOT cause Y.
z = rng.normal(size=n)
x = z + rng.normal(size=n)               # ice-cream sales
y = z + rng.normal(size=n)               # swimming accidents
x_set = rng.normal(size=n)               # an experiment: we set X by coin, ignoring Z
y_exp = z + rng.normal(size=n)
resid = lambda a: a - np.polyval(np.polyfit(z, a, 1), z)    # remove the part explained by Z
print(f"corr(X, Y) = {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]:.4f}  (exact 0.5000)")
print(f"corr(X, Y) after removing Z = {np.corrcoef(resid(x), resid(y))[0, 1]:+.4f}  (exact 0)")
print(f"corr when X is set at random = {np.corrcoef(x_set, y_exp)[0, 1]:+.4f}  (exact 0)")

# 2. Weak students choose tutoring; tutoring truly adds 5 marks.
weak = rng.random(n) < 0.5
tutored = rng.random(n) < np.where(weak, 0.8, 0.2)
score = np.where(weak, 50, 70) + 5 * tutored
print(f"Average score: tutored {score[tutored].mean():.1f}, not tutored {score[~tutored].mean():.1f}")
gain = [score[tutored & (weak == w)].mean() - score[~tutored & (weak == w)].mean() for w in (True, False)]
print(f"Within weak / within strong students, tutoring gain: {gain[0]:.1f} / {gain[1]:.1f}")

# 3. Pure noise: 10 unrelated variables, 12 observations each. Is some pair "correlated"?
data = rng.normal(size=(100_000, 12, 10))
best = np.empty(100_000)
for i in range(100_000):
    c = np.corrcoef(data[i].T)
    best[i] = np.abs(c[np.triu_indices(10, 1)]).max()
t_value = 0.6 * np.sqrt(10) / np.sqrt(1 - 0.36)
print(f"One pair, |r| above 0.6: exact {2 * t_dist.sf(t_value, 10):.4f}")
print(f"Some pair among 45 has |r| above 0.6: simulation {np.mean(best > 0.6):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
corr(X, Y) = 0.5006  (exact 0.5000)
corr(X, Y) after removing Z = -0.0002  (exact 0)
corr when X is set at random = -0.0009  (exact 0)
Average score: tutored 59.0, not tutored 66.0
Within weak / within strong students, tutoring gain: 5.0 / 5.0
One pair, |r| above 0.6: exact 0.0392
Some pair among 45 has |r| above 0.6: simulation 0.8416
```

The first three lines show a correlation of 0.5 from a hidden cause that vanishes when we remove $Z$ or set $X$ at random. The tutoring lines show a hidden cause that reverses the sign of the gap. The last line shows that a search through 45 pairs of pure noise finds a "strong" correlation about 84% of the time.

## Common Mistakes

- **"A correlates with B, so A causes B."** Always list the other three stories: B causes A, a hidden Z causes both, or it is a coincidence. Ask which is the most reasonable and what data would separate them.
- **"No correlation means no cause."** A real cause can be hidden by a confounder (Example 2), or be non-linear (a medicine that helps at a medium dose and hurts at a high dose has a correlation near 0). Also see [11.4](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md).
- **Thinking that controlling for variables always helps.** Adjusting for the wrong variable (one that is an effect of both, or a step between $X$ and $Y$) can create a false link. Think about the causal story before you adjust.
- **Mixing up groups and individuals.** A correlation in a group does not mean that every person follows the pattern.
- **Forgetting that searching creates coincidences.** The more comparisons you make, the more false "findings" you get (Example 3). Decide your question first.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a model that predicts well from a correlation (for example "snow in the photo means husky") can fail when the hidden cause changes. Causal thinking is a main topic in research on robust AI. Feature importance is not the same as causal effect ([21.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Computer science:** an A/B test randomizes users, so that a change in the product can be called the cause of a change in clicks. Without randomization, the users who see the new feature may be different users.
- **Medicine and public health:** randomized trials exist to break the link between who takes a treatment and who is healthy to start with. (This file is not medical advice.)
- **Economics and policy:** "Cities with more police have more crime" may mean that police are sent where crime is high (reverse cause).
- **Everyday life:** headlines, advertising and advice from friends. "Successful people wake up at 5 am" does not show that waking at 5 makes you successful.
- **Try this today:** for the next headline "X is linked to Y", write the four stories A, B, C and D. Then ask: "Was X assigned at random? How big was the sample? How many things did they test?"

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** "Cities with more fire engines have more fire damage." Give a hidden cause that explains this without engines causing damage.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The size of the fire (or the size of the city). Big fires bring more fire engines and cause more damage. Bigger cities have more engines and more buildings that can burn. The fire engines are sent because of the damage (reverse cause), and they do not cause it.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $X = 2Z + \varepsilon_1$ and $Y = Z + \varepsilon_2$, with $Z, \varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$ independent and each with variance 1. Find $\rho_{XY}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 2 \times 1 \times 1 = 2$. $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4 + 1 = 5$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 1 + 1 = 2$.

$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5 \times 2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.632$$

There is still no arrow from $X$ to $Y$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A study finds that people who own a smartwatch have a lower resting heart rate. Give one explanation of each type: reverse cause, hidden cause, coincidence.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Reverse cause: people who already exercise a lot (and so have a low heart rate) are more likely to buy a smartwatch. Hidden cause: income or interest in fitness leads to both owning a watch and being fit. Coincidence: in a small study with many measured traits, one trait may look linked just by chance. The simplest way to test the cause is a trial that gives watches to random people. Only then can the watch be called the cause of any change.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Use the setting of Example 2, with weak students joining tutoring with probability 0.9 and strong ones with probability 0.1. Find the gap in average marks (tutored minus not tutored). Then find it if a coin ($p = 0.5$ for everyone) decides who is tutored.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Students choose: tutored weak share $= \frac{0.5 \times 0.9}{0.5} = 0.9$, so base mark $0.9 \times 50 + 0.1 \times 70 = 52$ and mark $57$. Not tutored: weak share $= \frac{0.5 \times 0.1}{0.5} = 0.1$, so $0.1 \times 50 + 0.9 \times 70 = 68$. The gap is $57 - 68 = -11$.

Coin decides: both groups have 50% weak students, so both have base mark 60. The tutored mark is 65 and the other is 60, so the gap is $+5$, the true effect.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** In the model $X = aZ + \varepsilon_1$, $Y = bZ + \varepsilon_2$ (independent $Z, \varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$, variances $\sigma_Z^2, s_1^2, s_2^2$), show that $\rho_{XY} = 0$ if and only if $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. What does this say about hidden causes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From the derivation, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = ab \thinspace \sigma_Z^2$. Since $\sigma_Z^2 \gt 0$ and the denominator of $\rho$ is positive, $\rho_{XY} = 0$ exactly when $ab = 0$, that is, when $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.

So a hidden cause creates a correlation as soon as it affects **both** variables, however weakly. The sign of $\rho$ is the sign of $ab$: if $Z$ pushes $X$ up and $Y$ down, the correlation is negative. The size of the correlation says nothing about whether it is causal.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check problem 4 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000
weak = rng.random(n) < 0.5

for name, p_weak, p_strong in [("students choose", 0.9, 0.1), ("coin decides", 0.5, 0.5)]:
    tutored = rng.random(n) < np.where(weak, p_weak, p_strong)
    score = np.where(weak, 50, 70) + 5 * tutored             # tutoring really adds 5 marks
    gap = score[tutored].mean() - score[~tutored].mean()
    print(f"{name:>15}: tutored minus not tutored = {gap:+.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
students choose: tutored minus not tutored = -11.00
   coin decides: tutored minus not tutored = +5.03
```

The exact values are $-11$ and $+5$.

</details>

## Summary

- Correlation describes what we **see**. Causation describes what happens when we **change** something.
- Four stories for a correlation: $X$ causes $Y$, $Y$ causes $X$, a hidden $Z$ causes both, or coincidence.
- A hidden cause can create a correlation (ice cream and swimming accidents) or hide a real effect (tutoring).
- Comparing people who are alike on $Z$ removes the effect of a known confounder. Random assignment removes all of them.
- A correlation found by searching many pairs in a small data set is weak evidence.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\rho_{XY} = \frac{ab \thinspace \sigma_Z^2}{\sqrt{(a^2 \sigma_Z^2 + s_1^2)(b^2 \sigma_Z^2 + s_2^2)}} \ne 0 \quad \text{with no arrow from } X \text{ to } Y}
$$
