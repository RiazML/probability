# 22.7 Law of Small Numbers

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.7 Law of Small Numbers

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [22.6 Regression to the Mean](22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md). You need [7.2 Binomial](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md) and [17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md).

> **Big idea:** Small samples swing wildly, so the best and the worst results almost always come from the smallest groups, and a few cases tell you very little.

---

## Why It Matters

"Three of my four friends got the flu after the shot." "This new batter scored two fifties in three matches." "The best schools in the country are all small schools." Each of these uses a small sample as if it were a big one. The **law of small numbers** is a joke name for this mistake: people treat a small sample as if the Law of Large Numbers already worked for it. The real law ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) needs many cases. This file shows how big the swings are, how to read a sample size, and what to do when you only have a little data.

## Intuition

Two hospitals record births. The big one has about 45 births a day and the small one about 15. In both, a baby is a boy or a girl with equal chance. On some days, more than 60% of the babies are boys. Which hospital has more of these "boy days"?

Many people say "about the same". The answer is: the small hospital, by far. With 15 babies, a day with 10 or more boys happens on 15% of the days. With 45 babies, a day with 28 or more boys happens on only 6.8% of the days. A small number of births swings far from the 50-50 average. A large number of births stays close.

The same idea explains why the best and the worst schools, shops or players in a list are often the small ones. A small school's average is made from few students, so luck moves it a lot. It is not that small schools are special. They are just noisier.


## Definition

Suppose each case is a success with probability $p$, independently, and you look at $n$ cases. The **sample proportion** is $\hat{p} = \frac{\text{number of successes}}{n}$. Its mean and its standard deviation are:

$$
E[\hat{p}] = p, \qquad \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

**In words:** the sample proportion is correct on average, but its typical error shrinks only like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. The size of this typical error is called the **standard error**.

A quick **margin of error** for about 95% of samples is:

$$
\text{margin} \approx 1.96 \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \le \frac{0.98}{\sqrt{n}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

**In words:** with $n$ people, a sample proportion is usually within $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ of the truth. With 100 people, within about 10 points. With 10,000 people, within about 1 point. (The largest value of $p(1-p)$ is $\frac{1}{4}$, at $p = \frac{1}{2}$, which gives the upper bound.)

## Why It Is True

The number of successes is binomial, $S \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ ([7.2](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md)), with $E[S] = np$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S) = np(1-p)$. Dividing by $n$ and using $\operatorname{Var}(aS) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(S)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[\hat{p}] &= \frac{np}{n} = p && \text{(linearity)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot np(1-p) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n} && \text{(} a = \tfrac{1}{n} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the standard deviation is $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$, and it shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. For a normal-shaped $\hat{p}$ (the Central Limit Theorem, [17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)), about 95% of the values are within 1.96 standard deviations of $p$, which gives the margin of error above.

**Why extremes come from small groups.** Look at a list of many groups with the same $p$ but different sizes. The observed rate of a group with $n$ cases is spread with standard deviation $\sigma_n \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. The highest and the lowest values in a list come from the groups with the widest spread, which are the smallest groups. This has nothing to do with the groups being different. It is the same effect as regression to the mean ([22.6](22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md)): extreme observed results contain a lot of luck.

> **Note:** The formulas need the cases to be independent and to come from the same population. A sample of your friends, or of people who chose to answer a survey, may be biased in a way that more people would not fix.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Hospitals

**Problem.** Boys and girls are equally likely. Find the chance that more than 60% of the babies in a day are boys, for a hospital with 15 births and a hospital with 45 births per day.

**Solution.**

1. For 15 births, "more than 60%" means more than 9 boys, so 10 or more. Let $B \sim \text{Bin}(15, 0.5)$. Then $P(B \le 9) = 0.8491$, so $P(B \ge 10) = 1 - 0.8491 = 0.1509$.
2. For 45 births, "more than 60%" means more than 27 boys, so 28 or more. With $B \sim \text{Bin}(45, 0.5)$, $P(B \ge 28) = 0.0676$.
3. The standard deviations of the share of boys are $\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{15}} = 0.129$ and $\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{45}} = 0.0745$.

**Answer.** About 15.1% of the days for the small hospital, and 6.8% for the big hospital: more than twice as often.

**Check.** The standard deviation for 45 births is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = 0.577$ times that for 15 births. The simulation below gives $0.1508$ and $0.0674$.

### Example 2: A New Batter

**Problem.** In a league, 25% of innings give a score of 50 or more (a "fifty"). A new batter has 2 fifties in his first 3 innings (67%). A veteran has 50 fifties in 200 innings (25%). Which is better? Give a sensible estimate for the new batter. (Made-up numbers.)

**Solution.**

1. The margin of error for 3 innings, using the observed 0.67, is $1.96 \sqrt{\frac{0.67 \times 0.33}{3}} = 1.96 \times 0.2715 = 0.53$. So the true rate could be anywhere from about 14% to 100%.
2. A simple fix is to **shrink** the estimate toward the league rate by adding imaginary innings. Add 20 imaginary innings at the league rate: 5 fifties in 20 innings. The new batter becomes $\frac{2 + 5}{3 + 20} = \frac{7}{23} \approx 0.304$.
3. For the veteran: $\frac{50 + 5}{200 + 20} = \frac{55}{220} = 0.25$. He hardly moves.

**Answer.** The new batter's estimate is about 30%, not 67%. The veteran's is 25%. The new batter might be better, but three innings are too few to say.

**Check.** This rule is the posterior mean of a Beta distribution with 5 successes and 15 failures as the prior ([9.5 Beta](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md), [22.2](22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md)). The number 20 says how much you trust the league rate. It is a choice, not a law. The more innings the batter plays, the less the imaginary ones matter: after 100 real innings with 40 fifties, the estimate is $\frac{45}{120} = 0.375$.

### Example 3: How Many People Do You Need?

**Problem.** You ask 20 classmates if they like a new canteen dish and 11 say yes (55%). What is the margin of error? How many people would you need for a margin of 5 points?

**Solution.**

1. Margin: $1.96 \sqrt{\frac{0.55 \times 0.45}{20}} = 1.96 \times 0.1112 = 0.218$. So the "55%" could easily be anything from about 33% to 77%.
2. For a margin of 0.05 at worst case $p = 0.5$: $1.96 \times \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{n}} = 0.05$, so $\sqrt{n} = \frac{0.98}{0.05} = 19.6$ and $n = 384.2$. You need about 385 people.

**Answer.** The margin is about 22 points with 20 people. For 5 points you need about 385 people, if they are chosen at random.

**Check.** To cut the margin by a factor of 4.4 (from 22 to 5 points), the sample must be about 19 times larger (from 20 to 385). Because of the $\sqrt{n}$ law, each extra bit of precision costs more than the last one.

## Simulate It

The code checks the hospital example and then creates 15,000 schools that all have a true pass rate of 0.60, in three sizes. We see who ends up at the top and at the bottom of the list.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# 1. Two hospitals, boys and girls equally likely. A "boy day" has more than 60% boys.
n_days = 1_000_000
for births in [15, 45]:
    boys = rng.binomial(births, 0.5, size=n_days)
    exact = binom.sf(int(0.6 * births), births, 0.5)           # P(boys > 60% of births)
    print(f"{births:>2} births a day: boy days {np.mean(boys > 0.6 * births):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

# 2. 15,000 schools whose true pass rate is 0.60 for every school. Sizes: 20, 100 or 500.
size = np.repeat([20, 100, 500], 5_000)
rate = rng.binomial(size, 0.60) / size
for s in [20, 100, 500]:
    print(f"size {s:>3}: SD of observed rate {rate[size == s].std():.4f}, exact {np.sqrt(0.6 * 0.4 / s):.4f}")
order = np.argsort(rate)
for name, picked in [("worst 50", size[order[:50]]), ("best 50", size[order[-50:]])]:
    print(f"{name}: {np.sum(picked == 20)} of 50 are the small schools (size 20)")
```

Output:

```text
15 births a day: boy days 0.1508, exact 0.1509
45 births a day: boy days 0.0674, exact 0.0676
size  20: SD of observed rate 0.1075, exact 0.1095
size 100: SD of observed rate 0.0484, exact 0.0490
size 500: SD of observed rate 0.0223, exact 0.0219
worst 50: 50 of 50 are the small schools (size 20)
best 50: 50 of 50 are the small schools (size 20)
```

All 15,000 schools are exactly equal in quality, yet the 50 best and the 50 worst on the list are all small schools. A ranking by raw averages mostly measures the size of the school.

## Common Mistakes

- **Trusting a few cases.** "Three out of four" is not 75%. It is "somewhere between 20% and 100%". Always ask "out of how many?".
- **Ranking groups by raw averages when the sizes differ.** The top and the bottom of the list will be full of small groups. Compare groups of the same size, or shrink the small ones toward the average first (Example 2).
- **Seeing a cause in a small-sample swing.** A small school with great results may have no special method. Look for the same result in the next year and in larger samples.
- **Believing that a small sample must "look like" the population.** Ten coin flips do not have to be 5 heads and 5 tails. A short sequence can be far from the average and still be fair ([22.5](22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md)).
- **Thinking that a larger sample fixes everything.** More cases remove noise, not bias. A large survey of people who chose to answer can still be wrong ([22.10](22.10-Survivorship-Bias.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a model tested on 20 examples can seem to be 90% accurate by luck. The test set must be big enough, and a very small class in the data gets noisy estimates ([21.1.6](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)).
- **Computer science:** a benchmark that runs a program 3 times has a large margin of error. Run it many times and report the spread. Randomized algorithms use many repeats to make an answer reliable ([21.3.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.1-Randomized-Algorithms.md)).
- **Statistics:** polls, surveys and A/B tests report a margin of error, which is exactly $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ in the quick form ([21.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Sport:** after 3 matches a player's average means little. After 100 it means a lot. Rankings early in a season mostly show luck.
- **Everyday life:** one bad meal at a restaurant, one friend's story, a product with five reviews. A birthday coincidence also feels rare, but with enough people it is common ([2.11.1](../02-COUNTING/2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md)).
- **Try this today:** when you read "X% of people ...", find the sample size. Compute $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ in your head. If the margin is larger than the difference you care about, treat the result as "no information yet".

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A fair coin is flipped 10 times, and separately 100 times. Find the chance of at least 80% heads in each case. (Use a calculator or code for the 100-flip case.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For 10 flips we need 8 or more heads:

$$P(\text{at least 8 heads}) = \frac{45 + 10 + 1}{1024} = \frac{56}{1024} \approx 0.0547$$

For 100 flips we need 80 or more heads, and the chance is about $5.6 \times 10^{-10}$, which is practically 0. A short run can show 80% heads easily. A long run almost never can.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A poll asks 400 randomly chosen people. What is the quick margin of error $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{400}} = \frac{1}{20} = 0.05$$

About 5 percentage points. (The more exact worst-case value is $\frac{0.98}{20} = 0.049$.)

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Four of your five customers like a new product (80%). Find the 95% margin of error. How many customers do you need for a margin of 5 points if you expect about 80%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Margin for $n = 5$: $1.96 \sqrt{\frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{5}} = 1.96 \times 0.1789 = 0.35$. So "80%" could be anywhere from about 45% to 100%.

For a margin of 0.05 with $p = 0.8$: $1.96 \sqrt{\frac{0.16}{n}} = 0.05$, so $\sqrt{n} = \frac{1.96 \times 0.4}{0.05} = 15.68$ and $n = 245.9$, about 246 customers.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** The league rate of fifties is 30%. Use the shrinking rule with 10 imaginary innings at the league rate. A new batter has 3 fifties in 4 innings. What is the estimate?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The imaginary innings are $10 \times 0.3 = 3$ fifties in 10 innings. So:

$$\frac{3 + 3}{4 + 10} = \frac{6}{14} \approx 0.429$$

The raw 75% falls to about 43%.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$ is at most $\frac{1}{2}$, and use it to find how many people $n$ guarantee that the standard deviation of $\hat{p}$ is at most 0.01, whatever $p$ is. By what factor must $n$ grow to cut the standard deviation in half?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$p(1-p) = \frac{1}{4} - \left(p - \frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \le \frac{1}{4}$, so $\sqrt{p(1-p)} \le \frac{1}{2}$, with equality at $p = \frac{1}{2}$.

So $\sigma_{\hat{p}} \le \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}$. We need $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}} \le 0.01$, so $\sqrt{n} \ge 50$ and $n \ge 2500$.

To halve $\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}$ we need $\sqrt{n}$ to double, so $n$ must be 4 times larger.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Half of all people like a dish. Find the chance that a poll of 20 people shows 60% or more, or 40% or less, liking it. Do the same for a poll of 200 people.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_polls = 1_000_000
for n_people in [20, 200]:
    # Half of all people like the dish. Each poll asks n_people at random.
    likes = rng.binomial(n_people, 0.5, size=n_polls)
    high, low = round(0.60 * n_people), round(0.40 * n_people)     # poll shows 60% or more, or 40% or less
    far = (likes >= high) | (likes <= low)
    exact = binom.sf(high - 1, n_people, 0.5) + binom.cdf(low, n_people, 0.5)
    print(f"{n_people:>3} people: P(poll shows 60%+ or 40%-) simulation {far.mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
 20 people: P(poll shows 60%+ or 40%-) simulation 0.5035, exact 0.5034
200 people: P(poll shows 60%+ or 40%-) simulation 0.0057, exact 0.0057
```

With 20 people, half of the polls are at least 10 points away from the truth. With 200 people, only about 1 in 175.

</details>

## Summary

- The sample proportion has standard deviation $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$, so small samples swing a lot.
- The quick margin of error is about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. To halve it, use 4 times more data.
- In a list of groups of different sizes, the best and the worst are usually the smallest groups.
- With little data, shrink the estimate toward the overall average instead of trusting the raw number.
- A bigger sample removes noise, but not bias.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \le \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}}
$$
