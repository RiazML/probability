# 22.6 Regression to the Mean

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 22: Probability for Life](README.md) / 22.6 Regression to the Mean

**Level:** Uses (Life) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [22.5 Gambler's Fallacy](22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md). You need [11.2 Correlation Coefficient](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) and [12.7 Conditionals](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.7-Conditionals.md).

> **Big idea:** An extreme result is usually a mix of real skill and unusual luck, so the next result is expected to be less extreme, even when nothing has changed.

---

## Why It Matters

The worst students get extra lessons and improve. The best salesperson of the month does worse next month. A patient joins a study when the pain is at its worst and feels better after a week. In each case people look for a cause: the lessons, the pressure, the medicine. Often the only cause is **regression to the mean**: a result chosen because it was extreme tends to be followed by a result closer to the average. Modules [11](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md) and [12](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.10-Bivariate-Normal.md) prove the formula. This file shows how to use it to avoid wrong stories about praise, punishment, treatments and rankings.

## Intuition

A class takes a first test. The marks are not only skill. They also contain luck: a good guess, a topic you happened to revise, a headache, a noisy room. Suppose Rafi scores 80 when the class average is 60. Some of his 80 is skill and some is good luck. On the second test, his skill is still there, but luck is new and random, so on average it is zero. He expects to score less than 80, say 73, while still being above average.

Now look at the weakest student, who scored 40. Part of that was bad luck. On the second test the luck is new, so she expects to do better, say 47, and still below average. Nobody taught her anything. She did not "try harder". Her score moved toward the average because her bad luck did not repeat.

The same thing is true backwards in time. The students with the very best marks on test 2 were, on average, **less** extreme on test 1. So regression to the mean is not a force that pulls things over time. It is a fact about selecting extremes from noisy measurements.

## Picture

```text
   test 1 score        expected test 2 score
        80  ------------>  72.8     (moves down 7.2)
        70  ------------>  66.4
        60  ------------>  60.0     <- class mean: stays
        50  ------------>  53.6
        40  ------------>  47.2     (moves up 7.2)

   Each score moves 36% of the way back to the mean (here the
   correlation between the two tests is 0.64, so 1 - 0.64 = 0.36).
```

The expected second score is always between the first score and the mean. The farther from the mean you start, the larger the move.

## Definition

Let two measurements $X_1$ and $X_2$ of the same kind of thing (two tests, two seasons, two readings) have the same mean $\mu$ and the same standard deviation $\sigma$, with correlation $\rho$ ([11.2](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.2-Correlation-Coefficient.md)). For jointly normal variables ([12.10](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.10-Bivariate-Normal.md)), the best guess of the second measurement is:

$$
E[X_2 \mid X_1 = x] = \mu + \rho \thinspace (x - \mu)
$$

**In words:** start from the mean and add only the fraction $\rho$ of how far the first result was from the mean. When $\rho$ is less than 1, the guess is closer to the mean than $x$ is. This pull toward the average is called **regression to the mean**.

A useful model is **score = skill + luck**. Write $X_i = T + \varepsilon_i$, where $T$ is the true skill (the same in both tests) and $\varepsilon_i$ is luck (new each time, with mean 0, independent of $T$). Then:

$$
\rho = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(T)}{\operatorname{Var}(T) + \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)}
$$

**In words:** $\rho$, the **reliability** of a measurement, is the share of the spread in the scores that comes from real differences in skill. The more luck there is, the lower $\rho$ is and the stronger the pull to the mean.

## Why It Is True

Take $X_i = T + \varepsilon_i$ with $\operatorname{Var}(T) = \sigma_T^2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma_{\varepsilon}^2$. Since $T$ and the luck terms are independent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X_1) &= \sigma_T^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon}^2 && \text{(variance of a sum of independent terms)} \\
\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) &= \operatorname{Cov}(T + \varepsilon_1, T + \varepsilon_2) = \sigma_T^2 && \text{(only } T \text{ is shared)} \\
\rho &= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_1) \operatorname{Var}(X_2)}} = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon}^2} && \text{(definition of correlation)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The best straight-line guess of $X_2$ from $X_1$ has slope $\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)}{\operatorname{Var}(X_1)} = \rho$, because the two variances are equal ([14.5](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)). When $T$ and the luck terms are normal, this line is exactly the conditional mean ([12.7](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.7-Conditionals.md)):

$$
E[X_2 \mid X_1 = x] = \mu + \rho (x - \mu)
$$

There is also a two-step view. Given $X_1 = x$, your best guess of the skill $T$ is $\mu + \rho(x - \mu)$ (the observed score is **shrunk** toward the mean, because part of it is luck). The luck in test 2 has mean 0. So the best guess of $X_2$ equals the best guess of $T$.

Two special cases: if there is no luck ($\rho = 1$) the second score equals the first and nothing regresses. If it is all luck ($\rho = 0$), the best guess is always the mean, whatever the first score was.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Tests

**Problem.** Scores on each test have mean 60. Skill has standard deviation 8 and luck has standard deviation 6, independent, on both tests. (Made-up numbers.) Find $\rho$. What do you expect on test 2 from a student who scored 80, and from a student who scored 40?

**Solution.**

1. Variances: $\sigma_T^2 = 64$ and $\sigma_{\varepsilon}^2 = 36$. So $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 100$ and $\sigma = 10$.
2. Reliability: $\rho = \frac{64}{100} = 0.64$.
3. Score 80: $60 + 0.64 \times (80 - 60) = 60 + 12.8 = 72.8$.
4. Score 40: $60 + 0.64 \times (40 - 60) = 60 - 12.8 = 47.2$.

**Answer.** $\rho = 0.64$. The student with 80 is expected to get 72.8. The student with 40 is expected to get 47.2.

**Check.** Both move 7.2 marks toward the mean 60, which is 36% of the 20-mark distance. The simulation confirms $\rho$ and the averages of groups.

### Example 2: The Tutoring Trap

**Problem.** A school gives free tutoring to the bottom 10% of students on test 1 (the model of Example 1). Tutoring truly adds 2 marks. Without tutoring, how much do these students gain on test 2 on average? What gain will the school see with tutoring?

**Solution.**

1. The mean of the bottom 10% of a normal distribution is $\mu - \sigma \frac{\varphi(z)}{0.10}$, where $z = -1.2816$ is the 10% point and $\varphi(z) = 0.1755$ is the standard normal density there. So the mean is $60 - 10 \times \frac{0.1755}{0.10} = 60 - 17.55 = 42.45$.
2. The expected test-2 score for this group, without any tutoring: $60 + 0.64 \times (42.45 - 60) = 60 - 11.23 = 48.77$.
3. The gain from regression alone: $48.77 - 42.45 = 6.32$.
4. Add the real effect of tutoring: $6.32 + 2 = 8.32$.

**Answer.** Even with no tutoring, the group gains about 6.3 marks. With a real effect of 2 marks, the school sees a gain of about 8.3 marks and may think the tutoring is worth about 8.3 marks. Only 2 of them are real.

**Check.** The simulation in Practice problem 6 gives $+8.34$ for the tutored group, and $-0.71$ for the other students (who are slightly above average on test 1, so they regress slightly down). To measure the tutoring fairly, you need a **control group** chosen by chance from the same low scorers.

### Example 3: Looking Backwards

**Problem.** Use the model of Example 1. The top 10% on test 2 are chosen. What was their average on test 1?

**Solution.**

1. The mean of the top 10% on test 2: $60 + 10 \times \frac{0.1755}{0.10} = 77.55$.
2. By symmetry, $E[X_1 \mid X_2 = x] = 60 + 0.64(x - 60)$. For the group: $60 + 0.64 \times (77.55 - 60) = 60 + 11.23 = 71.23$.

**Answer.** The top group on test 2 had an average of only 71.2 on test 1. They were "improving" in the sense of the group average, but nobody taught them anything.

**Check.** Regression works in both directions. Extreme **groups** chosen on one measurement look less extreme on the other, in the past or in the future. It is about selection and noise, not about time.

## Simulate It

We create 2 million students with skill and luck, and look at the extreme groups on both tests.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000
skill = rng.normal(60, 8, n)                       # true ability
test1 = skill + rng.normal(0, 6, n)                # score = skill + luck
test2 = skill + rng.normal(0, 6, n)                # a second test, new luck
rho = 64 / 100                                     # reliability = Var(skill) / Var(score)
print(f"Correlation between the tests: {np.corrcoef(test1, test2)[0, 1]:.4f}  (exact {rho:.4f})")

# Bottom 10% on test 1: how do the same students do on test 2?
low = test1 <= np.quantile(test1, 0.10)
mean_low = 60 - 10 * norm.pdf(norm.ppf(0.10)) / 0.10          # exact mean of the bottom 10%
print(f"Bottom 10%, test 1: {test1[low].mean():.2f} (exact {mean_low:.2f})")
print(f"Bottom 10%, test 2: {test2[low].mean():.2f} (exact {60 + rho * (mean_low - 60):.2f})")

# Top 10% on test 2: how did they do on test 1? (Looking backwards in time gives the same effect.)
high = test2 >= np.quantile(test2, 0.90)
mean_high = 60 + 10 * norm.pdf(norm.ppf(0.10)) / 0.10
print(f"Top 10%, test 2:    {test2[high].mean():.2f} (exact {mean_high:.2f})")
print(f"Top 10%, test 1:    {test1[high].mean():.2f} (exact {60 + rho * (mean_high - 60):.2f})")
print(f"SD of test 1 and test 2: {test1.std():.2f}, {test2.std():.2f}  (exact 10)")
```

Output:

```text
Correlation between the tests: 0.6397  (exact 0.6400)
Bottom 10%, test 1: 42.46 (exact 42.45)
Bottom 10%, test 2: 48.80 (exact 48.77)
Top 10%, test 2:    77.54 (exact 77.55)
Top 10%, test 1:    71.23 (exact 71.23)
SD of test 1 and test 2: 10.00, 9.99  (exact 10)
```

The students chosen for a low score on test 1 gain about 6.3 marks on test 2 with no help at all. The students chosen for a high score on test 2 had a lower score on test 1. Also, the spread of the whole class stays at 10 on both tests: regression does not make everyone average, it only moves the **chosen extreme group**.

## Common Mistakes

- **Giving a cause to a change that is only regression.** "The coach shouted and the team improved", "the prize made the student worse". If you chose the group because it was extreme, expect it to move toward the mean without any cause.
- **Thinking that everything becomes average.** The whole population keeps its spread. New extreme cases appear in every round (different people with luck this time). The mean is not a magnet for each person.
- **Comparing a chosen group with itself and not with a control group.** To test a treatment, compare the chosen group with a similar group chosen in the same way and not treated ([22.8](22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md)).
- **Ignoring how much luck there is.** The pull depends on $\rho$. For a very reliable measurement (a long exam, a long season) the pull is small. For a noisy one (one match, one day's sales), it is large.
- **Believing it only works forward in time.** The top group on test 2 was also less extreme on test 1. The effect comes from selecting a group by a noisy score, not from the calendar.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** when you pick the best model out of many by its score on one validation set, its score on new data is lower. The best score was partly luck. This is the "winner's curse", and it is regression to the mean ([21.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)). Shrinking estimates toward the mean is also what ridge regression and Bayesian priors do.
- **Computer science:** a server with its worst response time of the week will look better next week. Do not "fix" it before you check that it was not just a bad day. A/B tests use a control group for this reason ([21.2](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Medicine:** patients often join a study when their symptoms are at the worst, so they improve even with a dummy pill. This is one reason for trials with a control group. (This file is not medical advice.)
- **Sport and business:** the best rookie often does worse in the second year, and the top fund of the year is usually not the top fund of the next year.
- **Everyday life:** a very hot day is usually followed by a cooler one. A restaurant that was amazing once is often a little less amazing the next time.
- **Try this today:** the next time something "got better (or worse) after I did X", ask: "was it extremely good or bad just before? Would it probably have moved toward normal anyway?" If you can, look at a similar case where you did nothing.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Two quizzes have mean 50 and correlation 0.5. A student scores 80 on quiz 1. What do you expect on quiz 2?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X_2 \mid X_1 = 80] = 50 + 0.5 \times (80 - 50) = 50 + 15 = 65$$

The student is still above average, but only half of the way up.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A hospital opens a special safety program after the month with the most accidents in a year. Next month, accidents fall by a third. Give an explanation other than the program.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Regression to the mean. The program was started because the month was extreme. Accident counts have a random part, and an extremely high month is usually followed by a more normal one. To see the real effect of the program, compare with several months before and after, or with a similar hospital that did not start it.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The mean score is 70. A student scored 90 on the first test. What do you expect on the second test if the correlation between the tests is (a) 0.8, (b) 0.2?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $70 + 0.8 \times 20 = 86$. (b) $70 + 0.2 \times 20 = 74$.

With reliable tests (0.8) the score stays near 90. With noisy tests (0.2) most of the 90 was luck, and the expected score falls to 74.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In the model "score = skill + luck", the skill has standard deviation 6 and the luck has standard deviation 8. The mean is 60. Find the reliability $\rho$. A student scores 90. What is the best guess of the student's true skill?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\rho = \frac{36}{36 + 64} = 0.36$$

The best guess of the skill is $60 + 0.36 \times (90 - 60) = 60 + 10.8 = 70.8$.

So most of the 30-mark lead is expected to be luck here, because the luck has more spread than the skill.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For standardized variables $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ (mean 0, variance 1, correlation $\rho$), find the numbers $a$ and $b$ that minimize $E[(Z_2 - a - b Z_1)^2]$. What is the minimum value? Explain why the same line of reasoning gives $E[Z_1 \mid Z_2] = \rho Z_2$ in the other direction.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand and use $E[Z_i] = 0$, $E[Z_i^2] = 1$, $E[Z_1 Z_2] = \rho$:

$$E[(Z_2 - a - bZ_1)^2] = 1 + a^2 + b^2 - 2b\rho$$

The terms with $a$ are smallest at $a = 0$. For $b$, the derivative $2b - 2\rho$ is zero at $b = \rho$, and the second derivative is positive. The minimum value is $1 + \rho^2 - 2\rho^2 = 1 - \rho^2$.

So the best line is $\rho Z_1$. The conditions are symmetric in $Z_1$ and $Z_2$, so swapping the roles gives the best line $\rho Z_2$ for $Z_1$. Regression to the mean works in both directions because the correlation $\rho$ is the same in both.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2: give the bottom 10% of test 1 a tutoring that adds 2 marks to test 2. Find the average gain of the tutored group and of the other students.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 2_000_000
skill = rng.normal(60, 8, n)
test1 = skill + rng.normal(0, 6, n)
test2 = skill + rng.normal(0, 6, n)

# The school gives tutoring to the bottom 10%. The tutoring truly adds 2 marks.
tutored = test1 <= np.quantile(test1, 0.10)
gain = test2 + 2.0 * tutored - test1
mean_low = 60 - 17.55                                  # exact mean of the bottom 10% on test 1
mean_rest = (60 - 0.1 * mean_low) / 0.9                # exact mean of the other 90%
print(f"Gain, tutored: {gain[tutored].mean():+.2f}  (exact {2 - 0.36 * (mean_low - 60):+.2f})")
print(f"Gain, others:  {gain[~tutored].mean():+.2f}  (exact {-0.36 * (mean_rest - 60):+.2f})")
```

Output:

```text
Gain, tutored: +8.34  (exact +8.32)
Gain, others:  -0.71  (exact -0.70)
```

Only 2 of the 8.3 marks of gain are due to the tutoring.

</details>

## Summary

- A result chosen because it is extreme contains both skill and luck. The luck does not repeat.
- Expected next result: $\mu + \rho (x - \mu)$. The lower the reliability $\rho$, the stronger the pull toward the mean.
- It works backwards too, and it does not make the whole population average. It is about selecting extremes from noisy numbers.
- Never give credit (or blame) for a change to a treatment, a coach or a reward without a control group.
- Shrink extreme numbers toward the average when you judge people, funds or models by one noisy score.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X_2 \mid X_1 = x] = \mu + \rho \thinspace (x - \mu)}
$$
