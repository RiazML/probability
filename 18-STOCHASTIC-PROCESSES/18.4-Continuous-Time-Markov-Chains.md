# 18.4 Continuous-Time Markov Chains

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](README.md) / 18.4 Continuous-Time Markov Chains

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [18.3 Markov Chains](18.3-Markov-Chains/README.md)

> **Big idea:** A continuous-time Markov chain waits in each state for an exponential time and then jumps; one table of rates (the generator matrix $Q$) describes it, and its long-run law is the solution of $\pi Q = 0$.

---

## Why It Matters

In [18.3](18.3-Markov-Chains/README.md) time moved in ticks: day 1, day 2, day 3. Real systems do not tick. A server crashes at some moment, a customer arrives at some moment, a molecule reacts at some moment. A **continuous-time Markov chain** keeps the "no memory" idea of [18.3.1](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.1-Markov-Property.md) but lets the jumps happen at any time. This is the model behind the Poisson process ([18.5](18.5-Poisson-Process.md)), queues ([18.7](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md)), reliability of machines, chemical reactions and epidemics.

## Intuition

A machine is **Up** or **Down**. When it is Up, it fails after a random time with mean 5 hours. When it is Down, a technician repairs it after a random time with mean 1 hour. What does the random time look like?

It must have no memory. If the machine has already been Up for 3 hours, the time until failure should look just like it did at the start. Only one continuous distribution has this property: the **exponential** ([9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)). So the time in each state is exponential. A failure rate of $0.2$ per hour gives mean $\frac{1}{0.2} = 5$ hours, and a repair rate of $1$ per hour gives mean 1 hour.

This gives a simple recipe. Stay in the current state for an exponential time. Then jump to a new state. Then forget everything and repeat. The picture shows one path of the machine. It is a **step function**: it is flat, then jumps, then is flat again.

## Definition

Let $\lbrace X(t) : t \ge 0 \rbrace$ be a process with a finite or countable state space $\mathcal{S}$ and continuous time ([18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)). It is a **continuous-time Markov chain** (CTMC) if, for all times $s, t \ge 0$,

$$
P(X(s + t) = j \mid X(s) = i, \text{ the whole past up to time } s) = P(X(s + t) = j \mid X(s) = i) = p_{ij}(t)
$$

where $p_{ij}(t)$ does not depend on $s$. The matrix $P(t)$ with entries $p_{ij}(t)$ is the **transition function**.

**In words:** given the present state, the future does not depend on the past, and the chance to be in $j$ after time $t$ depends only on the state $i$ now and on the length $t$.

**Rates.** For each pair of different states $i \ne j$, the **rate** $q_{ij} \ge 0$ is the number of jumps from $i$ to $j$ per unit time, while the chain sits in $i$. Write

$$
q_i = \sum_{j \ne i} q_{ij}
$$

for the **total rate of leaving** state $i$. Put all rates into the **generator matrix** $Q$: the off-diagonal entries are $q_{ij}$, and the diagonal entries are $q_{ii} = -q_i$. So every row of $Q$ sums to $0$. For the machine, with state order (Up, Down):

$$
Q = \begin{pmatrix} -0.2 & 0.2 \cr 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**The recipe (jump chain and holding times).** A CTMC does the following, again and again.

1. When the chain enters state $i$, it stays there for a **holding time** $H_i \sim \text{Exp}(q_i)$. Here $q_i$ is the rate, so the mean holding time is $\frac{1}{q_i}$.
2. Then it jumps to state $j \ne i$ with probability $\frac{q_{ij}}{q_i}$, independently of the holding time.

**In words:** wait an exponential time with the total leaving rate, then choose where to go in proportion to the rates.

> **Note:** The list of states visited, with the waiting times removed, is an ordinary Markov chain of [18.3](18.3-Markov-Chains/README.md). It is called the **jump chain**, and its transition probabilities are $\frac{q_{ij}}{q_i}$.

**Kolmogorov equations.** The matrix $P(t)$ satisfies

$$
P'(t) = P(t) Q \quad \text{(forward)}, \qquad P'(t) = Q P(t) \quad \text{(backward)}, \qquad P(0) = I
$$

and its solution is the **matrix exponential**

$$
P(t) = e^{tQ} = I + tQ + \frac{t^2 Q^2}{2!} + \frac{t^3 Q^3}{3!} + \cdots
$$

**In words:** $Q$ is the speed at which the transition probabilities start to change, and $e^{tQ}$ is what you get after running for time $t$. It is the continuous-time version of $P^n$ in [18.3.2](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.2-Transition-Matrix-and-N-Step-Transitions.md).

**Stationary distribution.** A probability vector $\pi$ is **stationary** if $\pi P(t) = \pi$ for every $t \ge 0$. This holds exactly when

$$
\pi Q = 0, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1
$$

Written out, for every state $j$ these are the **balance equations**:

$$
\pi_j \thinspace q_j = \sum_{i \ne j} \pi_i \thinspace q_{ij}
$$

**In words:** in the long run, the rate at which the chain leaves state $j$ equals the rate at which it arrives. For a finite chain in which every state can reach every other state, there is exactly one such $\pi$, and $\pi_j$ is the long-run share of time spent in $j$ (the argument is the same as in [18.3.5](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md)).

## Why It Is True

**Why the exponential, and why the recipe works.** Think of the chain in state $i$ as having one alarm clock for each state $j \ne i$. The clock for $j$ rings after an $\text{Exp}(q_{ij})$ time, and the clocks are independent. The first clock to ring decides the jump. Let $C_j \sim \text{Exp}(q_{ij})$ be the clock for $j$ and $M_j$ the minimum of all the other clocks. By the rule that rates add ([9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)), $M_j \sim \text{Exp}(q_i - q_{ij})$. The holding time is the minimum of all clocks, and it is $\text{Exp}(q_i)$. The probability that clock $j$ rings first is

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(C_j \lt M_j) &= \int_0^{\infty} q_{ij} e^{-q_{ij} t} \cdot P(M_j \gt t) \thinspace dt && \text{(condition on } C_j = t \text{)} \\
&= \int_0^{\infty} q_{ij} e^{-q_{ij} t} e^{-(q_i - q_{ij}) t} \thinspace dt && \text{(survival function of } M_j \text{)} \\
&= q_{ij} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-q_i t} \thinspace dt = \frac{q_{ij}}{q_i} && \text{(integrate)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the recipe (an $\text{Exp}(q_i)$ wait, then a jump to $j$ with probability $\frac{q_{ij}}{q_i}$) is the same as "run all the clocks and follow the first one". The memoryless property of the exponential is what makes the Markov property true: at any moment, every running clock starts afresh.

**Why $Q$ is a rate.** Take a very short time $h$. The chance of leaving state $i$ within time $h$ is $1 - e^{-q_i h}$, and for small $h$ this is $q_i h$ plus a term much smaller than $h$. We write that small term as $o(h)$ (read: small o of $h$): it means a quantity that divided by $h$ tends to 0. The chance of two or more jumps in time $h$ is also $o(h)$. If the chain leaves, it goes to $j$ with probability $\frac{q_{ij}}{q_i}$. So

$$
p_{ij}(h) = q_i h \cdot \frac{q_{ij}}{q_i} + o(h) = q_{ij} h + o(h) \quad (i \ne j), \qquad p_{ii}(h) = 1 - q_i h + o(h)
$$

In matrix form, $P(h) = I + hQ + o(h)$.

**Where the Kolmogorov equations come from.** Running for time $t + h$ is the same as running for $t$ and then for $h$ (the chain is Markov, and this is the Chapman-Kolmogorov rule of [18.3.2](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.2-Transition-Matrix-and-N-Step-Transitions.md) in continuous time): $P(t + h) = P(t) P(h)$. Then

$$
\frac{P(t + h) - P(t)}{h} = P(t) \frac{P(h) - I}{h} = P(t) \frac{hQ + o(h)}{h} \xrightarrow{h \to 0} P(t) Q
$$

This is the forward equation. Splitting as $P(h) P(t)$ gives the backward equation $P'(t) = Q P(t)$. The equation $P' = P Q$ with $P(0) = I$ is solved by the series $e^{tQ}$, in the same way that $y' = ay$ is solved by $e^{at}$.

**Why $\pi Q = 0$ means stationary.** Suppose $\pi Q = 0$. By the backward equation, the vector $\pi P(t)$ has derivative $\pi P'(t) = \pi Q P(t) = 0 \cdot P(t) = 0$. A function with zero derivative is constant, so $\pi P(t) = \pi P(0) = \pi$ for all $t$. Conversely, if $\pi P(t) = \pi$ for all $t$, differentiate at $t = 0$: $0 = \pi P'(0) = \pi Q$.

**The two-state chain in closed form.** Let the states be Up and Down, with failure rate $\alpha$ and repair rate $\beta$. Write $p(t) = p_{\text{Up},\text{Up}}(t)$, the chance to be Up at time $t$ when we start Up. The chain is either Up (and then leaves at rate $\alpha$) or Down (and then arrives at rate $\beta$), so

$$
p'(t) = -\alpha \thinspace p(t) + \beta \thinspace (1 - p(t)) = \beta - (\alpha + \beta) \thinspace p(t), \qquad p(0) = 1
$$

The constant $\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}$ makes the right side zero. The difference $p(t) - \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}$ shrinks at rate $\alpha + \beta$, so

$$
p(t) = \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} + \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} e^{-(\alpha + \beta) t}
$$

(Check: at $t = 0$ it equals $\frac{\beta + \alpha}{\alpha + \beta} = 1$.) As $t \to \infty$, $p(t) \to \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}$, which is the stationary probability of Up. Compare with the discrete chain in [18.3.2](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.2-Transition-Matrix-and-N-Step-Transitions.md): there the factor was $(1 - a - b)^n$, and here it is $e^{-(\alpha + \beta) t}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Machine That Fails and Is Repaired

**Problem.** A machine fails at rate $\alpha = 0.2$ per hour and is repaired at rate $\beta = 1$ per hour. It is Up at time 0. Find (a) the mean time Up and Down, (b) the long-run fraction of time Up, (c) $P(\text{Up at time } 2)$, (d) the number of Down hours in a month of 720 hours.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The holding times are exponential, so the mean Up time is $\frac{1}{0.2} = 5$ hours and the mean Down time is $\frac{1}{1} = 1$ hour.
2. (b) Solve $\pi Q = 0$. The balance equation for Up says: rate out equals rate in, $0.2 \pi_U = 1 \cdot \pi_D$. With $\pi_U + \pi_D = 1$ we get $\pi_D = 0.2 \pi_U$, so $1.2 \pi_U = 1$ and

$$
\pi_U = \frac{1}{1.2} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.8333, \qquad \pi_D = \frac{1}{6}
$$

3. (c) Use the closed form with $\alpha + \beta = 1.2$ and $t = 2$:

$$
p(2) = \frac{5}{6} + \frac{1}{6} e^{-2.4} = 0.83333 + \frac{0.09072}{6} = 0.83333 + 0.01512 = 0.84845
$$

4. (d) The Down share is $\frac{1}{6}$, so $720 \times \frac{1}{6} = 120$ hours.

**Answer.** (a) 5 hours and 1 hour. (b) $\frac{5}{6}$. (c) about $0.8485$. (d) about 120 hours.

**Check.** The share of time Up equals mean Up time divided by the mean cycle time: $\frac{5}{5 + 1} = \frac{5}{6}$. Also $p(2) = 0.8485$ is already close to $\frac{5}{6}$ because $e^{-2.4}$ is small. The simulation below gives $0.8514$ for $p(2)$.

### Example 2: A Cloud Machine with Three States

**Problem.** A virtual machine is Healthy (H), Degraded (D) or Failed (F). Rates per day: H to D is 2; D to H is 1 and D to F is 1; F to H is 4 (a restart). Find $Q$, the mean time spent in each state per visit, the long-run share of time in each state, and the probability that a Degraded machine fails before it recovers.

**Solution.**

1. Write $Q$ (order H, D, F). Each diagonal entry makes its row sum to $0$:

$$
Q = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 & 0 \cr 1 & -2 & 1 \cr 4 & 0 & -4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

2. The leaving rates are $q_H = 2$, $q_D = 2$, $q_F = 4$, so the mean times per visit are $0.5$, $0.5$ and $0.25$ days.
3. From D there are two clocks, with rates 1 and 1. By the competing-clocks result, the machine fails before it recovers with probability $\frac{q_{DF}}{q_D} = \frac{1}{2}$.
4. Balance equations (rate out equals rate in):
   - H: $2 \pi_H = 1 \cdot \pi_D + 4 \pi_F$
   - D: $2 \pi_D = 2 \pi_H$
   - F: $4 \pi_F = 1 \cdot \pi_D$
5. The D equation gives $\pi_D = \pi_H$. The F equation gives $\pi_F = \frac{\pi_D}{4} = \frac{\pi_H}{4}$. The sum is $\pi_H (1 + 1 + 0.25) = 2.25 \thinspace \pi_H = 1$, so $\pi_H = \frac{4}{9}$.
6. Check the H equation: $2 \cdot \frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{9}$ and $\frac{4}{9} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9}$. It holds.

**Answer.** $\pi = \left( \frac{4}{9}, \frac{4}{9}, \frac{1}{9} \right)$. The machine is Failed about 11% of the time. A Degraded machine fails first with probability $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Check.** The jump chain (H goes to D; D goes to H or F with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each; F goes to H) has stationary law $(0.4, 0.4, 0.2)$. This is different from $\pi$, because the chain stays only $0.25$ days in F but $0.5$ days in H and D. Weighting by the mean holding times $0.5, 0.5, 0.25$ gives $(0.2, 0.2, 0.05)$, and dividing by the total $0.45$ gives $\left( \frac{4}{9}, \frac{4}{9}, \frac{1}{9} \right)$. The simulation below gives $(0.4424, 0.4455, 0.1121)$.

## Simulate It

The code runs a continuous-time chain with exactly the recipe of this file: an exponential holding time, then a jump chosen by $\frac{q_{ij}}{q_i}$. It checks Example 1 (c) and Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def simulate(Q, start, t_end):
    """Run a continuous-time chain; return the state at t_end and the time spent in each state."""
    state, t, spent = start, 0.0, np.zeros(len(Q))
    while True:
        rate = -Q[state, state]                      # total rate of leaving this state
        hold = rng.exponential(1 / rate)             # holding time ~ Exp(rate)
        if t + hold >= t_end:
            spent[state] += t_end - t
            return state, spent
        spent[state] += hold
        t += hold
        jump = Q[state].clip(min=0) / rate           # jump to j with probability q_ij / q_i
        state = rng.choice(len(Q), p=jump)

# Two-state machine (0 = Up, 1 = Down): failure rate 0.2, repair rate 1 per hour.
Q2 = np.array([[-0.2, 0.2], [1.0, -1.0]])
ends = [simulate(Q2, 0, 2.0)[0] for _ in range(20_000)]
print(f"P(Up at t=2): simulation {np.mean(np.array(ends) == 0):.4f}, exact {5/6 + (1/6) * np.exp(-2.4):.4f}")

# Three-state cloud machine (0 = Healthy, 1 = Degraded, 2 = Failed): long-run share of time.
Q3 = np.array([[-2.0, 2.0, 0.0], [1.0, -2.0, 1.0], [4.0, 0.0, -4.0]])
_, spent = simulate(Q3, 0, 50_000.0)
print("time shares:", np.round(spent / spent.sum(), 4))
print("exact pi:   ", np.round([4/9, 4/9, 1/9], 4))
```

Output:

```text
P(Up at t=2): simulation 0.8514, exact 0.8485
time shares: [0.4424 0.4455 0.1121]
exact pi:    [0.4444 0.4444 0.1111]
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up a rate and a probability.** A rate of $0.2$ per hour is not a probability of $0.2$. It gives mean time $\frac{1}{0.2} = 5$ hours. A rate can be larger than 1 (the repair rate is $1$ per hour, the restart rate is $4$ per day).
- **Rows of $Q$ that do not sum to 0.** Rows of the discrete matrix $P$ sum to 1. Rows of $Q$ sum to 0, because the diagonal entry is minus the total leaving rate. If a row does not sum to 0, a rate is missing.
- **Using the stationary law of the jump chain.** The jump chain counts visits. The real chain counts time. A state with a long holding time deserves more weight (Example 2 shows $(0.4, 0.4, 0.2)$ against $\left( \frac{4}{9}, \frac{4}{9}, \frac{1}{9} \right)$).
- **Writing $P(t) = tQ$, or taking $e^{Q}$ entry by entry.** The correct formula is the matrix exponential $e^{tQ}$, a full matrix series. The first-order guess $I + tQ$ works only for tiny $t$.
- **Thinking that the chain jumps at fixed ticks.** The jump times are random. Between jumps the chain does not move, and the time between jumps has no memory.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** continuous-time Markov decision processes in reinforcement learning ([21.1.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)), and stochastic simulation of chemical reactions (the Gillespie algorithm is exactly the recipe of this file).
- **Computer science:** the number of jobs in a server is a CTMC ([18.7](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md), [21.3.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)). Availability of a system is the stationary probability of the "Up" states.
- **Engineering:** reliability of machines with repair, as in Example 1 ([21.7](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)).
- **Biology and medicine:** birth and death of cells, and the spread of a disease between the states Susceptible, Infected and Recovered ([21.9](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.9-Biology-and-Genetics.md)).
- **Everyday life:** the status of a phone (online or offline), a taxi (free or busy), or a call centre agent.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A phone is Online or Offline. It goes Online to Offline at rate 3 per hour, and Offline to Online at rate 6 per hour. Write $Q$. Find the mean time of one Online stay and the long-run fraction of time Online.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In the order (Online, Offline), the first row of $Q$ is $(-3, \thinspace 3)$ and the second row is $(6, \thinspace -6)$. The mean Online stay is $\frac{1}{3}$ hour, which is 20 minutes. The balance equation for Online is $3 \pi_{\text{On}} = 6 \pi_{\text{Off}}$, so $\pi_{\text{On}} = 2 \pi_{\text{Off}}$, and with the sum equal to 1:

$$\pi_{\text{On}} = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \pi_{\text{Off}} = \frac{1}{3}$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Some entries of a generator matrix are missing. Its rows are $(-3, 1, a)$, $(2, b, 2)$ and $(0, 5, c)$. Find $a$, $b$, $c$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every row of $Q$ sums to 0. Row 1: $-3 + 1 + a = 0$, so $a = 2$. Row 2: $2 + b + 2 = 0$, so $b = -4$. Row 3: $0 + 5 + c = 0$, so $c = -5$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A server has two independent disks. Disk A fails at rate $0.1$ per year and disk B at rate $0.3$ per year. Find the mean time until the first failure, and the probability that disk A is the first to fail.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The first failure is the minimum of two independent exponentials, so it is exponential with the sum of the rates, $0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4$:

$$E[\min] = \frac{1}{0.4} = 2.5 \text{ years}$$

By the competing-clocks result, disk A is first with probability

$$\frac{0.1}{0.1 + 0.3} = \frac{1}{4}$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A chain on the states $0, 1, 2$ moves up ($0 \to 1$ and $1 \to 2$) at rate 2, and moves down ($1 \to 0$ and $2 \to 1$) at rate 3. Find the stationary distribution.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The balance equation for state 0 says $2 \pi_0 = 3 \pi_1$, so $\pi_1 = \frac{2}{3} \pi_0$. For state 2: $3 \pi_2 = 2 \pi_1$, so $\pi_2 = \frac{2}{3} \pi_1 = \frac{4}{9} \pi_0$. The sum must be 1:

$$\pi_0 \left( 1 + \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{4}{9} \right) = \tfrac{19}{9} \pi_0 = 1 \implies \pi = \left( \tfrac{9}{19}, \tfrac{6}{19}, \tfrac{4}{19} \right) \approx (0.4737, 0.3158, 0.2105)$$

(Check state 1: out rate $5 \cdot \frac{6}{19} = \frac{30}{19}$, in rate $2 \cdot \frac{9}{19} + 3 \cdot \frac{4}{19} = \frac{30}{19}$.)

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that if $\pi Q = 0$, then $\pi P(t) = \pi$ for every $t \ge 0$. Use the backward equation $P'(t) = Q P(t)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $v(t) = \pi P(t)$. Differentiate, then use the backward equation and $\pi Q = 0$:

$$v'(t) = \pi P'(t) = \pi Q P(t) = 0 \cdot P(t) = 0$$

A vector function with derivative 0 for all $t$ is constant. So $v(t) = v(0) = \pi P(0) = \pi I = \pi$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the answer of Problem 4 by simulation: run the chain and find the share of time in each state.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
Q = np.array([[-2.0, 2.0, 0.0], [3.0, -5.0, 2.0], [0.0, 3.0, -3.0]])
state, spent = 0, np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(300_000):
    rate = -Q[state, state]
    spent[state] += rng.exponential(1 / rate)      # holding time in this state
    state = rng.choice(3, p=Q[state].clip(min=0) / rate)
print("simulation:", np.round(spent / spent.sum(), 4))

```

## Summary

- A continuous-time Markov chain waits an $\text{Exp}(q_i)$ time in state $i$ and then jumps to $j$ with probability $\frac{q_{ij}}{q_i}$. Memoryless holding times are what make it Markov.
- The generator matrix $Q$ holds the rates; its rows sum to 0. For short times, $P(h) \approx I + hQ$.
- The transition function is $P(t) = e^{tQ}$, and it solves the Kolmogorov equations $P' = PQ = QP$.
- The stationary law solves $\pi Q = 0$: for every state, rate out equals rate in. For the two-state machine, $\pi_{\text{Up}} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(t) = e^{tQ}, \qquad \pi Q = 0, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1}
$$
