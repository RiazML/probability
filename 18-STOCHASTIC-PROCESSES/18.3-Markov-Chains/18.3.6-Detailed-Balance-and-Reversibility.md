# 18.3.6 Detailed Balance and Reversibility

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.3 Markov Chains](README.md) / 18.3.6 Detailed Balance and Reversibility

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [18.3.5 Stationary Distribution](18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md)

> **Big idea:** If the flow of probability from $i$ to $j$ equals the flow from $j$ to $i$ for every pair of states, then the law is stationary and the chain looks the same when time runs backwards; checking one pair at a time is much easier than solving all the balance equations together.

---

## Why It Matters

In [18.3.5](18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md) we found $\pi$ by solving a system of equations. For many chains there is a shortcut: guess that each road is balanced on its own. This is **detailed balance**. It gives $\pi$ in one line for walks on graphs and for queues. It also works in reverse: to build a chain with a **target** distribution, choose steps that satisfy detailed balance. That is the idea of Metropolis-Hastings in MCMC ([19.7.1](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/19.7.1-Metropolis-Hastings.md)).

## Intuition

Film a chain in equilibrium and play the film backwards. For a two-way street with the same traffic in both directions, you cannot tell which way the film runs. For a roundabout where cars go clockwise, you can tell at once. The first is **reversible**, the second is not.

Look at the weather chain in equilibrium, where $\pi = (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{6})$. On a typical day, Sunny is followed by Cloudy with probability $\frac{1}{2} \times 0.2 = 0.1$ (share of all days with the move Sunny to Cloudy). The move Cloudy to Sunny has share $\frac{1}{3} \times 0.3 = 0.1$. They are equal. The same is true for the other two pairs, so a film of the weather, played backwards, has the same statistics as the original. The weather chain is reversible.

## Picture

```text
  Reversible: every road is a two-way street with equal traffic

     Sunny --- 0.10 ---> Cloudy        pi_S p_SC = 0.5 x 0.2 = 0.10
     Sunny <-- 0.10 ---- Cloudy        pi_C p_CS = 1/3 x 0.3 = 0.10


  Not reversible: a roundabout (traffic goes mostly one way)

            A ---- 0.23 ----> B           A to B: 0.23 per step
            ^                 |           B to A: 0.10 per step
          0.23                0.23        the traffic circulates
            |                 v
            +------ C <-------+
```

On the left the two flows between a pair are equal. On the right they differ, and a current circulates around the loop A, B, C. (The numbers on the right are the flows $\frac{1}{3} \times 0.7$ clockwise and $\frac{1}{3} \times 0.3$ the other way, rounded.)

## Definition

Let $P$ be a transition matrix and $\pi$ a probability vector. The **detailed balance equations** are

$$
\pi_i \thinspace p_{ij} = \pi_j \thinspace p_{ji} \qquad \text{for all states } i, j
$$

If they hold, the chain is called **reversible** with respect to $\pi$.

**In words:** in equilibrium, the share of time that the chain moves from $i$ to $j$ equals the share of time that it moves from $j$ to $i$.

**Theorem 1.** If $\pi$ is a probability vector that satisfies detailed balance, then $\pi$ is a stationary distribution.

**Theorem 2 (the film runs both ways).** Let $X_0 \sim \pi$ with $\pi$ stationary and $\pi_i \gt 0$ for all $i$. Fix $N$ and look at the time-reversed sequence $Y_n = X_{N-n}$. Then $Y$ is a Markov chain with transition probabilities

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{\pi_j \thinspace p_{ji}}{\pi_i}
$$

and $q_{ij} = p_{ij}$ for all $i, j$ exactly when detailed balance holds. So the chain is reversible if and only if the reversed film has the same law as the original.

**Theorem 3 (cycle test, Kolmogorov).** An irreducible chain is reversible if and only if for every closed loop of states $i_1, i_2, \dots, i_k, i_1$ the product of the probabilities in one direction equals the product in the other:

$$
p_{i_1 i_2} \thinspace p_{i_2 i_3} \cdots p_{i_k i_1} = p_{i_1 i_k} \thinspace p_{i_k i_{k-1}} \cdots p_{i_2 i_1}
$$

We prove the "only if" half in Practice 5.

**Theorem 4 (walks on a line, birth-death chains).** If a chain moves only between neighbours on a line of states $0, 1, \dots, K$ (it can also stay), then it is reversible, and

$$
\pi_{i+1} = \pi_i \frac{p_{i, i+1}}{p_{i+1, i}}
$$

**Theorem 5 (random walk on a graph).** Let a walker sit on a vertex of a connected graph and move to a uniformly random neighbour. If vertex $i$ has $d_i$ neighbours (its **degree**) and the graph has $E$ edges, then

$$
\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{2E}
$$

**In words:** a walker on a graph spends time in proportion to how many roads meet at a place.

## Why It Is True

**Theorem 1.** Sum the detailed balance equation over $i$, and use the fact that the row of $P$ at $j$ sums to 1:

$$
\sum_i \pi_i p_{ij} = \sum_i \pi_j p_{ji} = \pi_j \sum_i p_{ji} = \pi_j
$$

This is the balance equation $\pi P = \pi$.

**Theorem 2.** By Bayes' rule ([4.4](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)), for a stationary chain:

$$
P(X_n = j \mid X_{n+1} = i) = \frac{P(X_n = j) \thinspace P(X_{n+1} = i \mid X_n = j)}{P(X_{n+1} = i)} = \frac{\pi_j \thinspace p_{ji}}{\pi_i}
$$

This is $q_{ij}$. It is also true that the past is independent of the future given the present, which holds the same way in both directions ([18.3.1](18.3.1-Markov-Property.md), Practice 4), so $Y$ is a Markov chain. Finally $q_{ij} = p_{ij}$ means $\pi_j p_{ji} = \pi_i p_{ij}$, which is detailed balance.

**Theorem 4.** Think of a cut between the states $\lbrace 0, \dots, i \rbrace$ (call this set $A$) and the rest. Write the balance equation $\pi_j = \sum_l \pi_l p_{lj}$ for each $j \in A$ and add them. Also write $\pi_j = \pi_j \sum_l p_{jl}$ for each $j \in A$ and add them. The two sums are equal ($\sum_{j \in A} \pi_j$), so subtract:

$$
\sum_{j \in A} \sum_{l} \pi_l p_{lj} = \sum_{j \in A} \sum_{l} \pi_j p_{jl}
$$

The terms with both $j$ and $l$ in $A$ appear on both sides and cancel. What remains is the flow into $A$ equals the flow out of $A$:

$$
\sum_{j \in A, \thinspace l \notin A} \pi_l p_{lj} = \sum_{j \in A, \thinspace l \notin A} \pi_j p_{jl}
$$

On a line with neighbour steps only, the only pair joining $A = \lbrace 0, \dots, i \rbrace$ to the rest is $(i, i + 1)$. So $\pi_{i+1} p_{i+1, i} = \pi_i p_{i, i+1}$, which is detailed balance for this pair. All other pairs have $p_{ij} = p_{ji} = 0$ and satisfy it trivially.

**Theorem 5.** For a neighbour $j$ of $i$ we have $p_{ij} = \frac{1}{d_i}$ and $p_{ji} = \frac{1}{d_j}$. Try $\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{2E}$:

$$
\pi_i p_{ij} = \frac{d_i}{2E} \cdot \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{2E} = \frac{d_j}{2E} \cdot \frac{1}{d_j} = \pi_j p_{ji}
$$

For non-neighbours, both sides are 0. So detailed balance holds, and by Theorem 1 this $\pi$ is stationary. It is a distribution because $\sum_i d_i = 2E$ (each edge is counted at both ends). If the graph is connected, the chain is irreducible, and by Theorem 1 of [18.3.5](18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md) it is the only stationary distribution.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Weather Chain Is Reversible

**Problem.** Show that the weather chain satisfies detailed balance with $\pi = (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{6})$. Then use detailed balance, and no other equation, to find $\pi$.

**Solution.**

1. Check the three pairs:

$$
\pi_S p_{SC} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.2 = 0.1, \qquad \pi_C p_{CS} = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 0.3 = 0.1
$$

$$
\pi_S p_{SR} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.1 = 0.05, \qquad \pi_R p_{RS} = \tfrac{1}{6} \times 0.3 = 0.05
$$

$$
\pi_C p_{CR} = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 0.2 = 0.0667, \qquad \pi_R p_{RC} = \tfrac{1}{6} \times 0.4 = 0.0667
$$

2. All three pairs are balanced, so the chain is reversible.
3. Now find $\pi$ from scratch. The first pair gives $0.2 \pi_S = 0.3 \pi_C$, so $\pi_C = \frac{2}{3} \pi_S$. The second gives $0.1 \pi_S = 0.3 \pi_R$, so $\pi_R = \frac{1}{3} \pi_S$.
4. Add: $\pi_S \left( 1 + \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3} \right) = 2 \pi_S = 1$, so $\pi_S = \frac{1}{2}$, $\pi_C = \frac{1}{3}$ and $\pi_R = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Answer.** The chain is reversible, and detailed balance gives $\pi = (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{6})$ with two short equations instead of a system.

**Check.** The cycle test (Theorem 3) agrees: Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy, Sunny has product $0.2 \times 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.012$, and the opposite loop Sunny, Rainy, Cloudy, Sunny has $0.1 \times 0.4 \times 0.3 = 0.012$.

### Example 2: A Random Surfer on an Undirected Network

**Problem.** A tiny network has four computers and the links A-B, A-C, B-C and C-D. A packet moves each step to a random neighbour of its current computer. Find the long-run share of time at each computer.

**Solution.**

1. Count the neighbours: A has 2 (B and C), B has 2 (A and C), C has 3 (A, B and D), and D has 1 (C). The sum is $2 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 8 = 2E$, since there are $E = 4$ links.
2. By Theorem 5, $\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{8}$:

$$
\pi_A = \frac{2}{8} = 0.25, \quad \pi_B = \frac{2}{8} = 0.25, \quad \pi_C = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375, \quad \pi_D = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125
$$

3. Check one balance equation directly: $\pi_C = \pi_A \cdot \frac{1}{2} + \pi_B \cdot \frac{1}{2} + \pi_D \cdot 1 = 0.125 + 0.125 + 0.125 = 0.375$. It holds.

**Answer.** $\pi = (0.25, 0.25, 0.375, 0.125)$.

**Check.** The busiest computer (C, with 3 links) gets the most visits, and the computer with one link (D) the fewest. The graph has a triangle (A, B, C), so the chain is aperiodic, and the second code block below confirms $\pi$ with an eigenvector.

### Example 3: A Packet Buffer (Birth-Death Chain)

**Problem.** A buffer holds at most 3 packets. In each time slot: with probability $0.3$ one packet arrives (it is lost if the buffer is full); otherwise, with probability $0.5$ one packet leaves (nothing happens if the buffer is empty); otherwise, with probability $0.2$, nothing happens. Find the long-run share of time with $0, 1, 2, 3$ packets, and the probability that the buffer is full.

**Solution.**

1. The state is the number of packets. The chain moves up by 1 with probability $p_{i, i+1} = 0.3$ ($i \lt 3$) and down by 1 with probability $p_{i, i-1} = 0.5$ ($i \gt 0$). It is a birth-death chain, so Theorem 4 applies:

$$
\pi_{i+1} = \pi_i \cdot \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 0.6 \thinspace \pi_i
$$

2. So $\pi_1 = 0.6 \pi_0$, $\pi_2 = 0.36 \pi_0$, $\pi_3 = 0.216 \pi_0$.
3. The total must be 1: $\pi_0 (1 + 0.6 + 0.36 + 0.216) = 2.176 \thinspace \pi_0 = 1$, so $\pi_0 = \frac{1}{2.176} = 0.4596$.
4. Then $\pi_1 = 0.2757$, $\pi_2 = 0.1654$ and $\pi_3 = 0.2160 \times 0.4596 = 0.0993$.

**Answer.** $\pi = (0.4596, 0.2757, 0.1654, 0.0993)$. The buffer is full about 9.9% of the time, and the mean number of packets is $0.2757 + 2 \times 0.1654 + 3 \times 0.0993 = 0.9044$.

**Check.** The probabilities decrease by the factor $0.6$ because the buffer is drained faster ($0.5$) than it is filled ($0.3$). The simulation in Practice 6 gives $(0.4599, 0.2755, 0.1654, 0.0991)$.

### Example 4: A Chain That Is Not Reversible

**Problem.** A walker moves around a triangle $0, 1, 2$. At each step it goes clockwise ($0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0$) with probability $0.7$ and anticlockwise with probability $0.3$. Find $\pi$ and test detailed balance.

**Solution.**

1. Every column of $P$ has the entries $0.7$ and $0.3$ (one from each neighbour), so it sums to 1: $P$ is doubly stochastic. By Practice 5 of [18.3.5](18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md), $\pi = (\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3})$.
2. Test the pair $(0, 1)$: $\pi_0 p_{01} = \frac{1}{3} \times 0.7 = 0.2333$, and $\pi_1 p_{10} = \frac{1}{3} \times 0.3 = 0.1$. They are not equal.
3. The cycle test also fails: clockwise product $0.7^3 = 0.343$, anticlockwise product $0.3^3 = 0.027$.

**Answer.** $\pi$ is uniform, but detailed balance fails, so the chain is not reversible.

**Check.** This shows that detailed balance is stronger than stationarity: a stationary distribution can exist without it. A film of this walker played backwards shows it going mostly anticlockwise, which is a different chain.

## Simulate It

The code simulates one long path and counts the share of steps that go from $i$ to $j$. For the weather chain the flow matrix is symmetric (detailed balance). For the clockwise triangle it is not. A second block finds $\pi$ for the four-computer network.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def flow_matrix(P, n_steps=1_000_000):
    """Simulate one path; return the share of steps that go from i to j."""
    cum = P.cumsum(axis=1)
    u = rng.random(n_steps)
    path = np.empty(n_steps + 1, dtype=int)
    path[0] = state = 0
    for t in range(n_steps):
        state = int(np.searchsorted(cum[state], u[t]))
        path[t + 1] = state
    counts = np.zeros(P.shape)
    np.add.at(counts, (path[:-1], path[1:]), 1)
    return counts / n_steps

np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)
weather = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.3, 0.5, 0.2], [0.3, 0.4, 0.3]])
pi = np.array([1/2, 1/3, 1/6])
exact = pi[:, None] * weather                  # exact flows pi_i * p_ij
sim = flow_matrix(weather)
print("weather: exact flow matrix is symmetric:", np.allclose(exact, exact.T))
print("weather: largest |F_ij - F_ji| in the simulation:", np.abs(sim - sim.T).max().round(4))

# A 3-cycle that prefers to go clockwise: pi is uniform, but detailed balance fails.
cyc = np.array([[0, 0.7, 0.3], [0.3, 0, 0.7], [0.7, 0.3, 0]])
sim = flow_matrix(cyc)
print("cycle: flow 0->1 =", sim[0, 1].round(4), "(exact 0.2333)")
print("cycle: flow 1->0 =", sim[1, 0].round(4), "(exact 0.1000)")
```

Output:

```text
weather: exact flow matrix is symmetric: True
weather: largest |F_ij - F_ji| in the simulation: 0.0001
cycle: flow 0->1 = 0.2336 (exact 0.2333)
cycle: flow 1->0 = 0.1003 (exact 0.1000)
```

A second short block checks the degree rule of Example 2 with an eigenvector:

```python
import numpy as np

# Random walk on a graph with edges A-B, A-C, B-C, C-D: pi_i = degree / (2 * edges).
adj = np.array([[0, 1, 1, 0], [1, 0, 1, 0], [1, 1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1, 0]])
G = adj / adj.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
lam, V = np.linalg.eig(G.T)
v = V[:, np.argmax(lam.real)].real
print("graph walk pi (eigenvector):", np.round(v / v.sum(), 4))
print("graph walk degree / 8:      ", adj.sum(axis=1) / 8)
```

Output:

```text
graph walk pi (eigenvector): [0.25  0.25  0.375 0.125]
graph walk degree / 8:       [0.25  0.25  0.375 0.125]
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Believing that every stationary chain satisfies detailed balance.** Detailed balance is stronger. The clockwise triangle (Example 4) has a stationary distribution but is not reversible.
- **Checking only some pairs.** Detailed balance must hold for **all** pairs $i, j$. One failure is enough to lose reversibility.
- **Using detailed balance to find $\pi$ when it does not hold.** If you assume $\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}$ and the chain is not reversible, you get a wrong answer or a contradiction. If the equations have a solution, the answer is right (Theorem 1). If they have none, go back to $\pi = \pi P$.
- **Forgetting the normalization.** Detailed balance gives ratios such as $\pi_{i+1} = 0.6 \pi_i$. Add the sum $\sum \pi_i = 1$ at the end (Example 3).
- **Mixing up the degree rule.** $\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{2E}$ is for a walker who picks a neighbour with equal probability. With unequal probabilities (weights), use $\pi_i \propto$ the total weight at $i$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Metropolis-Hastings builds a chain that satisfies detailed balance for the target posterior, so that the posterior is its stationary law ([19.7.1](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/19.7.1-Metropolis-Hastings.md)). Gibbs sampling and many energy-based models use the same idea.
- **Computer science:** random walks on undirected networks (peer-to-peer search, web crawling) visit nodes in proportion to their degree. Queues and buffers are birth-death chains ([21.3.4](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
- **Physics:** a physical system in thermal equilibrium is reversible: its microscopic motion looks the same forwards and backwards in time ([21.8](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.8-Physics.md)).
- **Genetics and biology:** simple models of gene frequency in a population are birth-death chains ([21.9](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.9-Biology-and-Genetics.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a two-way street with equal traffic is reversible, a roundabout is not.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Show that every two-state chain with transition probabilities $a$ (from state 1 to 2) and $b$ (from state 2 to 1) is reversible.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There is only one pair to check. With $\pi = \left( \frac{b}{a+b}, \frac{a}{a+b} \right)$:

$$\pi_1 p_{12} = \frac{b}{a + b} \cdot a = \frac{ab}{a + b} = \frac{a}{a + b} \cdot b = \pi_2 p_{21}$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A walker moves on the path $1 - 2 - 3$, each step to a random neighbour. Find $\pi$ by Theorem 5.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The degrees are $d = (1, 2, 1)$ and $E = 2$ edges, so $2E = 4$:

$$\pi = \left( \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4} \right)$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A chain on $\lbrace 0, 1, 2 \rbrace$ moves up and down as follows: $p_{01} = 0.4$, $p_{10} = 0.2$, $p_{12} = 0.4$, $p_{21} = 0.8$ (and it stays in place with the remaining probability). Find $\pi$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is a birth-death chain. By Theorem 4:

$$\pi_1 = \pi_0 \cdot \frac{0.4}{0.2} = 2 \pi_0, \qquad \pi_2 = \pi_1 \cdot \frac{0.4}{0.8} = \pi_0$$

So $\pi = (c, 2c, c)$ with $4c = 1$:

$$\pi = \left( \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4} \right)$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A walker goes around a triangle, clockwise with probability $0.8$ and anticlockwise with probability $0.2$. Show that it is not reversible, using the cycle test.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Going once clockwise around the loop $0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0$ has product $0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.512$. Going the other way, $0 \to 2 \to 1 \to 0$, has product $0.2 \times 0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.008$. The products differ, so the chain is not reversible. (Its stationary distribution is still uniform, since $P$ is doubly stochastic.)

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove the "only if" half of Theorem 3: if detailed balance holds with all $\pi_i \gt 0$, then the product around every loop is the same in both directions.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Detailed balance says $p_{ij} = \frac{\pi_j}{\pi_i} p_{ji}$. Apply it to each step of the loop $i_1 \to i_2 \to \dots \to i_k \to i_1$:

$$p_{i_1 i_2} \cdots p_{i_k i_1} = \frac{\pi_{i_2}}{\pi_{i_1}} p_{i_2 i_1} \cdot \frac{\pi_{i_3}}{\pi_{i_2}} p_{i_3 i_2} \cdots \frac{\pi_{i_1}}{\pi_{i_k}} p_{i_1 i_k}$$

The fractions telescope: the numerator of each is the denominator of the next one, and the last numerator $\pi_{i_1}$ cancels the first denominator. They multiply to 1. What remains is $p_{i_2 i_1} p_{i_3 i_2} \cdots p_{i_1 i_k}$, the product around the loop in the opposite direction.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 3: simulate the buffer and compare the share of time at each level with $(0.4596, 0.2757, 0.1654, 0.0993)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_slots = 1_000_000

# Buffer with room for 3 packets. Each slot: arrival with probability 0.3
# (lost if the buffer is full), else departure with probability 0.5
# (nothing leaves an empty buffer), else nothing happens (probability 0.2).
u = rng.random(n_slots)
level = 0
time_at = np.zeros(4)
for t in range(n_slots):
    if u[t] < 0.3:
        level = min(level + 1, 3)
    elif u[t] < 0.8:
        level = max(level - 1, 0)
    time_at[level] += 1

weights = 0.6 ** np.arange(4)                    # detailed balance: pi_{i+1} = 0.6 pi_i
pi = weights / weights.sum()
print("share of slots, simulation:", np.round(time_at / n_slots, 4))
print("share of slots, exact:     ", np.round(pi, 4))
print(f"mean level: simulation {(np.arange(4) * time_at).sum() / n_slots:.4f}, exact {(np.arange(4) * pi).sum():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
share of slots, simulation: [0.4599 0.2755 0.1654 0.0991]
share of slots, exact:      [0.4596 0.2757 0.1654 0.0993]
mean level: simulation 0.9037, exact 0.9044
```

</details>

## Summary

- **Detailed balance** $\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}$ says that, in equilibrium, the flow from $i$ to $j$ equals the flow from $j$ to $i$.
- If a probability vector satisfies it, that vector is stationary. A chain with this property is **reversible**: the film run backwards has the same law.
- Birth-death chains and random walks on graphs are always reversible: $\pi_{i+1} = \pi_i \frac{p_{i,i+1}}{p_{i+1,i}}$, and $\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{2E}$.
- Stationary does not imply reversible: a chain with a circulating current (the clockwise triangle) has a stationary law but fails detailed balance.
- MCMC designs chains with detailed balance for a target law.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\pi_i \thinspace p_{ij} = \pi_j \thinspace p_{ji} \implies \pi = \pi P}
$$
