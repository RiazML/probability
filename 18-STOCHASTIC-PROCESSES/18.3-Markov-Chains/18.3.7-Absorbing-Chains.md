# 18.3.7 Absorbing Chains

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.3 Markov Chains](README.md) / 18.3.7 Absorbing Chains

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [18.3.6 Detailed Balance and Reversibility](18.3.6-Detailed-Balance-and-Reversibility.md)

> **Big idea:** In a chain that ends in a trap, one matrix inverse $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$ gives the expected number of visits to every state, the expected time until the chain is trapped, and the probability of each trap.

---

## Why It Matters

Many random processes end: a game of Snakes and Ladders ends at the last square, a gambler stops at 0 or at the goal, a customer buys or leaves, a patient recovers or dies. The long-run law of such a chain is boring (it sits in a trap), but the **journey** is the interesting part. How long does it last? Which trap is entered? The answers come from one small matrix inverse, and they extend the first-step equations of [18.2.2](../18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md) to any chain.

## Intuition

You play Snakes and Ladders and ask: "How many turns will a game take?" The game is a Markov chain. The state is your square. The last square is a trap: when you get there the game stops, and you stay for ever. Every other square is transient ([18.3.3](18.3.3-Classification-of-States.md)): you pass through it a few times, or not at all.

You can find the answer by **first-step analysis**: the expected time from a square is 1 turn plus the average of the expected times from the squares where you may land. That gives one equation per square. Solving all of them together is a matrix problem, and the matrix $N$ below solves it for every start at once.

## Picture

```text
  Canonical form: write the transient states first, the absorbing ones last

                     to transient    to absorbing
     from transient  [     Q              R     ]
     from absorbing  [     0              I     ]

  A mini board: a fair coin moves the token 1 or 2 squares; reach 5 to win

   +-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+
   |  0  |  1  |  2  |  3  |  4  |  5  |
   |start|     |     |snake|     | end |
   +-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+
            ^           |
            +-----------+   landing on 3 slides the token back to 1
```

The matrix $Q$ holds the moves among the transient states, $R$ the moves into the traps, and $I$ says that a trap keeps the chain for ever.

## Definition

A state $i$ is **absorbing** if $p_{ii} = 1$. A chain is an **absorbing chain** if it has at least one absorbing state and from every state an absorbing state can be reached. Then every non-absorbing state is transient (Theorem 3 of [18.3.3](18.3.3-Classification-of-States.md)).

Order the states so that the $t$ transient states come first and the $a$ absorbing states last. Then the matrix has the **canonical form**

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} Q & R \cr 0 & I \end{pmatrix}
$$

where $Q$ is $t \times t$ (transient to transient), $R$ is $t \times a$ (transient to absorbing), $0$ is a block of zeros and $I$ is the $a \times a$ identity.

The **fundamental matrix** of the chain is

$$
N = (I - Q)^{-1} = I + Q + Q^2 + Q^3 + \cdots
$$

**Theorem 1.** $Q^n \to 0$: the chain is absorbed with probability 1.

**Theorem 2.** $N_{ij}$ is the expected number of visits to the transient state $j$ when the chain starts in the transient state $i$ (time $0$ counts as a visit if $j = i$).

**Theorem 3.** The vector $\mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1}$ (row sums of $N$) gives the expected number of steps until absorption: $t_i$ is the expected time when starting from $i$.

**Theorem 4.** The matrix $B = NR$ gives the absorption probabilities: $B_{ik}$ is the probability that the chain starting from $i$ is absorbed in the absorbing state $k$.

**In words:** $N$ counts visits, its row sums give the length of the journey, and $NR$ tells where the journey ends.

## Why It Is True

**Theorem 1.** Each transient state can reach some absorbing state. There are finitely many states, so there are a number $m$ and a number $\varepsilon \gt 0$ such that from every transient state, the chain is absorbed within $m$ steps with probability at least $\varepsilon$. So the probability of still being transient after $m$ steps is at most $1 - \varepsilon$, which means every row of $Q^m$ sums to at most $1 - \varepsilon$. The row sums of $Q^{2m}$ are at most $(1 - \varepsilon)^2$, and in general those of $Q^{km}$ are at most $(1 - \varepsilon)^k \to 0$. All entries are non-negative, so every entry of $Q^n$ tends to 0.

**Theorem 2.** For transient states $i, j$, the entry $(Q^n)_{ij}$ is the probability of being at $j$ at time $n$ starting from $i$ (the chain cannot have visited an absorbing state, since it would not have returned). The number of visits to $j$ is the sum of the indicators $I_{\lbrace X_n = j \rbrace}$ over $n \ge 0$. By linearity of expectation:

$$
E[\text{visits to } j] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (Q^n)_{ij} = (I + Q + Q^2 + \cdots)_{ij}
$$

To see that this sum is $(I - Q)^{-1}$, multiply out:

$$
(I - Q)(I + Q + \dots + Q^{n-1}) = I - Q^n \to I
$$

The partial sums converge (they are bounded and increasing entrywise, by Theorem 1), and in the limit $(I - Q) N = I$. So $I - Q$ is invertible and its inverse is $N$.

**Theorem 3.** The number of steps until absorption is the total number of visits to all transient states, so $t_i = \sum_j N_{ij}$.

**Theorem 4.** The chain is absorbed in $k$ at step $n + 1$ if it is at a transient state $j$ at step $n$ and jumps to $k$. Add over $j$ and $n$:

$$
B_{ik} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \sum_{j} (Q^n)_{ij} R_{jk} = \sum_{j} N_{ij} R_{jk} = (NR)_{ik}
$$

> **Tip:** You can skip the inverse and use first-step analysis. Theorem 3 says exactly that $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{1} + Q\mathbf{t}$, which is $(I - Q)\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{1}$: the expected time is 1 step plus the average of the expected times from where you land. Solve a small system by hand, or let the computer invert $I - Q$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Mini Snakes and Ladders Board

**Problem.** The board has squares $0, 1, \dots, 5$ as in the Picture. A fair coin decides each move: heads moves 1 square, tails moves 2 squares. Reaching or passing square 5 ends the game. Square 3 is a snake head: a token that lands on 3 slides back to square 1. Find the expected number of turns from the start.

**Solution.**

1. Square 3 is never occupied, because the token moves on at once. So the transient states are $0, 1, 2, 4$. Let $t_s$ be the expected number of turns from square $s$.
2. From square 4, both moves (to 5 or to 6) end the game, so $t_4 = 1$.
3. From square 2: heads goes to 3, which slides to 1; tails goes to 4. One turn is used:

$$
t_2 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_4
$$

4. From square 1: heads goes to 2; tails goes to 3, which slides back to 1. So

$$
t_1 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_1 \quad \implies \quad t_1 = 2 + t_2
$$

5. Put $t_1 = 2 + t_2$ and $t_4 = 1$ into the equation for $t_2$: $t_2 = 1 + 1 + \frac{1}{2} t_2 + \frac{1}{2}$, so $\frac{1}{2} t_2 = 2.5$ and $t_2 = 5$. Then $t_1 = 7$.
6. From square 0: heads goes to 1, tails goes to 2:

$$
t_0 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 7 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 5 = 1 + 3.5 + 2.5 = 7
$$

**Answer.** The game lasts 7 turns on average. In matrix language, $Q$ has the rows $(0, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, 0)$, $(0, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, 0)$, $(0, \frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{1}{2})$ and $(0, 0, 0, 0)$ for the states $0, 1, 2, 4$, and $\mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1} = (7, 7, 5, 1)$.

**Check.** Without the snake, the same board would take only $3.5625$ turns on average (Practice 3). So the single snake doubles the length of the game. The computer inverse in "Simulate It" gives the same vector $(7, 7, 5, 1)$.

### Example 2: Gambler's Ruin With the Fundamental Matrix

**Problem.** A gambler starts with $k$ taka, plays a fair game for 1 taka per round, and stops at 0 or at 4 taka. Find $N$, the expected game length and the probability of reaching 4 for $k = 1, 2, 3$.

**Solution.**

1. The transient states are the fortunes $1, 2, 3$ and the absorbing states are $0$ and $4$. The matrices are

$$
Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \cr \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \cr 0 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & 0 \cr 0 & 0 \cr 0 & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}
$$

where the columns of $R$ are the absorbing states $0$ and $4$.

2. Invert $I - Q$ (by computer, or by hand with the first-step equations):

$$
N = (I - Q)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 1 & 0.5 \cr 1 & 2 & 1 \cr 0.5 & 1 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

For example, starting from fortune 2, the gambler is at fortune 2 on average 2 times (time 0 included).

3. The row sums give the expected length: $\mathbf{t} = (1.5 + 1 + 0.5,\ 1 + 2 + 1,\ 0.5 + 1 + 1.5) = (3, 4, 3)$.
4. The absorption probabilities $B = NR$. For the start $k = 1$: $B_{1, 4} = 1.5 \times 0 + 1 \times 0 + 0.5 \times \frac{1}{2} = 0.25$ and $B_{1, 0} = 1.5 \times \frac{1}{2} = 0.75$. For $k = 2$: $B_{2, 4} = 1 \times \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$. For $k = 3$: $B_{3, 4} = 1.5 \times \frac{1}{2} = 0.75$.

**Answer.** Expected number of rounds $(3, 4, 3)$ and $P(\text{reach } 4) = (0.25, 0.5, 0.75)$.

**Check.** These match the formulas of [18.2.2](../18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md): expected duration $k(4 - k)$ gives $3, 4, 3$, and the chance of reaching the goal is $\frac{k}{4}$. Each row of $B$ sums to 1, because absorption is certain.

### Example 3: Expected Game Length on a 30-Square Board

**Problem.** A board has squares $0, 1, \dots, 29$ and the goal is to reach or pass square 30. You roll one fair die and move that many squares. Ladders: $3 \to 11$, $6 \to 17$, $12 \to 22$, $20 \to 28$. Snakes: $16 \to 5$, $24 \to 13$, $26 \to 8$, $29 \to 10$. Find the expected number of turns from square 0, and the chance that the game ends within 10 turns.

**Solution.**

1. The squares with a snake head or a ladder foot are never occupied. That leaves $30 - 8 = 22$ transient squares. Build $Q$ ($22 \times 22$): from square $s$, each roll $1, \dots, 6$ has probability $\frac{1}{6}$, and leads to $s + \text{roll}$ (after sliding or climbing if that square has a snake or a ladder), or to the goal if $s + \text{roll} \ge 30$.
2. A matrix of this size is solved by computer: $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$ and $\mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1}$ give $t_0 = 11.59$ turns.
3. The chance that the game is over within 10 turns is $1 - (Q^{10}\mathbf{1})_0$, where $(Q^{10} \mathbf{1})_0$ is the probability of still playing after 10 turns. The computer gives $0.5735$.

**Answer.** The expected length is about $11.59$ turns, and $P(\text{game over within 10 turns}) \approx 0.5735$.

**Check.** Without any snakes or ladders, the board needs about $9.05$ turns (about 30 squares divided by the average roll $3.5$, plus a little for the last roll). Here the four snakes cost more than the four ladders gain, so the game is longer, $11.59$. The second code block below plays 200,000 games and gets $11.601$ turns and $0.5739$.

## Simulate It

The function `fundamental` computes $\mathbf{t}$ and $B$ from $Q$ and $R$. The first block applies it to the mini board and to the gambler.

```python
import numpy as np

def fundamental(Q, R):
    N = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(len(Q)) - Q)        # N = (I - Q)^-1 = I + Q + Q^2 + ...
    return N.sum(axis=1), N @ R                  # expected time to absorption, absorption probs

# Mini board (squares 0,1,2,4 transient; 5 = finish; square 3 is a snake back to 1).
Q = np.array([[0, .5, .5, 0], [0, .5, .5, 0], [0, .5, 0, .5], [0, 0, 0, 0]])
t, _ = fundamental(Q, np.array([[0], [0], [0], [1.0]]))
print("mini board, expected turns from squares 0, 1, 2, 4:", t)

# Fair gambler's ruin with goal 4: transient fortunes 1, 2, 3; absorbing 0 and 4.
Q = np.array([[0, .5, 0], [.5, 0, .5], [0, .5, 0]])
t, B = fundamental(Q, np.array([[.5, 0], [0, 0], [0, .5]]))
print("gambler, expected rounds from 1, 2, 3:", t)
print("gambler, P(reach 4) from 1, 2, 3:     ", B[:, 1])
```

Output:

```text
mini board, expected turns from squares 0, 1, 2, 4: [7. 7. 5. 1.]
gambler, expected rounds from 1, 2, 3: [3. 4. 3.]
gambler, P(reach 4) from 1, 2, 3:      [0.25 0.5  0.75]
```

The second block builds $Q$ for the 30-square board of Example 3, then plays 200,000 games to compare with the exact answers.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
jumps = {3: 11, 6: 17, 12: 22, 20: 28, 16: 5, 24: 13, 26: 8, 29: 10}   # ladders, then snakes
squares = [s for s in range(30) if s not in jumps]       # squares where a token can rest
pos = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(squares)}
Q, R = np.zeros((22, 22)), np.zeros((22, 1))
for s in squares:
    for roll in range(1, 7):
        if s + roll >= 30: R[pos[s], 0] += 1 / 6         # finished
        else: Q[pos[s], pos[jumps.get(s + roll, s + roll)]] += 1 / 6
t = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(22) - Q).sum(axis=1)            # expected turns from each square

jump_to = np.arange(36)
for a, b in jumps.items(): jump_to[a] = b
n_games = 200_000
where, turns = np.zeros(n_games, dtype=int), np.zeros(n_games, dtype=int)
playing = np.ones(n_games, dtype=bool)
while playing.any():
    turns += playing
    where = np.where(playing, jump_to[np.minimum(where + rng.integers(1, 7, n_games), 35)], where)
    playing &= where < 30
print(f"30 squares, E[turns]: simulation {turns.mean():.3f}, exact {t[0]:.3f}")
p10 = 1 - np.linalg.matrix_power(Q, 10)[0].sum()
print(f"P(over within 10 turns): simulation {(turns <= 10).mean():.4f}, exact {p10:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
30 squares, E[turns]: simulation 11.601, exact 11.591
P(over within 10 turns): simulation 0.5739, exact 0.5735
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting that time 0 counts as a visit.** The entry $N_{ii}$ is at least 1, because the start itself is a visit. The row sum of $N$ is then exactly the number of steps until absorption, so do not add or subtract 1.
- **Putting absorbing states into $Q$.** $Q$ holds only the transient states. If an absorbing state is in $Q$, then $I - Q$ is singular (no inverse).
- **Counting the snake head as a state.** A square where the token slides at once is never occupied. It is not a state, and its row does not appear in $Q$.
- **Mixing up $N$ with $Q^n$.** The matrix $Q^n$ is the chance of being in each state at time $n$ (not yet absorbed). The matrix $N$ is the sum of all $Q^n$: it counts visits over the whole game.
- **Expecting $NR$ to depend on the number of steps.** $B = NR$ gives the probability of the final trap, whenever it happens. It does not say when.

## Where It Is Used

- **Games:** the expected length of Snakes and Ladders, Monopoly-like games and card games with a fixed end ([21.10](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** in reinforcement learning, an episode ends in a terminal state, which is an absorbing state. The expected episode length and the chance of each outcome come from $N$ and $NR$ ([21.1.5](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)).
- **Computer science:** the expected number of flips until a pattern like HH appears, and the average running time of a randomized algorithm that stops in a goal state ([21.3.1](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.1-Randomized-Algorithms.md)).
- **Biology and medicine:** a patient moves among health states until recovery or death; a gene variant either takes over the population or disappears ([21.9](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.9-Biology-and-Genetics.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a customer journey ends in buy or leave, a job application ends in an offer or a rejection.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** States 1 and 2 are transient and state 3 is absorbing. From each of 1 and 2 the chain goes to the other transient state with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ and to state 3 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Find the expected time to absorption from state 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By symmetry $t_1 = t_2 = t$. First step: one step is used, and with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ we are at the other transient state:

$$t = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t \quad \implies \quad t = 2$$

</details>

**2. (Medium)** Flip a fair coin until you see two heads in a row (HH). Use an absorbing chain with the states "start / last flip was T" (call it 0), "last flip was H" (call it 1) and "HH" (absorbing). Find the expected number of flips.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $t_0$ and $t_1$ be the expected numbers of further flips. From state 0: heads goes to state 1, tails stays at 0. From state 1: heads ends the game, tails goes back to 0.

$$t_0 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_0, \qquad t_1 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_0$$

Put $t_1$ into the first: $t_0 = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} t_0 + \frac{1}{2} t_0 = 1.5 + 0.75 t_0$. So $0.25 t_0 = 1.5$:

$$t_0 = 6$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Take the mini board of Example 1 without the snake (square 3 is an ordinary square). Find the expected number of turns from square 0.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Now the transient states are $0, 1, 2, 3, 4$. From square 4, both moves finish: $t_4 = 1$. From square 3, heads goes to 4 and tails goes to 5 (finish):

$$t_3 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_4 = 1.5$$

$$t_2 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_3 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_4 = 1 + 0.75 + 0.5 = 2.25$$

$$t_1 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_3 = 1 + 1.125 + 0.75 = 2.875$$

$$t_0 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_2 = 1 + 1.4375 + 1.125 = 3.5625$$

The snake in Example 1 raises the expected length from $3.5625$ to $7$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A gambler wins each round with probability $0.6$ and loses with probability $0.4$. The game stops at 0 or at 3 taka. Starting with 1 taka, find the probability of reaching 3 and the expected number of rounds, using $N$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The transient states are 1 and 2. In the order (1, 2), $Q$ has the rows $(0, 0.6)$ and $(0.4, 0)$, so $I - Q$ has the rows $(1, -0.6)$ and $(-0.4, 1)$, with determinant $1 - 0.24 = 0.76$. Inverting a $2 \times 2$ matrix gives $N$ with the rows $\frac{1}{0.76}(1, 0.6)$ and $\frac{1}{0.76}(0.4, 1)$.

Only state 2 can reach the goal 3 directly, with probability $0.6$. So

$$P(\text{reach } 3 \mid \text{start } 1) = N_{12} \times 0.6 = \frac{0.6}{0.76} \times 0.6 = \frac{0.36}{0.76} = \frac{9}{19} \approx 0.4737$$

The expected number of rounds from 1 is the first row sum:

$$t_1 = \frac{1 + 0.6}{0.76} = \frac{1.6}{0.76} \approx 2.105$$

(The formula of [18.2.2](../18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md) gives $\frac{1 - (2/3)}{1 - (2/3)^3} = \frac{9}{19}$, the same.)

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that (a) $\mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1}$ satisfies $(I - Q)\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{1}$, and (b) every row of $B = NR$ sums to 1 (the chain is certain to be absorbed).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Since $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$, we have $(I - Q) N \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$, that is $(I - Q) \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{1}$, which is the first-step equation $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{1} + Q\mathbf{t}$.

(b) Each row of $P$ sums to 1, so for each transient state the sum of the row of $Q$ and the row of $R$ is 1: $Q\mathbf{1} + R\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$, so $R\mathbf{1} = (I - Q)\mathbf{1}$. Then

$$B \mathbf{1} = N R \mathbf{1} = N (I - Q) \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$$

because $N (I - Q) = I$. So the absorption probabilities from each start add to 1.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 2: the expected number of flips until HH is $6$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_games = 200_000

# States: 0 = no useful head yet, 1 = last flip was H, 2 = HH (absorbing).
state = np.zeros(n_games, dtype=int)
flips = np.zeros(n_games, dtype=int)
while (state < 2).any():
    playing = state < 2
    heads = rng.random(n_games) < 0.5
    flips += playing
    # From state 0: H -> 1, T -> 0.  From state 1: H -> 2, T -> 0.
    state = np.where(playing, np.where(heads, state + 1, 0), state)

print(f"expected flips until HH: simulation {flips.mean():.3f}, exact 6.000")
```

Output:

```text
expected flips until HH: simulation 5.996, exact 6.000
```

</details>

## Summary

- An **absorbing state** has $p_{ii} = 1$. In the canonical form of an absorbing chain, the block $Q$ holds the moves among transient states and the block $R$ holds the moves into the traps.
- The chain is absorbed with probability 1, because $Q^n \to 0$.
- The **fundamental matrix** $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$ counts the expected visits to each transient state.
- The expected time to absorption is $\mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1}$, and the absorption probabilities are $B = NR$.
- Snakes and Ladders, gambler's ruin and waiting for a pattern of coin flips are all absorbing chains.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{N = (I - Q)^{-1}, \qquad \mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1}, \qquad B = NR}
$$
