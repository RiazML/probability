# 18.3.1 Markov Property

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.3 Markov Chains](README.md) / 18.3.1 Markov Property

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [18.2.4 Recurrence and Transience](../18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.4-Recurrence-and-Transience.md)

> **Big idea:** A Markov chain has no memory beyond the present: once you know today's state, all earlier days add nothing about tomorrow, so the whole chain is fixed by a starting distribution and one table of one-step probabilities.

---

## Why It Matters

Most real processes depend on a long past, and describing a long past is hard. The **Markov property** cuts the problem down: only the present matters. With it, a process with infinitely many possible histories is described by one small table. This one idea gives us PageRank, speech recognition, queueing models, reinforcement learning and the sampling methods of [Module 19](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/README.md). The rest of this section builds on it.

## Intuition

You want to forecast tomorrow's weather. Today is Cloudy. Does it help to know that yesterday was Rainy? In a Markov model the answer is no. The forecaster has one rule for each kind of today. For a Cloudy day the rule says: tomorrow is Sunny with probability $0.3$, Cloudy with probability $0.5$, and Rainy with probability $0.2$. The rule is the same if yesterday was Sunny, Cloudy or Rainy.

This is the **weather chain** that we use in the whole section. It has three **states** (the possible values): Sunny, Cloudy and Rainy. The probabilities of tomorrow, given today, are:

| Today | Tomorrow Sunny | Tomorrow Cloudy | Tomorrow Rainy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunny | $0.7$ | $0.2$ | $0.1$ |
| Cloudy | $0.3$ | $0.5$ | $0.2$ |
| Rainy | $0.3$ | $0.4$ | $0.3$ |

Each row sums to 1, because tomorrow must be something. A Markov chain has "memory of one day" only. This is not true for real weather, but it is a simple model that works well, and it is easy to repair when it is too simple (see Example 3).

> **Warning:** "No memory" does not mean "no dependence". Tomorrow depends strongly on today. The Markov property says that **the past before today adds nothing once today is known**.

## Picture

```text
   past: X_0, ..., X_{n-1}        present: X_n          future: X_{n+1}
  +-----------------------+      +-----------+
  |  Rainy, Sunny, Sunny, |      |           | --- 0.3 --> Sunny
  |  Cloudy, Rainy, ...   |      |  Cloudy   | --- 0.5 --> Cloudy
  |  (any history at all) |      |  (today)  | --- 0.2 --> Rainy
  +-----------------------+      +-----------+
     ignored by the forecast       the only thing the forecast reads
```

The forecast for tomorrow reads only the present state. The whole history on the left can change, and the three numbers on the right stay the same.

## Definition

Let $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ be a stochastic process ([18.1](../18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)) whose values lie in a finite or countable **state space** $\mathcal{S}$. The process is a **Markov chain** if for every time $n$ and all states $i_0, \dots, i_{n-1}, i, j$ (where the condition has positive probability):

$$
P(X_{n+1} = j \mid X_n = i, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_0 = i_0) = P(X_{n+1} = j \mid X_n = i)
$$

This is the **Markov property**.

**In words:** given the present, the future does not depend on the past.

We always assume the chain is **time-homogeneous**: the right-hand side does not depend on $n$. Then we write it as

$$
p_{ij} = P(X_{n+1} = j \mid X_n = i)
$$

and call $p_{ij}$ the **transition probability** from state $i$ to state $j$. We also write

$$
\mu_0(i) = P(X_0 = i)
$$

for the **initial distribution**, the law of the starting state. The numbers $p_{ij}$ are non-negative, and for every $i$ they satisfy $\sum_j p_{ij} = 1$. We collect them into a matrix in [18.3.2](18.3.2-Transition-Matrix-and-N-Step-Transitions.md).

**The path formula.** For a Markov chain, the probability of a whole path is

$$
P(X_0 = i_0, X_1 = i_1, \dots, X_n = i_n) = \mu_0(i_0) \thinspace p_{i_0 i_1} \thinspace p_{i_1 i_2} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} i_n}
$$

**In words:** the chance of a path is the chance of the starting state times the chance of each step along the way.

> **Note:** The simple random walk of [18.2.1](../18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md) is a Markov chain: $S_{n+1} = S_n + \xi_{n+1}$, and the new step $\xi_{n+1}$ is independent of the whole past. Here $p_{i, i+1} = p$ and $p_{i, i-1} = 1 - p$.

## Why It Is True

The path formula is the multiplication rule ([4.2](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)) with the Markov property applied at every step.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) &= P(X_0 = i_0) \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(X_k = i_k \mid X_{k-1} = i_{k-1}, \dots, X_0 = i_0) && \text{(multiplication rule)} \\
&= \mu_0(i_0) \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(X_k = i_k \mid X_{k-1} = i_{k-1}) && \text{(Markov property)} \\
&= \mu_0(i_0) \prod_{k=1}^{n} p_{i_{k-1} i_k} && \text{(time-homogeneous)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The path formula also shows why "given the present, the past and the future are independent". Cut a path at time $n$, where the chain is in state $i$. The formula splits into two parts: one that holds the past, and one that holds the future.

$$
P(\text{past path}, X_n = i, \text{future path}) = \underbrace{\mu_0(i_0) p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} i}}_{\text{depends only on the past and } i} \times \underbrace{p_{i j_1} p_{j_1 j_2} \cdots p_{j_{m-1} j_m}}_{\text{depends only on } i \text{ and the future}}
$$

The first part equals $P(\text{past path}, X_n = i)$. Divide both sides by $P(X_n = i)$:

$$
P(\text{past path}, \text{future path} \mid X_n = i) = P(\text{past path} \mid X_n = i) \cdot p_{i j_1} \cdots p_{j_{m-1} j_m}
$$

and the last product is exactly $P(\text{future path} \mid X_n = i)$. So the past and the future are independent given the present.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Probability of a Weather Path

**Problem.** Use the weather chain. (a) Today (day 0) is Cloudy. Find the probability that the next three days are Sunny, Sunny, Rainy. (b) The first day is chosen at random with $\mu_0 = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ for (Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy). Find the probability of the path Rainy, Cloudy, Cloudy, Sunny.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The start is fixed, so $\mu_0(\text{Cloudy}) = 1$. The three steps are Cloudy $\to$ Sunny, Sunny $\to$ Sunny, Sunny $\to$ Rainy:

$$
P = p_{CS} \cdot p_{SS} \cdot p_{SR} = 0.3 \times 0.7 \times 0.1 = 0.021
$$

2. (b) The starting state Rainy has probability $0.2$. The steps are Rainy $\to$ Cloudy, Cloudy $\to$ Cloudy, Cloudy $\to$ Sunny:

$$
P = 0.2 \times p_{RC} \times p_{CC} \times p_{CS} = 0.2 \times 0.4 \times 0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.012
$$

**Answer.** (a) $0.021$, about 2.1%. (b) $0.012$, about 1.2%.

**Check.** Both answers are small, as they must be: a specific path of three or four days is only one of many. For (a), the probabilities of all 27 paths of three days from Cloudy add up to 1.

### Example 2: A Text Generator

**Problem.** A tiny text generator writes a sentence one word at a time. It starts with "the". After "the" it writes "cat" or "dog" with probability $0.5$ each. After "cat" it writes "sat" with probability $0.6$ and "ran" with probability $0.4$. After "dog" it writes "sat" with probability $0.3$ and "ran" with probability $0.7$. After "sat" or "ran" the sentence ends ("."). Find the probability of the sentence "the dog ran ." and of any sentence that contains "sat".

**Solution.**

1. This is a Markov chain: the next word depends only on the current word, not on the words before it. The states are the, cat, dog, sat, ran, and "." (the end). The end is a state that the chain never leaves.
2. The sentence "the dog ran ." is the path the $\to$ dog $\to$ ran $\to$ ".":

$$
P = 0.5 \times 0.7 \times 1 = 0.35
$$

3. For "sat", add the two paths that contain it:

$$
P(\text{sat}) = 0.5 \times 0.6 \times 1 + 0.5 \times 0.3 \times 1 = 0.30 + 0.15 = 0.45
$$

**Answer.** $0.35$ and $0.45$.

**Check.** The four sentences are cat-sat $(0.30)$, cat-ran $(0.20)$, dog-sat $(0.15)$ and dog-ran $(0.35)$. They add to $1$. This is the idea behind **bigram** language models: the next word depends on the last word only. Real language is not this simple, and modern models read a long context ([21.1.3](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.3-Language-Models.md)).

### Example 3: Fixing a Process That Is Not Markov

**Problem.** In some town, tomorrow's rain depends on today and yesterday:

- it rained today and yesterday: rain tomorrow with probability $0.6$;
- it rained today but not yesterday: rain tomorrow with probability $0.3$;
- it did not rain today: rain tomorrow with probability $0.1$.

Let $R_n = 1$ if it rains on day $n$. The sequence $R_1, R_2, \dots$ is not a Markov chain. Make it one, and find the probability of rain on day 4, given rain on days 1 and 2.

**Solution.**

1. The sequence $R_n$ is not Markov: the chance of rain tomorrow given "rain today" is $0.6$ or $0.3$, depending on yesterday.
2. Enlarge the state. Let $Y_n = (R_{n-1}, R_n)$, the pair (yesterday, today). Now the forecast is a function of $Y_n$ only, and the next pair is $Y_{n+1} = (R_n, R_{n+1})$. So $Y_n$ is a Markov chain with four states. For example, from the state (rain, rain), the next state is (rain, rain) with probability $0.6$ and (rain, dry) with probability $0.4$.
3. Rain on days 1 and 2 means $Y_2 = (\text{rain}, \text{rain})$. Look at day 3:
   - rain on day 3 (probability $0.6$): then $Y_3 = (\text{rain}, \text{rain})$, and rain on day 4 has probability $0.6$;
   - no rain on day 3 (probability $0.4$): then $Y_3 = (\text{rain}, \text{dry})$, and rain on day 4 has probability $0.1$.
4. Combine the two cases (law of total probability, [4.3](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)):

$$
P(\text{rain on day 4}) = 0.6 \times 0.6 + 0.4 \times 0.1 = 0.36 + 0.04 = 0.40
$$

**Answer.** $0.40$.

**Check.** The answer lies between $0.1$ and $0.6$, as it should. The simulation in Practice 5 gives $0.4002$. A rule that reads the last $k$ days can always be turned into a Markov chain by taking the last $k$ days as the state.

## Simulate It

The code simulates the weather chain from a Sunny day. It checks the path formula, and it checks that "yesterday" does not change the forecast when today is Cloudy.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs = 1_000_000
P = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1],     # from Sunny (0)
              [0.3, 0.5, 0.2],     # from Cloudy (1)
              [0.3, 0.4, 0.3]])    # from Rainy (2)
cum = P.cumsum(axis=1)

# Start every run on a Sunny day (state 0) and simulate 3 days.
x = np.zeros((n_runs, 4), dtype=int)
for n in range(3):
    u = rng.random(n_runs)
    x[:, n + 1] = (u[:, None] > cum[x[:, n]]).sum(axis=1)   # next state from row x_n

# Path probability: P(S, S, C, R) = 0.7 * 0.2 * 0.2
is_path = (x[:, 1] == 0) & (x[:, 2] == 1) & (x[:, 3] == 2)
print(f"P(S,S,C,R): simulation {is_path.mean():.5f}, exact {0.7 * 0.2 * 0.2:.5f}")

# Markov property: P(X_3 = Rainy | X_2 = Cloudy) with different yesterdays X_1.
cloudy_today = x[:, 2] == 1
for yesterday, name in [(0, "Sunny"), (1, "Cloudy"), (2, "Rainy")]:
    sel = cloudy_today & (x[:, 1] == yesterday)
    est = (x[sel, 3] == 2).mean()
    print(f"P(Rainy tomorrow | Cloudy today, {name:6s} yesterday) = {est:.4f}  (n = {sel.sum()})")
print("Exact, whatever yesterday was: 0.2000")
```

Output:

```text
P(S,S,C,R): simulation 0.02811, exact 0.02800
P(Rainy tomorrow | Cloudy today, Sunny  yesterday) = 0.2008  (n = 140021)
P(Rainy tomorrow | Cloudy today, Cloudy yesterday) = 0.2004  (n = 99869)
P(Rainy tomorrow | Cloudy today, Rainy  yesterday) = 0.1983  (n = 40022)
Exact, whatever yesterday was: 0.2000
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking "Markov" means "independent".** Tomorrow depends strongly on today. The Markov property only says that the days before today add nothing. Even $X_{n+2}$ and $X_n$ are dependent: it matters whether it is Sunny or Rainy today when you forecast two days ahead.
- **Forgetting the starting distribution.** The path formula has the factor $\mu_0(i_0)$. A chain is described by $\mu_0$ and the $p_{ij}$ together. Different starts give different laws, with the same rules.
- **Checking the Markov property with a single history.** The property must hold for every history. If the probability changes for even one history, the chain is not Markov in those states (see the cards in Practice 2).
- **Giving up when a process is not Markov.** Put more into the state (the last $k$ days, the cards seen so far, a counter) and the process often becomes Markov, as in Example 3.
- **Mixing it up with Markov's inequality.** The names are alike because both are named after Andrey Markov, but [16.1](../../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.1-Markov-Inequality.md) is a bound on tail probabilities and has nothing to do with chains.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a reinforcement learning agent chooses actions in a **Markov decision process**, where the next state depends only on the current state and action ([21.1.5](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)). Early language models are Markov chains on words ([21.1.3](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.3-Language-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** Google's PageRank models a web surfer who clicks a random link. The next page depends only on the current page ([18.3.8](18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md)).
- **Sampling:** MCMC builds a Markov chain whose long-run behaviour is a target distribution ([19.7](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/README.md)).
- **Biology:** DNA models (hidden Markov models) and population models where the next generation depends on the present one.
- **Everyday life:** board games like Snakes and Ladders, where your next square depends only on your square and the dice ([18.3.7](18.3.7-Absorbing-Chains.md)).

**1. (Easy)** In the weather chain, today (day 0) is Sunny. Find the probability that the next three days are Cloudy, Rainy, Sunny.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The steps are Sunny to Cloudy, Cloudy to Rainy, Rainy to Sunny:

$$P = p_{SC} \cdot p_{CR} \cdot p_{RS} = 0.2 \times 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.012$$

</details>

**2. (Medium)** A box has 2 red and 2 black cards. You draw 3 cards one by one without putting them back. Let $X_n$ be the colour of card $n$. Show that $X_1, X_2, X_3$ is not a Markov chain.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Compare $P(X_3 = \text{red} \mid X_2 = \text{black}, X_1 = \text{black})$ and $P(X_3 = \text{red} \mid X_2 = \text{black}, X_1 = \text{red})$.

After two black cards, the box has 2 red cards left, so the first probability is $\frac{2}{2} = 1$.

After one red and one black card, the box has 1 red and 1 black card left, so the second probability is $\frac{1}{2}$.

The chance of red at step 3, given $X_2 = \text{black}$, changes with $X_1$. The Markov property fails. (The chain of the **number of red cards left** together with the number of cards left would be Markov.)

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the weather chain, start with $\mu_0 = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$ for (Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy). Find $P(X_0 = \text{Rainy}, X_1 = \text{Sunny}, X_2 = \text{Cloudy})$ and $P(X_0 = X_1 = X_2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The path Rainy, Sunny, Cloudy has probability

$$0.5 \times p_{RS} \times p_{SC} = 0.5 \times 0.3 \times 0.2 = 0.03$$

For three equal days, add the paths S,S,S and C,C,C and R,R,R. Each has probability $\mu_0(i) p_{ii}^2$:

$$P = 0.2 \times 0.7^2 + 0.3 \times 0.5^2 + 0.5 \times 0.3^2 = 0.098 + 0.075 + 0.045 = 0.218$$

</details>

**4. (Hard)** Let $X$ be a Markov chain. Prove that for any states $a, i, b$ with $P(X_n = i, X_{n-1} = a) \gt 0$: $P(X_{n-1} = a, X_{n+1} = b \mid X_n = i) = P(X_{n-1} = a \mid X_n = i) \cdot P(X_{n+1} = b \mid X_n = i)$. In words: given today, yesterday and tomorrow are independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the multiplication rule and the Markov property:

$$P(X_{n-1} = a, X_n = i, X_{n+1} = b) = P(X_{n-1} = a, X_n = i) \cdot P(X_{n+1} = b \mid X_n = i, X_{n-1} = a) = P(X_{n-1} = a, X_n = i) \cdot p_{ib}$$

Divide by $P(X_n = i)$:

$$P(X_{n-1} = a, X_{n+1} = b \mid X_n = i) = P(X_{n-1} = a \mid X_n = i) \cdot p_{ib}$$

Since $p_{ib} = P(X_{n+1} = b \mid X_n = i)$, this is the claim.

</details>

**5. (Simulate)** Check Example 3 by simulation: the probability of rain on day 4, given rain on days 1 and 2, is $0.40$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs = 1_000_000

# Rain on day 1 and day 2, then follow the two-day rule for days 3 and 4.
# P(rain tomorrow) = 0.6 if it rained today and yesterday,
#                    0.3 if it rained today but not yesterday,
#                    0.1 if it did not rain today.
def rain_tomorrow(yesterday, today):
    p = np.where(today, np.where(yesterday, 0.6, 0.3), 0.1)
    return rng.random(yesterday.size) < p

day1 = np.ones(n_runs, dtype=bool)
day2 = np.ones(n_runs, dtype=bool)
day3 = rain_tomorrow(day1, day2)
day4 = rain_tomorrow(day2, day3)
print(f"P(rain day 4 | rain days 1, 2): simulation {day4.mean():.4f}, exact {0.6*0.6 + 0.4*0.1:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(rain day 4 | rain days 1, 2): simulation 0.4002, exact 0.4000
```

</details>

## Summary

- A **Markov chain** has the property that, given the present state, the future is independent of the past.
- A time-homogeneous chain is described by the **initial distribution** $\mu_0$ and the **transition probabilities** $p_{ij}$, with $\sum_j p_{ij} = 1$ for every $i$.
- The probability of a path is the starting probability times one transition probability per step.
- A process with a longer memory can be made Markov by putting the recent past into the state.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) = \mu_0(i_0) \thinspace p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} i_n}}
$$
