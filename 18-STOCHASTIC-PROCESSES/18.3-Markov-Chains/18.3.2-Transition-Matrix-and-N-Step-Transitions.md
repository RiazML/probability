# 18.3.2 Transition Matrix and N-Step Transitions

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.3 Markov Chains](README.md) / 18.3.2 Transition Matrix and N-Step Transitions

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [18.3.1 Markov Property](18.3.1-Markov-Property.md)

> **Big idea:** Put the one-step probabilities in a matrix $P$; then the probability of going from $i$ to $j$ in exactly $n$ steps is the entry $(i, j)$ of the matrix power $P^n$, and the law of the chain at time $n$ is the starting row vector times $P^n$.

---

## Why It Matters

The Markov property says that one table of one-step probabilities describes the chain. Now we ask the questions people really care about: what is the weather in three days? How likely is the server to be down at 5 pm? To answer them we must look many steps ahead. Matrix multiplication does exactly this, and a computer does it in one line. The same powers $P^n$ will also show us where the chain settles in the long run ([18.3.5](18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md) and [18.3.8](18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md)).

## Intuition

Today is Sunny. What is the chance that it is Rainy the day after tomorrow? There are three ways to get there, depending on tomorrow:

- Sunny tomorrow, then Rainy: $0.7 \times 0.1 = 0.07$.
- Cloudy tomorrow, then Rainy: $0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.04$.
- Rainy tomorrow, then Rainy: $0.1 \times 0.3 = 0.03$.

These three cases cannot happen together, so we add them: $0.07 + 0.04 + 0.03 = 0.14$. This is the law of total probability ([4.3](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)), with tomorrow's state as the partition. The sum "first step times second step, added over the middle state" is exactly the rule for multiplying matrices. So two steps ahead means $P \times P = P^2$.

## Picture

```text
                    day 1             day 2
                +-- 0.7 --> Sunny  -- 0.1 --> Rainy     0.7 x 0.1 = 0.07
  Sunny --------+-- 0.2 --> Cloudy -- 0.2 --> Rainy     0.2 x 0.2 = 0.04
  (day 0)       +-- 0.1 --> Rainy  -- 0.3 --> Rainy     0.1 x 0.3 = 0.03
                                                         total     = 0.14
```

To go from Sunny to Rainy in two steps, add the three paths through the middle state. That sum is the entry in row Sunny and column Rainy of $P^2$.

## Definition

Number the states $1, 2, \dots, k$. The **transition matrix** of the chain is the $k \times k$ matrix $P$ whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $p_{ij} = P(X_{n+1} = j \mid X_n = i)$. For the weather chain, with the order Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy:

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \cr 0.3 & 0.5 & 0.2 \cr 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Row $i$ of $P$ is the law of tomorrow's state when today is $i$. So every entry is at least $0$ and every row sums to $1$. A matrix with these two properties is called a **stochastic matrix**.

The **$n$-step transition probability** is

$$
p_{ij}^{(n)} = P(X_{m+n} = j \mid X_m = i)
$$

the chance of being in $j$ exactly $n$ steps after being in $i$. It does not depend on $m$ because the chain is time-homogeneous.

**Theorem 1 (Chapman-Kolmogorov).** For all $m, n \ge 0$:

$$
p_{ij}^{(m+n)} = \sum_{l} p_{il}^{(m)} \thinspace p_{lj}^{(n)}, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad P^{m+n} = P^m P^n
$$

**In words:** to go from $i$ to $j$ in $m + n$ steps, go to some state $l$ in $m$ steps, then from $l$ to $j$ in $n$ steps, and add over all the middle states $l$.

**Theorem 2.** The entry $p_{ij}^{(n)}$ is the $(i, j)$ entry of the matrix power $P^n$. If the chain starts with the row vector $\mu_0 = (\mu_0(1), \dots, \mu_0(k))$, then at time $n$ its law is the row vector

$$
\mu_n = \mu_0 P^n
$$

**In words:** to move the whole distribution one day forward, multiply it by $P$ from the left. To move it $n$ days forward, multiply by $P$ a total of $n$ times.

## Why It Is True

**Chapman-Kolmogorov.** Fix the start $X_0 = i$ and the end $X_{m+n} = j$. The state $X_m$ at the middle time is some state $l$. The events $\lbrace X_m = l \rbrace$ for different $l$ cover everything and do not overlap, so by the law of total probability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{ij}^{(m+n)} &= \sum_{l} P(X_{m+n} = j, X_m = l \mid X_0 = i) && \text{(total probability over } X_m \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{l} P(X_m = l \mid X_0 = i) \thinspace P(X_{m+n} = j \mid X_m = l, X_0 = i) && \text{(multiplication rule)} \\
&= \sum_{l} p_{il}^{(m)} \thinspace p_{lj}^{(n)} && \text{(Markov property: forget } X_0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Matrix powers.** For $n = 1$, $p_{ij}^{(1)} = p_{ij}$ is the entry of $P$. Take $m = 1$ in Chapman-Kolmogorov: $p_{ij}^{(n+1)} = \sum_l p_{il} \thinspace p_{lj}^{(n)}$. The right side is the rule for the $(i, j)$ entry of the product of $P$ and the matrix of $n$-step probabilities. By induction on $n$, the matrix of $n$-step probabilities is $P^n$.

**The law at time $n$.** By total probability over the starting state:

$$
P(X_n = j) = \sum_{i} \mu_0(i) \thinspace p_{ij}^{(n)} = (\mu_0 P^n)_j
$$

**Rows still sum to 1.** If $\mathbf{1}$ is the column of ones, then $P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$ (each row sums to 1). So $P^2 \mathbf{1} = P(P\mathbf{1}) = P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$, and in the same way $P^n \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$. Every power of a stochastic matrix is stochastic.

**A two-state chain in closed form.** Let a machine be Up or Down. Each hour, if Up it goes Down with probability $a$, and if Down it comes Up with probability $b$:

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - a & a \cr b & 1 - b \end{pmatrix}
$$

Write $\lambda = 1 - a - b$. Then for all $n \ge 0$:

$$
P^n = \frac{1}{a + b} \begin{pmatrix} b & a \cr b & a \end{pmatrix} + \frac{\lambda^n}{a + b} \begin{pmatrix} a & -a \cr -b & b \end{pmatrix}
$$

To check it, call the two matrices $\Pi$ and $E$ (each with its factor $\frac{1}{a+b}$). Direct multiplication gives $\Pi \Pi = \Pi$, $E E = E$ and $\Pi E = E \Pi = 0$ (the zero matrix). For $n = 1$ the formula gives $P$: the top-left entry is $\frac{b + \lambda a}{a + b} = \frac{b + a - a^2 - ab}{a + b} = \frac{(1 - a)(a + b)}{a + b} = 1 - a$, and the other entries work the same way. So $P = \Pi + \lambda E$. Since the mixed products vanish, $(\Pi + \lambda E)^n = \Pi + \lambda^n E$. As $n$ grows, $\lambda^n \to 0$ (because $\lvert \lambda \rvert \lt 1$ when $0 \lt a + b \lt 2$), and $P^n$ tends to $\Pi$: every row becomes $\left(\frac{b}{a+b}, \frac{a}{a+b}\right)$.

> **Tip:** The number $\lambda$ here is an **eigenvalue** of $P$ (the other one is $1$). The speed at which $P^n$ settles is set by the size of $\lambda$. We use this idea in [18.3.8](18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two and Three Days Ahead

**Problem.** Use the weather chain. (a) Find the row of $P^2$ for a Cloudy start. (b) Today the weather is chosen with $\mu_0 = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$. Find the law of tomorrow's weather. (c) Today is Sunny. Find the law of the weather in 3 days.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The row of $P^2$ for Cloudy is $(0.3, 0.5, 0.2)$ times $P$, which is a sum over the middle state. The first entry is:

$$
p_{CS}^{(2)} = 0.3 \times 0.7 + 0.5 \times 0.3 + 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.21 + 0.15 + 0.06 = 0.42
$$

2. The second and third entries:

$$
p_{CC}^{(2)} = 0.3 \times 0.2 + 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.2 \times 0.4 = 0.06 + 0.25 + 0.08 = 0.39
$$

$$
p_{CR}^{(2)} = 0.3 \times 0.1 + 0.5 \times 0.2 + 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.03 + 0.10 + 0.06 = 0.19
$$

3. (b) Multiply $\mu_0 = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$ by $P$. Each entry is a column of $P$ weighted by $\mu_0$:

$$
\mu_1(\text{Sunny}) = 0.2 \times 0.7 + 0.3 \times 0.3 + 0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.14 + 0.09 + 0.15 = 0.38
$$

$$
\mu_1(\text{Cloudy}) = 0.2 \times 0.2 + 0.3 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.04 + 0.15 + 0.20 = 0.39
$$

$$
\mu_1(\text{Rainy}) = 0.2 \times 0.1 + 0.3 \times 0.2 + 0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.02 + 0.06 + 0.15 = 0.23
$$

4. (c) For a Sunny start, $\mu_0 = (1, 0, 0)$, so the law after $n$ days is row Sunny of $P^n$. Day 2: multiply the first row $(0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$ by $P$:

$$
\mu_2 = (0.49 + 0.06 + 0.03, \quad 0.14 + 0.10 + 0.04, \quad 0.07 + 0.04 + 0.03) = (0.58, 0.28, 0.14)
$$

5. Day 3: multiply $\mu_2$ by $P$ once more:

$$
\mu_3 = (0.406 + 0.084 + 0.042, \quad 0.116 + 0.140 + 0.056, \quad 0.058 + 0.056 + 0.042) = (0.532, 0.312, 0.156)
$$

**Answer.** (a) $(0.42, 0.39, 0.19)$. (b) $(0.38, 0.39, 0.23)$. (c) $(0.532, 0.312, 0.156)$.

**Check.** Each answer sums to 1: $0.42 + 0.39 + 0.19 = 1$, $0.38 + 0.39 + 0.23 = 1$ and $0.532 + 0.312 + 0.156 = 1$. The simulation below reproduces (c).

### Example 2: A Server That Breaks Down

**Problem.** A server is Up or Down at the start of each hour. If Up, it is Down next hour with probability $a = 0.1$. If Down, it is Up next hour with probability $b = 0.5$. The server is Up now. Find the probability that it is Up three hours later, and the long-run probability that it is Up.

**Solution.**

1. Here $\lambda = 1 - a - b = 1 - 0.1 - 0.5 = 0.4$ and $a + b = 0.6$.
2. By the closed form, the (Up, Up) entry of $P^3$ is

$$
p_{UU}^{(3)} = \frac{b}{a + b} + \frac{\lambda^3 a}{a + b} = \frac{0.5}{0.6} + \frac{0.064 \times 0.1}{0.6} = 0.8333 + 0.0107 = 0.8440
$$

3. Check by hand with Chapman-Kolmogorov. First $p_{UU}^{(2)} = 0.9 \times 0.9 + 0.1 \times 0.5 = 0.86$, so $p_{UD}^{(2)} = 0.14$. Then $p_{UU}^{(3)} = 0.86 \times 0.9 + 0.14 \times 0.5 = 0.774 + 0.070 = 0.844$.
4. In the long run $\lambda^n \to 0$, so the chance of Up tends to $\frac{b}{a + b} = \frac{0.5}{0.6} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.8333$, whatever the start.

**Answer.** $0.844$ after three hours, and $\frac{5}{6}$ in the long run.

**Check.** The long-run value is already close to $0.844$: the gap is $\frac{\lambda^3 a}{a + b} = 0.0107$, and it shrinks by the factor $0.4$ each hour.

### Example 3: Looking Four Days Ahead, and Counting Rainy Days

**Problem.** The weather chain starts Sunny. (a) Find $P(\text{Rainy on day 4})$ using $P^4 = P^2 P^2$. (b) Find the expected number of Rainy days among days 1, 2 and 3.

**Solution.**

1. (a) By Chapman-Kolmogorov with $m = n = 2$, we need row Sunny of $P^2$ and column Rainy of $P^2$. The rows of $P^2$ are $(0.58, 0.28, 0.14)$, $(0.42, 0.39, 0.19)$ and $(0.42, 0.38, 0.20)$. So the row is $(0.58, 0.28, 0.14)$ and the column is $(0.14, 0.19, 0.20)$:

$$
p_{SR}^{(4)} = 0.58 \times 0.14 + 0.28 \times 0.19 + 0.14 \times 0.20 = 0.0812 + 0.0532 + 0.0280 = 0.1624
$$

2. (b) Let $I_n = 1$ if day $n$ is Rainy. Then $E[I_n] = p_{SR}^{(n)}$. From Example 1 and the Intuition, $p_{SR}^{(1)} = 0.1$, $p_{SR}^{(2)} = 0.14$ and $p_{SR}^{(3)} = 0.156$. By linearity of expectation ([6.2](../../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)):

$$
E[I_1 + I_2 + I_3] = 0.1 + 0.14 + 0.156 = 0.396
$$

**Answer.** (a) $0.1624$. (b) $0.396$ Rainy days.

**Check.** The matrix power $P^4$ computed by computer has the entry $0.1624$ in row Sunny, column Rainy. The simulation in Practice 6 gives $0.3961$ for (b).

## Simulate It

The code simulates 3 days from Sunny and compares with $P^3$. Then it uses the eigen-decomposition $P = V \operatorname{diag}(\lambda) V^{-1}$, so that $P^n = V \operatorname{diag}(\lambda^n) V^{-1}$, and prints how fast $P^n$ approaches its limit, a matrix whose rows are all $\pi = (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{6})$ (we find $\pi$ in [18.3.5](18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md)).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
P = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.3, 0.5, 0.2], [0.3, 0.4, 0.3]])
cum = P.cumsum(axis=1)

# Simulate 400_000 chains that start Sunny and run 3 days: empirical row of P^3.
n_runs = 400_000
state = np.zeros(n_runs, dtype=int)
for _ in range(3):
    state = (rng.random(n_runs)[:, None] > cum[state]).sum(axis=1)
print("simulated row 1 of P^3:", np.bincount(state, minlength=3) / n_runs)
print("exact     row 1 of P^3:", np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 3)[0])

# Eigen-decomposition: P = V diag(lam) V^-1, so P^n = V diag(lam^n) V^-1.
lam, V = np.linalg.eig(P)
order = np.argsort(-lam)
lam, V = lam[order], V[:, order]
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(lam, 4))
P7 = V @ np.diag(lam**7) @ np.linalg.inv(V)
print("P^7 by eigenvalues equals P^7 by matrix power:", np.allclose(P7, np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 7)))

# How fast does P^n approach its limit? Every row tends to pi = (1/2, 1/3, 1/6).
pi = np.array([1/2, 1/3, 1/6])
for n in (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12):
    err = np.abs(np.linalg.matrix_power(P, n) - pi).max()
    print(f"n = {n:2d}: largest error {err:.2e}   0.4^n = {0.4**n:.2e}")
```

Output:

```text
simulated row 1 of P^3: [0.53252   0.3114025 0.1560775]
exact     row 1 of P^3: [0.532 0.312 0.156]
eigenvalues: [1.  0.4 0.1]
P^7 by eigenvalues equals P^7 by matrix power: True
n =  1: largest error 2.00e-01   0.4^n = 4.00e-01
n =  2: largest error 8.00e-02   0.4^n = 1.60e-01
n =  3: largest error 3.20e-02   0.4^n = 6.40e-02
n =  5: largest error 5.12e-03   0.4^n = 1.02e-02
n =  8: largest error 3.28e-04   0.4^n = 6.55e-04
n = 12: largest error 8.39e-06   0.4^n = 1.68e-05
```

The error falls by the factor $0.4$ at every step, the size of the second eigenvalue. After 12 days the rows of $P^n$ equal the limit to about 5 decimal places. In this table the largest error is $0.5 \times 0.4^n$ every time.

## Common Mistakes

- **Multiplying in the wrong order.** For a row vector the rule is $\mu_n = \mu_0 P^n$, with $\mu_0$ on the left. Writing $P \mu_0$ with a row vector does not even have the right shape, and with a column vector it gives the wrong answer.
- **Squaring each entry.** $P^2$ is the matrix product $P \times P$, not the list of numbers $p_{ij}^2$. In $P^2$ the entries are sums over the middle state.
- **Mixing up rows and columns.** Rows are "from" and columns are "to". Rows sum to 1. Columns do not have to sum to 1 (the columns of the weather matrix sum to $1.3$, $1.1$ and $0.6$).
- **Adding probabilities of the two steps.** The chance of going via one middle state is the product of the two step probabilities. The sum is over different middle states, never over the two steps.
- **Believing that $P^n$ tells about the path.** It gives the chance of being in $j$ at time $n$, not the chance of being in $j$ at every time $1, \dots, n$. For paths, use the path formula of [18.3.1](18.3.1-Markov-Property.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** in reinforcement learning, the value of a state is a sum over future steps weighted by $P^n$ ([21.1.5](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)). A bigram language model predicts $n$ words ahead with $P^n$.
- **Computer science:** repeated multiplication by the web's link matrix is the power method behind PageRank ([18.3.8](18.3.8-Convergence-to-Equilibrium.md)).
- **Reliability:** a machine that is Up or Down each hour, as in Example 2, gives the availability of a service after $n$ hours ([21.7](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)).
- **Weather and forecasting:** multi-day forecasts from a day-to-day transition table ([21.11](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a customer who may switch phone company each year, or a student who is Fit or Ill each week, follow the same table-and-powers recipe.

**3. (Medium)** For the weather chain with $\mu_0 = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$, find $\mu_2 = \mu_1 P$, where $\mu_1 = (0.38, 0.39, 0.23)$ is from Example 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\mu_2(\text{Sunny}) = 0.38 \times 0.7 + 0.39 \times 0.3 + 0.23 \times 0.3 = 0.266 + 0.117 + 0.069 = 0.452$$

$$\mu_2(\text{Cloudy}) = 0.38 \times 0.2 + 0.39 \times 0.5 + 0.23 \times 0.4 = 0.076 + 0.195 + 0.092 = 0.363$$

$$\mu_2(\text{Rainy}) = 0.38 \times 0.1 + 0.39 \times 0.2 + 0.23 \times 0.3 = 0.038 + 0.078 + 0.069 = 0.185$$

The three numbers add to $1$.

**4. (Medium)** A phone is On or Off. If On, it goes Off in the next hour with probability $a = 0.2$. If Off, it goes On with probability $b = 0.6$. Find the probability that an On phone is On two hours later, by Chapman-Kolmogorov and by the closed form, and find the long-run probability of On.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Chapman-Kolmogorov: $p_{OO}^{(2)} = 0.8 \times 0.8 + 0.2 \times 0.6 = 0.64 + 0.12 = 0.76$.

By the closed form, $\lambda = 1 - 0.2 - 0.6 = 0.2$ and $a + b = 0.8$:

$$p_{OO}^{(2)} = \frac{0.6}{0.8} + \frac{0.2^2 \times 0.2}{0.8} = 0.75 + 0.01 = 0.76$$

The two answers agree. The long-run probability of On is $\frac{b}{a + b} = \frac{0.6}{0.8} = 0.75$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_0, X_1, \dots$ be a Markov chain with matrix $P$, and let $Y_n = X_{2n}$ (look at every second step). Show that $Y$ is a Markov chain with transition matrix $P^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fix $n$ and any past $Y_0 = i_0, \dots, Y_{n-1} = i_{n-1}$, that is, $X_0 = i_0, X_2 = i_1, \dots, X_{2n-2} = i_{n-1}$, and $Y_n = X_{2n} = i$. This past is a set of events about times up to $2n$. The Markov property in the form of [18.3.1](18.3.1-Markov-Property.md) says that, given $X_{2n} = i$, everything after time $2n$ is independent of everything before. So

$$P(Y_{n+1} = j \mid Y_n = i, Y_{n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, Y_0 = i_0) = P(X_{2n+2} = j \mid X_{2n} = i) = p_{ij}^{(2)}$$

This does not depend on the earlier $Y$ values or on $n$. So $Y$ is a time-homogeneous Markov chain, and its transition matrix has entries $p_{ij}^{(2)}$, which is the matrix $P^2$ by Theorem 2. In the same way, $X_{kn}$ has matrix $P^k$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 3(b) by simulation: the expected number of Rainy days among the days 1, 2, 3 of a chain that starts Sunny is $0.396$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
P = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.3, 0.5, 0.2], [0.3, 0.4, 0.3]])
cum = P.cumsum(axis=1)

n_runs = 400_000
state = np.zeros(n_runs, dtype=int)          # start Sunny
rainy_days = np.zeros(n_runs)
for _ in range(3):                           # days 1, 2, 3
    state = (rng.random(n_runs)[:, None] > cum[state]).sum(axis=1)
    rainy_days += (state == 2)

exact = sum(np.linalg.matrix_power(P, n)[0, 2] for n in (1, 2, 3))
print(f"E[rainy days in 3 days]: simulation {rainy_days.mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[rainy days in 3 days]: simulation 0.3961, exact 0.3960
```

</details>

## Summary

- The **transition matrix** $P$ has $p_{ij}$ in row $i$, column $j$. Entries are non-negative and each row sums to 1.
- **Chapman-Kolmogorov:** $P^{m+n} = P^m P^n$, which is the law of total probability over the middle state.
- The $n$-step probabilities are the entries of $P^n$, and the law at time $n$ is $\mu_n = \mu_0 P^n$.
- $P^n$ quickly settles: for the weather chain the error falls by the factor $0.4$ (the second eigenvalue) at every step.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\mu_n = \mu_0 P^n, \qquad P^{m+n} = P^m P^n}
$$
