# 18.5 Poisson Process

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](README.md) / 18.5 Poisson Process

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [18.4 Continuous-Time Markov Chains](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md)

> **Big idea:** In a Poisson process, arrivals come at random at a constant rate $\lambda$: the number of arrivals in a time window is Poisson, the gaps between arrivals are independent exponentials, and splitting or merging streams gives new Poisson processes.

---

## Why It Matters

Calls to a help desk, requests to a web server, radioactive decays, insurance claims, typos in a long text: all of them are events that arrive at random times. The **Poisson process** is the standard model. It joins two ideas you know: the Poisson count of [7.5](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.5-Poisson.md) and the exponential waiting time of [9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md). It is also the simplest continuous-time chain of [18.4](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md) (a chain that only jumps up), and it is the arrival stream of the queues in [18.7](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md).

## Intuition

A help desk gets 4 calls per hour on average. The calls do not come in a neat line, one every 15 minutes. Sometimes two calls come in one minute, and sometimes nothing happens for an hour. Why does the Poisson count come out?

Cut one hour into 3600 seconds. In each second, a call arrives with a tiny probability, about $\frac{4}{3600}$, and the seconds do not influence each other. The number of calls is then a binomial with 3600 trials and a tiny success probability, which is almost exactly a Poisson ([7.10](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.10-Poisson-Approximation-to-Binomial.md)). The same picture gives the waiting time. The wait for the next call is the number of quiet seconds before the first call, and this is a geometric number that becomes an exponential time when the seconds get very short. So there are two views of one process: **count the arrivals** in a window (Poisson), or **time the gaps** between arrivals (exponential).

## Picture

```text
  N(t)
   4 |                                  +---------
   3 |                         +--------+
   2 |                  +------+
   1 |         +--------+
   0 +---------+--------+------+--------+--------------> t
     0         S1       S2     S3       S4
     |---T1----|--T2----|-T3---|--T4----|
```

The count $N(t)$ is a staircase that goes up by 1 at each arrival time $S_1, S_2, \dots$. The gaps $T_1, T_2, \dots$ between arrivals are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$.

## Definition

Let $N(t)$ be the number of arrivals in the time interval $[0, t]$. It is a **counting process**: it starts at 0 and only goes up, by steps of 1. It is a **Poisson process with rate** $\lambda$ if:

1. $N(0) = 0$.
2. It has **independent increments**: the numbers of arrivals in windows that do not overlap are independent ([18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)).
3. For all $s, t \ge 0$, the number of arrivals in a window of length $t$ is Poisson:

$$
N(s + t) - N(s) \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda t)
$$

Here $\lambda$ is the **rate**: the mean number of arrivals per unit time. The mean number in a window of length $t$ is $\lambda t$.

**In words:** arrivals come at a constant average rate, and what happens in one window tells you nothing about what happens in another window.

**Arrival times and gaps.** Let $S_n$ be the time of the $n$-th arrival, and $T_n = S_n - S_{n-1}$ (with $S_0 = 0$) the $n$-th **inter-arrival time**. The main theorem says that the process is also described by its gaps:

$$
T_1, T_2, \dots \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(\lambda), \qquad S_n = T_1 + \dots + T_n \sim \text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)
$$

Here $\lambda$ is the rate, so a gap has mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ and $S_n$ has mean $\frac{n}{\lambda}$. The two descriptions are equivalent: if you build a process from i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ gaps, its counts satisfy the three rules above, and the other way round.

> **Note:** The rate must be constant. If the rate changes with time (a busy hour and a quiet hour), the process is a **non-homogeneous** Poisson process, and the rules above do not hold.

## Why It Is True

### Inter-Arrival Times Are Exponential

The first arrival is later than $t$ exactly when the window $[0, t]$ has no arrivals:

$$
P(T_1 \gt t) = P(N(t) = 0) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^0}{0!} = e^{-\lambda t}
$$

This is the survival function of $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, so $T_1 \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$.

Now the second gap. Fix the time $s$ of the first arrival. After time $s$ the process counts arrivals in new windows $(s, s + u]$. By independent increments, these counts do not depend on what happened up to $s$, and by rule 3 they have the same law as at the start. So the process after $S_1$ is a fresh Poisson process with the same rate, and it is independent of $T_1$. Its first arrival is $T_2$, so $T_2 \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and $T_2$ is independent of $T_1$. Repeat the argument at $S_2$, $S_3$, and so on. So the gaps are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, and $S_n$ is a sum of $n$ of them, which is $\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$ by [9.4](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md).

### Exponential Gaps Give Poisson Counts

Now go the other way. Start with i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ gaps. The count $N(t)$ is at least $n$ exactly when the $n$-th arrival is by time $t$, so $N(t) = n$ means $S_n \le t \lt S_{n+1}$. Let $G_n(t) = P(S_n \gt t)$. We claim that

$$
G_n(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^k}{k!}
$$

To check it, differentiate the right side. Each term gives $-\lambda \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^k}{k!} + \lambda \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}$ (the second part is absent for $k = 0$), and in the sum over $k = 0, \dots, n - 1$ all terms cancel in pairs except the last one:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} G_n(t) = -\lambda \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} = -f_{S_n}(t)
$$

which is minus the $\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$ density. Both sides equal 1 at $t = 0$. So the claim holds. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(N(t) = n) &= P(S_n \le t \lt S_{n+1}) = P(S_{n+1} \gt t) - P(S_n \gt t) && \text{(the event } S_n \gt t \text{ is inside } S_{n+1} \gt t \text{)} \\
&= G_{n+1}(t) - G_n(t) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^n}{n!} && \text{(only the last term of } G_{n+1} \text{ is left)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $N(t) \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda t)$. The independence of increments follows from the same fresh-start argument.

### Splitting (Thinning)

Each arrival is **type A** with probability $p$ and **type B** with probability $1 - p$, independently of everything else. Write $q = 1 - p$. Let $N_A(t)$ and $N_B(t)$ be the counts of the two types. Given $N(t) = a + b$ arrivals, the number of type A is binomial, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(N_A = a, N_B = b) &= P(N = a + b) \binom{a + b}{a} p^a q^b && \text{(binomial split of } a + b \text{ arrivals)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^{a+b}}{(a + b)!} \cdot \frac{(a + b)!}{a! \thinspace b!} p^a q^b && \text{(Poisson PMF)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-p \lambda t} (p \lambda t)^a}{a!} \cdot \frac{e^{-q \lambda t} (q \lambda t)^b}{b!} && \text{(use } e^{-\lambda t} = e^{-p\lambda t} e^{-q \lambda t} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The result is a product of a $\text{Pois}(p \lambda t)$ PMF and a $\text{Pois}(q \lambda t)$ PMF. So $N_A(t)$ and $N_B(t)$ are **independent** Poisson variables. The full statement (not proved here) is that type A and type B are two **independent Poisson processes** with rates $p \lambda$ and $(1 - p) \lambda$.

**In words:** if you split a Poisson stream by independent coin flips, each part is again Poisson, and the parts do not influence each other, even though they come from one stream.

### Merging (Superposition)

Two independent Poisson processes with rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ run side by side, and we count all arrivals together. In every window of length $t$, the total count is a sum of two independent Poisson variables, which is $\text{Pois}((\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) t)$ ([15.3](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)). Counts in disjoint windows stay independent. So the **merged process** is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. By the competing-clocks result of [18.4](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md), each arrival of the merged process comes from process 1 with probability

$$
\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}
$$

independently of the others.

**In words:** rates add when you merge, and rates split by coin flips when you thin. Merging and splitting are two directions of the same fact.

### Given the Count, the Arrival Times Are Uniform

Suppose we know that exactly $n$ arrivals happened in $[0, t]$. Where are they? The answer is surprising: **they are $n$ independent uniform points, listed in order.**

**Theorem.** Given $N(t) = n$, the vector $(S_1, \dots, S_n)$ has the same law as the sorted values of $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Unif}(0, t)$ variables.

**Proof.** The arrival times are partial sums of the gaps, so for $0 \lt s_1 \lt \dots \lt s_n$ the joint density of $(S_1, \dots, S_n)$ is the product of the gap densities (the change of variables from gaps to arrival times has Jacobian 1):

$$
f(s_1, \dots, s_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda e^{-\lambda (s_i - s_{i-1})} = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda s_n} \qquad (s_0 = 0)
$$

The event $N(t) = n$ means that $S_n \le t$ and that the next gap is long enough: $S_{n+1} \gt t$, which has probability $e^{-\lambda (t - s_n)}$ given $S_n = s_n$. So the joint density of the arrival times together with the event $N(t) = n$ is

$$
\lambda^n e^{-\lambda s_n} \cdot e^{-\lambda (t - s_n)} = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda t} \qquad (0 \lt s_1 \lt \dots \lt s_n \le t)
$$

Divide by $P(N(t) = n) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^n}{n!}$:

$$
f(s_1, \dots, s_n \mid N(t) = n) = \frac{\lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}}{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^n / n!} = \frac{n!}{t^n}
$$

This is constant on the ordered region. It is the density of the sorted values of $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Unif}(0, t)$ variables: the unsorted vector has density $\frac{1}{t^n}$, and there are $n!$ orderings that give the same sorted vector ([13.7](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.7-Order-Statistics.md)).

**In words:** the rate $\lambda$ disappears. Once you know how many arrivals there were, each one is a uniform random point in the window. Useful facts follow: the $k$-th arrival has mean $\frac{kt}{n+1}$ (the $k$-th smallest of $n$ uniforms, [13.7](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.7-Order-Statistics.md)), and the number of arrivals in a sub-window of length $u$ is $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{u}{t})$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Calls to a Help Desk

**Problem.** Calls arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda = 4$ per hour. Find (a) the probability of no call in 30 minutes, (b) the probability of at least 3 calls in one hour, (c) the mean time and standard deviation of the time of the 5th call, (d) the probability that the next call comes within 10 minutes.

**Solution.**

1. (a) Thirty minutes is $t = 0.5$ hours, so $N \sim \text{Pois}(4 \times 0.5) = \text{Pois}(2)$:

$$
P(N = 0) = e^{-2} = 0.1353
$$

2. (b) In one hour $N \sim \text{Pois}(4)$. Use the complement:

$$
P(N \ge 3) = 1 - e^{-4}\left(1 + 4 + \frac{4^2}{2}\right) = 1 - 13 e^{-4} = 1 - 13 \times 0.018316 = 0.7619
$$

3. (c) The 5th arrival time is $S_5 \sim \text{Gamma}(5, 4)$, with mean $\frac{5}{4} = 1.25$ hours (75 minutes) and variance $\frac{5}{4^2}$, so the standard deviation is $\frac{\sqrt{5}}{4} = 0.559$ hours (about 33.5 minutes).
4. (d) Ten minutes is $\frac{1}{6}$ hour. The wait is $\text{Exp}(4)$:

$$
P(T \le \tfrac{1}{6}) = 1 - e^{-4/6} = 1 - e^{-0.6667} = 1 - 0.5134 = 0.4866
$$

**Answer.** (a) $0.1353$. (b) $0.7619$. (c) 75 minutes, standard deviation about 33.5 minutes. (d) $0.4866$.

**Check.** The mean gap is $\frac{1}{4}$ hour (15 minutes), and the chance that a gap is shorter than 10 minutes is a bit less than half, which fits: an exponential has median $\frac{\ln 2}{4} = 0.173$ hours $\approx$ 10.4 minutes.

### Example 2: Splitting and Merging

**Problem.** (a) Emails reach an inbox at rate 20 per hour. Each email is spam with probability $0.4$, independently. Find the probability of no spam in 15 minutes, the probability of at least one good email in 15 minutes, and the probability that there is no email at all in 15 minutes. (b) Two data centres send error alerts to one dashboard, independently: centre A at rate 2 per hour and centre B at rate 3 per hour. Find the probability that the next alert is from A, and the probability of no alert in 12 minutes.

**Solution.**

1. (a) By splitting, spam is a Poisson process with rate $0.4 \times 20 = 8$ per hour, and good email has rate $0.6 \times 20 = 12$ per hour, independent of spam. In 15 minutes ($0.25$ hour) the means are $2$ and $3$.
2. No spam: $e^{-2} = 0.1353$. At least one good email: $1 - e^{-3} = 1 - 0.0498 = 0.9502$.
3. No email at all: by independence, $e^{-2} \cdot e^{-3} = e^{-5} = 0.0067$. Directly, the stream has rate 20, so the mean in 15 minutes is $5$, and $P(N = 0) = e^{-5}$. The two answers agree.
4. (b) By merging, the dashboard sees a Poisson process with rate $2 + 3 = 5$ per hour. The next alert is from A with probability $\frac{2}{2 + 3} = 0.4$.
5. Twelve minutes is $0.2$ hours, so the mean count is $5 \times 0.2 = 1$ and $P(\text{no alert}) = e^{-1} = 0.3679$.

**Answer.** (a) $0.1353$, $0.9502$ and $0.0067$. (b) $0.4$ and $0.3679$.

**Check.** In (a), $P(\text{no email}) = e^{-5}$ equals the product of the two independent chances, as splitting says it must. If spam and good email were not independent, this equality would fail.

### Example 3: Arrival Times Given the Count

**Problem.** A server log shows exactly 3 requests between 10:00 and 11:00. Requests are a Poisson process. Find (a) the probability that all 3 arrived in the first 15 minutes, (b) the mean time of the first request, (c) the probability that the last request arrived after 10:50, (d) the probability that exactly one request arrived in the first 15 minutes.

**Solution.** Given the count, the 3 requests are 3 independent $\text{Unif}(0, 60)$ points (minutes after 10:00). The rate $\lambda$ is not needed.

1. (a) Each point is in the first 15 minutes with probability $\frac{15}{60} = \frac{1}{4}$, independently:

$$
P = \left( \tfrac{1}{4} \right)^3 = \tfrac{1}{64} = 0.0156
$$

2. (b) The first request is the smallest of 3 uniforms, with mean $\frac{60}{3 + 1} = 15$ minutes, so 10:15 on average.
3. (c) The last request is before 10:50 when all 3 points are in the first 50 minutes, which has probability $\left( \frac{50}{60} \right)^3 = \left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^3 = \frac{125}{216}$. So

$$
P(\text{last after 10:50}) = 1 - \frac{125}{216} = \frac{91}{216} = 0.4213
$$

4. (d) The number of points in the first 15 minutes is $\text{Bin}(3, \frac{1}{4})$:

$$
P(\text{exactly one}) = 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \left( \tfrac{3}{4} \right)^2 = \tfrac{27}{64} = 0.4219
$$

**Answer.** (a) $\frac{1}{64}$. (b) 15 minutes after 10:00. (c) $\frac{91}{216} \approx 0.4213$. (d) $\frac{27}{64} \approx 0.4219$.

**Check.** The simulation below uses a window of length 3 with 3 arrivals. It gives a mean first arrival of $0.7500 = \frac{3}{3 + 1}$ and a mean last arrival of $2.2552 \approx \frac{3 \cdot 3}{3 + 1}$, which agree with the formula $\frac{kt}{n+1}$.

## Simulate It

The code builds the process from exponential gaps and checks that the gaps look exponential, that $N(5)$ has mean and variance 10, that thinning gives uncorrelated counts, and that given $N(3) = 3$ the arrivals behave like sorted uniform points.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import exp, factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs, rate, t_end = 100_000, 2.0, 5.0

# Build the process from its inter-arrival times: i.i.d. Exp(rate) gaps, added up.
gaps = rng.exponential(1 / rate, size=(n_runs, 40))
arrivals = gaps.cumsum(axis=1)
counts = (arrivals <= t_end).sum(axis=1)             # N(5)

print(f"gap > 1:     simulation {np.mean(gaps > 1):.4f}, exact {exp(-rate):.4f}")
print(f"gap <= 0.25: simulation {np.mean(gaps <= 0.25):.4f}, exact {1 - exp(-0.5):.4f}")
print(f"N(5) mean {counts.mean():.3f}, variance {counts.var():.3f} (both exact 10)")
print(f"P(N(5) = 10): simulation {np.mean(counts == 10):.4f}, exact {exp(-10) * 10**10 / factorial(10):.4f}")

# Thinning: keep each arrival with probability 0.3 -> two independent Poisson counts.
keep = rng.random(arrivals.shape) < 0.3
kept = (keep & (arrivals <= t_end)).sum(axis=1)
dropped = counts - kept
print(f"kept mean {kept.mean():.3f} (exact 3), dropped mean {dropped.mean():.3f} (exact 7)")
print(f"correlation of kept and dropped: {np.corrcoef(kept, dropped)[0, 1]:.4f} (exact 0)")

# Order statistics: rate 1, window [0, 3]. Given N(3) = 3, the first arrival is the smallest of 3 uniforms.
arr1 = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_runs, 30)).cumsum(axis=1)
three = (arr1 <= 3).sum(axis=1) == 3
print(f"given N(3)=3: mean first arrival {arr1[three, 0].mean():.4f} (exact 0.75)")
print(f"given N(3)=3: mean last arrival  {arr1[three, 2].mean():.4f} (exact 2.25)")
```

Output:

```text
gap > 1:     simulation 0.1352, exact 0.1353
gap <= 0.25: simulation 0.3937, exact 0.3935
N(5) mean 10.002, variance 10.020 (both exact 10)
P(N(5) = 10): simulation 0.1234, exact 0.1251
kept mean 2.997 (exact 3), dropped mean 7.006 (exact 7)
correlation of kept and dropped: 0.0019 (exact 0)
given N(3)=3: mean first arrival 0.7500 (exact 0.75)
given N(3)=3: mean last arrival  2.2552 (exact 2.25)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing units.** If the rate is 4 per hour and the window is 30 minutes, the mean count is $4 \times 0.5 = 2$, not $4 \times 30$. Always change the window to the unit of the rate.
- **Expecting evenly spaced arrivals.** With a rate of 4 per hour the gaps are not all 15 minutes. The exponential density is largest at 0, so short gaps are the most common, and clusters and long quiet spells both appear (the simulation shows $P(\text{gap} \le 0.25) = 0.39$ when the mean gap is $0.5$).
- **Thinking an arrival is "due".** After a long quiet spell the next arrival is not more likely. The exponential gap is memoryless ([9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)), and counts in disjoint windows are independent.
- **Adding probabilities when merging.** Rates add ($\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$), not probabilities. And when splitting, the rates are $p \lambda$ and $(1 - p) \lambda$, so each part is slower than the whole.
- **Using a constant rate when the rate changes.** A server that is busy at noon and quiet at night does not give one Poisson process. Use a separate rate for each period.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** streams of events (clicks, purchases, messages) are modelled as point processes, and the Poisson process is the base model; arrivals of requests to an inference server are often assumed Poisson ([21.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/README.md)).
- **Computer science:** packet and request arrivals in networks and web servers, the input of the queue in [18.7](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md) ([21.3.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)). Splitting is how a load balancer divides traffic.
- **Insurance and finance:** the number of claims in a year is modelled as a Poisson process, and so are rare jumps in a price ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Physics and biology:** radioactive decays, photons hitting a detector, and mutations along a genome ([21.8](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.8-Physics.md)).
- **Everyday life:** phone calls to a shop, customers at a counter, and typos in a long document.

**3. (Medium)** Two independent Poisson streams have rates 2 and 3 per minute. Find the probability that the first three arrivals of the merged stream all come from stream 1, and the probability of no arrival in 6 seconds.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The merged stream has rate $5$ per minute. Each arrival comes from stream 1 with probability $\frac{2}{5}$, independently:

$$P(\text{first three from stream 1}) = \left( \tfrac{2}{5} \right)^3 = \tfrac{8}{125} = 0.064$$

Six seconds is $0.1$ minute, so the mean count is $5 \times 0.1 = 0.5$:

$$P(\text{no arrival}) = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Customers enter a shop as a Poisson process at rate 12 per hour. Each customer buys something with probability $0.25$, independently. Find the probability of no sale in one hour, and the mean number of sales in an 8-hour day.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Sales are a thinned Poisson process with rate $12 \times 0.25 = 3$ per hour. In one hour:

$$P(\text{no sale}) = e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$$

In 8 hours the number of sales is $\text{Pois}(24)$, with mean $24$.

**5. (Hard)** Let $N_1 \sim \text{Pois}(\mu_1)$ and $N_2 \sim \text{Pois}(\mu_2)$ be independent. Show that given $N_1 + N_2 = n$, the count $N_1$ is binomial with parameters $n$ and $\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_1 + \mu_2}$. Why does this explain the order-statistics theorem for a sub-window?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The sum $N_1 + N_2$ is $\text{Pois}(\mu_1 + \mu_2)$. For $0 \le k \le n$:

$$P(N_1 = k \mid N_1 + N_2 = n) = \frac{P(N_1 = k) P(N_2 = n - k)}{P(N_1 + N_2 = n)} = \frac{e^{-\mu_1} \mu_1^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{e^{-\mu_2} \mu_2^{n-k}}{(n-k)!} \cdot \frac{n!}{e^{-(\mu_1 + \mu_2)} (\mu_1 + \mu_2)^n}$$

The exponentials cancel and the rest is

$$\binom{n}{k} \left( \frac{\mu_1}{\mu_1 + \mu_2} \right)^k \left( \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1 + \mu_2} \right)^{n-k}$$

which is the $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{\mu_1}{\mu_1 + \mu_2})$ PMF. For a Poisson process, take the sub-window of length $u$ and the rest of $[0, t]$ as two independent counts with means $\lambda u$ and $\lambda (t - u)$. Given $n$ arrivals in all, the number in the sub-window is $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{u}{t})$, as if each arrival were an independent uniform point.

</details>

## Summary

- A Poisson process with rate $\lambda$ has independent increments, and the count in a window of length $t$ is $\text{Pois}(\lambda t)$.
- Equivalently, the gaps between arrivals are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, and the $n$-th arrival time is $\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$.
- Splitting by independent coin flips gives independent Poisson processes with rates $p \lambda$ and $(1 - p) \lambda$. Merging independent Poisson processes adds the rates.
- Given $N(t) = n$, the arrival times are $n$ independent $\text{Unif}(0, t)$ points, in order.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{N(t) \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda t), \qquad T_1, T_2, \dots \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(\lambda)}
$$
