# 18.2.2 Gambler's Ruin

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.2 Random Walks](README.md) / 18.2.2 Gambler's Ruin

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [18.2.1 Simple Random Walk](18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md) and [4.11.5 Gambler's Ruin](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md)

> **Big idea:** A random walk between two walls ends with probability 1, it lasts $k(N-k)$ steps on average when the game is fair, and against an opponent with unlimited money a fair game means certain ruin, but one that can take an infinitely long time on average.

---

## Why It Matters

In [4.11.5](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md) you found the chance $w_k$ of reaching the goal $N$ before 0: it is $\frac{k}{N}$ in a fair game and $\frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}$ with $r = \frac{q}{p}$ in a biased one. That file left three questions open. **How long** does the game last? Does it **always** end? And what happens when there is **no upper wall**, as when you play against a casino or a market with unlimited money? These questions decide how long a server buffer takes to fill or empty, and whether an insurance company survives. They are answered with the same tool, first-step analysis ([14.3](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)).

## Intuition

You have 1 taka, your opponent has 99 taka, and you flip a fair coin for 1 taka each round until one of you has all 100. You win only 1 time in 100. So you expect the game to be very short: you usually lose in the first few rounds. But the average length is exactly **99 rounds**. The reason is the rare games where you climb: a few games last thousands of rounds and pull the average up.

Now give each player 50 taka. The game is still fair, but now it lasts $50 \times 50 = 2{,}500$ rounds on average. The longest games happen when the two players are equal, because then neither wall is near.

A different question: what if the opponent has unlimited money? In a fair game you are sure to go broke eventually, because the walk will wander far enough down to touch 0. But the average waiting time for ruin is infinite. Ruin is certain, yet you cannot say when.

## Definition

Keep the setup of [4.11.5](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md). A walker starts at $k$ with $0 \le k \le N$ and moves $+1$ with probability $p$ and $-1$ with probability $q = 1 - p$ ([18.2.1](18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md)). Let $T$ be the first time the walk is at 0 or at $N$. Write $r = \frac{q}{p}$, let $w_k = P(\text{reach } N \text{ before } 0)$ as before, and define the **expected duration**

$$
D_k = E[T \mid \text{start at } k]
$$

**In words:** $D_k$ is the average number of rounds until the game stops.

**Result 1 (the game ends).** For every $p \in (0, 1)$, $P(T \lt \infty) = 1$, and $D_k$ is finite.

**Result 2 (expected duration).**

$$
D_k = k(N - k) \quad (p = \tfrac{1}{2}), \qquad D_k = \frac{k - N w_k}{q - p} \quad (p \ne \tfrac{1}{2})
$$

**In words:** in a fair game the duration is the product of the two stakes. In a biased game it is the distance the walk must travel on average, divided by the drift per round.

**Result 3 (no upper wall).** Let $T_0$ be the time of ruin when the walker has no upper goal. Then

$$
P(\text{ever ruined}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } p \le \tfrac{1}{2} \\
\left(\dfrac{q}{p}\right)^{k} & \text{if } p \gt \tfrac{1}{2}
\end{cases}
$$

and the average time to ruin is

$$
E[T_0] =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{k}{q - p} & \text{if } p \lt \tfrac{1}{2} \\
\infty & \text{if } p = \tfrac{1}{2}
\end{cases}
$$

**In words:** with a fair or unfavorable game, ruin is certain. With a favorable game you survive forever with probability $1 - r^k$. If the game is unfavorable, ruin comes on average after "distance over drift" rounds, and if it is exactly fair, the average waiting time is infinite.

> **Note:** Both $w_k$ and $D_k$ have a two-line proof with martingales ([20.8](../../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.8-Martingales.md)) and the optional stopping theorem ([20.9](../../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.9-Stopping-Times-and-Optional-Stopping.md)). The process $(q/p)^{S_n}$ is a martingale and gives $w_k$. The process $S_n - n(p - q)$ is a martingale and gives $D_k$. We only mention this here and use first-step equations, which need no extra theory.

## Why It Is True

**Result 1: the game ends.** From any position $j$ with $0 \lt j \lt N$, a run of $N$ up-steps in a row brings the walk to $N$ or the game has already ended. That run has probability $p^N = \delta \gt 0$. Split time into blocks of $N$ rounds. If the game is still running at the start of a block, it ends within the block with probability at least $\delta$. So

$$
P(T \gt mN) \le (1 - \delta)^m \to 0 \quad (m \to \infty)
$$

Hence $T$ is finite with probability 1. Also $E[T] = \sum_{n \ge 0} P(T \gt n) \le N \sum_{m \ge 0} P(T \gt mN) \le \frac{N}{\delta} \lt \infty$, because each block of $N$ times has at most $N$ terms, all at most $P(T \gt mN)$. So $D_k$ is finite, and we may write equations for it.

**The first-step equation.** For $0 \lt k \lt N$, condition on the first round. With probability $p$ we move to $k + 1$, with probability $q$ to $k - 1$, and in both cases one round has been used and the game restarts ([14.3](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)):

$$
D_k = 1 + p D_{k+1} + q D_{k-1}, \qquad D_0 = D_N = 0
$$

**In words:** the expected duration is one round plus the expected remaining duration from wherever the first round takes us.

**Result 2, fair game.** Put $p = q = \frac{1}{2}$ and rearrange: $D_{k+1} - 2D_k + D_{k-1} = -2$. Let $e_k = D_{k+1} - D_k$. Then $e_k - e_{k-1} = -2$, so $e_k = e_0 - 2k$. Add up the steps:

$$
D_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} e_j = k e_0 - 2 \cdot \frac{k(k-1)}{2} = k e_0 - k(k-1)
$$

The wall $D_N = 0$ gives $N e_0 = N(N-1)$, so $e_0 = N - 1$ and

$$
D_k = k(N-1) - k(k-1) = k(N - k)
$$

**Result 2, biased game.** Take $p \ne q$. The ruin probabilities satisfy their own equation $w_k = p w_{k+1} + q w_{k-1}$ from [4.11.5](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md), with $w_0 = 0$ and $w_N = 1$. Test the claim $D_k = \frac{k - N w_k}{q - p}$ in the right side of the first-step equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 + p D_{k+1} + q D_{k-1} &= 1 + \frac{p(k+1) + q(k-1) - N(p w_{k+1} + q w_{k-1})}{q - p} && \text{(insert the claim)} \\
&= 1 + \frac{k + (p - q) - N w_k}{q - p} && \text{(} p + q = 1 \text{ and the equation for } w_k \text{)} \\
&= 1 - 1 + \frac{k - N w_k}{q - p} = D_k && \text{(since } \tfrac{p - q}{q - p} = -1 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The walls also hold: $D_0 = \frac{0 - 0}{q-p} = 0$ and $D_N = \frac{N - N \cdot 1}{q - p} = 0$. The solution is unique, because the difference of two solutions solves $e_k = p e_{k+1} + q e_{k-1}$ with $e_0 = e_N = 0$, and the maximum argument of Practice 5 in [4.11.5](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md) shows that $e_k = 0$ for all $k$.

**Result 3: no upper wall.** The event **ruin happens before the walk reaches $N$** grows as $N$ grows, and its union over all $N$ is the event **ruin ever happens** (a ruin at a finite time has a finite maximum before it). By continuity of probability ([3.9](../../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.9-Continuity-of-Probability.md)), $P(\text{ever ruined}) = \lim_{N \to \infty} (1 - w_k)$.

- If $p \gt \frac{1}{2}$, then $r \lt 1$ and $r^N \to 0$, so $w_k \to 1 - r^k$ and the limit of $1 - w_k$ is $r^k$.
- If $p \lt \frac{1}{2}$, then $r \gt 1$ and $w_k = \frac{r^k - 1}{r^N - 1} \to 0$, so ruin has limit 1.
- If $p = \frac{1}{2}$, then $w_k = \frac{k}{N} \to 0$, so ruin has limit 1.

For the time of ruin: the stopped times $T$ increase to $T_0$ as $N \to \infty$ (when ruin is certain), so $E[T] \to E[T_0]$ by monotone convergence. If $p \lt \frac{1}{2}$, then $N w_k = \frac{N(r^k - 1)}{r^N - 1} \to 0$, so $D_k \to \frac{k}{q - p}$. If $p = \frac{1}{2}$, then $D_k = k(N - k) \to \infty$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Fair Game, Start With 3 Taka Against 7

**Problem.** You have 3 taka, your friend 7 taka, and you flip a fair coin for 1 taka each round until one of you has everything. How long does the game last on average? First test the formula on the small case $N = 4$, $k = 2$.

**Solution.**

1. Small case, $N = 4$. By symmetry $D_1 = D_3$. The equations are $D_1 = 1 + \frac{1}{2} D_2$ and $D_2 = 1 + \frac{1}{2} D_1 + \frac{1}{2} D_3 = 1 + D_1$.
2. Put $D_1 = 1 + \frac{1}{2} D_2$ into the second: $D_2 = 1 + 1 + \frac{1}{2} D_2$, so $\frac{1}{2} D_2 = 2$ and $D_2 = 4$, $D_1 = 3$. The formula gives $2 \times 2 = 4$ and $1 \times 3 = 3$. It agrees.
3. Real case, $N = 10$ and $k = 3$:

$$
D_3 = 3 \times (10 - 3) = 21 \text{ rounds}
$$

**Answer.** The game lasts 21 rounds on average. You win with probability $0.3$ (from 4.11.5).

**Check.** The table in "Simulate It" shows an average of $21.12$ in simulation. The poor player (you) usually loses fast, yet the average of 21 is much more than the 3 rounds of the quickest loss.

### Example 2: A Server Buffer (a Biased Walk)

**Problem.** A buffer holds up to 10 packets. In each tick, a packet arrives with probability $p = 0.45$ and one packet leaves with probability $q = 0.55$ (we count a tick as a single up or down move). It starts with 3 packets. The buffer **overflows** if it reaches 10 and **runs dry** if it reaches 0. Find the chance of overflow and the expected time until one of the two happens.

**Solution.**

1. The load is a walk with $p = 0.45$, $r = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{11}{9} \approx 1.2222$, $N = 10$, $k = 3$.
2. The overflow probability is $w_3 = \frac{1 - r^3}{1 - r^{10}}$. We have $r^3 \approx 1.8258$ and $r^{10} \approx 7.4388$, so

$$
w_3 = \frac{1 - 1.8258}{1 - 7.4388} = \frac{-0.8258}{-6.4388} \approx 0.1283
$$

3. The expected time is $D_3 = \frac{k - N w_3}{q - p}$ with $q - p = 0.1$:

$$
D_3 = \frac{3 - 10 \times 0.1283}{0.1} = \frac{3 - 1.283}{0.1} = \frac{1.717}{0.1} \approx 17.17
$$

**Answer.** Overflow before the buffer is empty has chance about $0.128$, and the expected time to either event is about $17.2$ ticks.

**Check.** Without the upper wall, the buffer would run dry after $\frac{k}{q - p} = \frac{3}{0.1} = 30$ ticks on average (Result 3). The upper wall can only stop the game earlier, so $D_3$ must be below 30, and $17.17$ is. The simulation gives $17.25$.

### Example 3: Playing Against Unlimited Money

**Problem.** A small insurance fund has capital 3 units. Each month it gains 1 unit with probability $0.55$ and loses 1 unit with probability $0.45$. Find the chance that the fund is ever ruined. Then repeat for a game that loses with probability $0.55$, and find the average time to ruin.

**Solution.**

1. Favorable game: $p = 0.55 \gt \frac{1}{2}$, so $r = \frac{0.45}{0.55} = \frac{9}{11}$ and, by Result 3,

$$
P(\text{ever ruined}) = r^3 = \left(\frac{9}{11}\right)^3 = \frac{729}{1331} \approx 0.5477
$$

So the fund survives forever with probability $1 - 0.5477 = 0.4523$.

2. Unfavorable game: $p = 0.45$. Ruin is certain, $P(\text{ever ruined}) = 1$. The average time to ruin is

$$
E[T_0] = \frac{k}{q - p} = \frac{3}{0.55 - 0.45} = \frac{3}{0.1} = 30 \text{ months}
$$

**Answer.** With the edge in your favor, ruin still happens with probability $0.548$. Against the edge, ruin is sure and takes 30 months on average.

**Check.** The simulation with a very high goal of 60 (where the upper wall can almost never matter) gives $P(\text{reach } 60) = 0.4511$ for $p = 0.55$, close to $0.4523$, and an average duration of $29.94$ for $p = 0.45$, close to $30$.

## Simulate It

The code plays many games with different values of $p$, start and goal, and prints the chance of reaching the goal and the average number of rounds next to the exact values from this file and from 4.11.5.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def play(p, start, goal, n_games):
    # Play many games at once. Return: reached the goal? and number of rounds.
    money = np.full(n_games, start)
    rounds = np.zeros(n_games, dtype=int)
    active = np.ones(n_games, dtype=bool)
    while active.any():
        money[active] += np.where(rng.random(active.sum()) < p, 1, -1)
        rounds[active] += 1
        active &= (money > 0) & (money < goal)
    return money == goal, rounds

def exact(p, k, n):
    if p == 0.5:
        return k / n, k * (n - k)
    q = 1 - p
    w = (1 - (q / p)**k) / (1 - (q / p)**n)
    return w, (k - n * w) / (q - p)

print("   p  start goal | P(goal) sim  exact | E[rounds] sim   exact")
cases = [(0.5, 3, 10, 100_000), (0.45, 3, 10, 100_000), (0.6, 3, 10, 100_000),
         (0.5, 10, 100, 20_000), (0.45, 3, 60, 100_000), (0.55, 3, 60, 100_000)]
for p, k, n, games in cases:
    won, rounds = play(p, k, n, games)
    w, d = exact(p, k, n)
    print(f"{p:5.2f} {k:5d} {n:5d} | {won.mean():10.4f} {w:7.4f} | {rounds.mean():10.2f} {d:8.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
   p  start goal | P(goal) sim  exact | E[rounds] sim   exact
 0.50     3    10 |     0.3008  0.3000 |      21.12    21.00
 0.45     3    10 |     0.1294  0.1283 |      17.25    17.17
 0.60     3    10 |     0.7159  0.7161 |      20.86    20.81
 0.50    10   100 |     0.0997  0.1000 |     894.18   900.00
 0.45     3    60 |     0.0000  0.0000 |      29.94    30.00
 0.55     3    60 |     0.4511  0.4523 |     240.56   241.38
```

The row $(0.5, 10, 100)$ has a long and uneven tail, so its simulated mean (894) fluctuates more than the others.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking a game with a likely early ruin is short on average.** In the fair game with 1 taka against 99, ruin comes fast 99% of the time, yet the average length is 99 rounds because of rare very long games.
- **Using $k(N-k)$ for a biased game.** That formula is only for $p = \frac{1}{2}$. For $p \ne \frac{1}{2}$ use $D_k = \frac{k - N w_k}{q - p}$.
- **Writing the equation before knowing the mean is finite.** The equation $D_k = 1 + p D_{k+1} + q D_{k-1}$ is only meaningful when $D_k \lt \infty$. Result 1 proves this for two walls. With one wall and a fair game the mean is infinite (Result 3), so the equation would be wrong there.
- **Mixing "certain" and "soon".** Against unlimited money, a fair game ends in ruin with probability 1, but $E[T_0] = \infty$. A sure event can still take an infinite expected time.
- **Forgetting the roles of $p$ and $q$ in $r$.** Use $r = \frac{q}{p}$ (loss over win). If you swap them you get the chance for the other player.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance and insurance:** the chance that a fund with capital $k$ is ever ruined is $r^k$, which decays geometrically with the capital. The continuous version is the Cramer-Lundberg ruin bound ([21.5](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Computer science:** the simple randomized algorithm for 2-SAT flips one variable at a time. Its distance from a solution behaves like a walk between two walls that is at least fair, and the bound $k(N-k) \le \frac{N^2}{4}$ gives an expected running time of order $N^2$ ([21.3.1](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.1-Randomized-Algorithms.md)).
- **Queues and buffers:** the time until a buffer overflows or empties ([18.7](../18.7-Queues-Intuition.md), [21.3.4](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
- **Biology:** a gene variant that either spreads to the whole population or disappears is a walk with two walls ([21.9](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.9-Biology-and-Genetics.md)).
- **Everyday life:** any "play until I reach my goal or lose my savings" plan, such as the roulette example of 4.11.5.

**2. (Easy)** You start with 1 taka and play a fair game until you have 50 taka or nothing. Find the chance of success and the expected number of rounds.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$w_1 = \frac{1}{50} = 0.02, \qquad D_1 = 1 \times 49 = 49 \text{ rounds}$$

Ruin comes in the very first round with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, yet the average is 49 rounds.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A game has $p = 0.4$, $N = 5$ and $k = 2$. Find the chance of reaching 5 and the expected number of rounds, as exact fractions.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $r = \frac{0.6}{0.4} = \frac{3}{2}$, $r^2 = \frac{9}{4}$ and $r^5 = \frac{243}{32}$:

$$w_2 = \frac{1 - 9/4}{1 - 243/32} = \frac{-5/4}{-211/32} = \frac{5}{4} \cdot \frac{32}{211} = \frac{40}{211} \approx 0.1896$$

With $q - p = 0.2$:

$$D_2 = \frac{2 - 5 \cdot \frac{40}{211}}{0.2} = \frac{\frac{422 - 200}{211}}{0.2} = \frac{222}{211} \cdot 5 = \frac{1110}{211} \approx 5.26$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A fund with capital 10 units gains 1 unit with probability $0.52$ and loses 1 unit with probability $0.48$ each period, against unlimited money. Find the chance it is never ruined. Then find it for a fair game.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $r = \frac{0.48}{0.52} = \frac{12}{13}$, the chance of ever being ruined is $r^{10}$:

$$P(\text{never ruined}) = 1 - \left(\frac{12}{13}\right)^{10} \approx 1 - 0.4491 = 0.5509$$

For a fair game the chance of ever being ruined is 1, so the chance of never being ruined is 0.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** (a) Check that $D_k = k(N-k)$ satisfies $D_k = 1 + \frac{1}{2} D_{k+1} + \frac{1}{2} D_{k-1}$. (b) Show that in a fair game, from any start, $P(T \gt mN^2) \le 4^{-m}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Let $f(k) = k(N-k)$. Expanding, $f(k+1) + f(k-1) = 2k(N-k) - 2 = 2f(k) - 2$, so

$$1 + \tfrac{1}{2} f(k+1) + \tfrac{1}{2} f(k-1) = 1 + f(k) - 1 = f(k)$$

and $f(0) = f(N) = 0$.

(b) From any start $j$, the expected remaining duration is $j(N-j) \le \frac{N^2}{4}$ (the product of two numbers with sum $N$ is largest when they are equal). By Markov's inequality ([16.1](../../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.1-Markov-Inequality.md)), $P(T \gt N^2) \le \frac{N^2/4}{N^2} = \frac{1}{4}$. If the game is still running after $N^2$ rounds, it is a fresh game from the current position, so the same bound applies again. After $m$ such blocks:

$$P(T \gt mN^2) \le \left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)^m$$

</details>

## Summary

- A game between two walls ends with probability 1, and its expected duration is finite.
- Condition on the first round: $D_k = 1 + p D_{k+1} + q D_{k-1}$ with $D_0 = D_N = 0$.
- Fair game: $D_k = k(N-k)$. Biased game: $D_k = \frac{k - N w_k}{q - p}$.
- With one wall only: ruin is certain if $p \le \frac{1}{2}$ and has probability $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^k$ if $p \gt \frac{1}{2}$. The average time to ruin is $\frac{k}{q-p}$ if $p \lt \frac{1}{2}$ and infinite if $p = \frac{1}{2}$.
- Martingales give the same results in two lines ([Module 20](../../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.8-Martingales.md)).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{D_k = k(N - k) \ (p = \tfrac{1}{2}), \qquad D_k = \frac{k - N w_k}{q - p} \ (p \ne \tfrac{1}{2})}
$$
