# 18.2.1 Simple Random Walk

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.2 Random Walks](README.md) / 18.2.1 Simple Random Walk

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [18.1 What is a Stochastic Process](../18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)

> **Big idea:** A simple random walk takes one step up or down at every tick, its position is a binomial variable in disguise ($S_n = 2K - n$), and after $n$ steps it is typically about $\sqrt{n}$ away from where it started, not $n$.

---

## Why It Matters

The **simple random walk** is the most studied stochastic process. It is the coin game of [18.1](../18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md), the model of a drunk person on a street, and the discrete skeleton of Brownian motion ([18.8](../18.8-Brownian-Motion.md)). Everything else in this section (ruin, reflection, return) is a question about it. Here we learn its basic facts: where it is after $n$ steps, how far it wanders, and how a small bias changes everything.

## Intuition

A robot stands on a long straight road, at position 0. Every second it tosses a coin. Heads: it moves 1 metre to the right. Tails: it moves 1 metre to the left. The robot never stays still and never moves more than 1 metre at a time.

After 10 seconds, where can it be? Always at an **even** position, because each step changes the position by 1, so after 10 steps the position and 10 have the same parity. It cannot be at 3. It could be at 10 (ten heads), but that is very unlikely: only 1 path in 1,024.

How far from the start will it usually be? Not 10 metres, and not 0. It is usually about 3 metres away, and $3 \approx \sqrt{10}$. After 100 seconds it is usually about 10 metres away, and after 10,000 seconds about 100 metres. Distance grows like the **square root** of time. This is the most famous fact about random walks.

## Definition

Let $\xi_1, \xi_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. **steps**, each equal to $+1$ with probability $p$ and to $-1$ with probability $q = 1 - p$. The **simple random walk** is

$$
S_0 = 0, \qquad S_n = \xi_1 + \xi_2 + \dots + \xi_n
$$

The walk is called **symmetric** (or **fair**) if $p = \frac{1}{2}$, and **biased** (or to have a **drift**) otherwise.

**In words:** start at 0 and add up independent steps of $+1$ or $-1$.

Let $K_n$ be the number of up-steps among the first $n$ steps. Then $K_n \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ ([7.2](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md)) and

$$
S_n = K_n - (n - K_n) = 2K_n - n
$$

**In words:** the position is the number of up-steps minus the number of down-steps.

The distribution of $S_n$ follows. For an integer $k$ with $\lvert k \rvert \le n$ and $n + k$ even:

$$
P(S_n = k) = \binom{n}{\frac{n+k}{2}} \thinspace p^{(n+k)/2} \thinspace q^{(n-k)/2}
$$

and $P(S_n = k) = 0$ for all other $k$. The mean and variance are

$$
E[S_n] = n(2p - 1), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = 4npq
$$

**In words:** the walk drifts by $2p - 1$ per step on average. Its spread is $2\sqrt{npq}$, which for the fair walk is $\sqrt{n}$.

## Why It Is True

**The distribution.** Suppose $S_n = k$. If there are $u$ up-steps and $n - u$ down-steps, then $k = u - (n - u) = 2u - n$, so $u = \frac{n+k}{2}$. This must be a whole number between 0 and $n$, which gives the parity rule and the range $\lvert k \rvert \le n$.

Every path with exactly $u$ up-steps has the same probability $p^u q^{n-u}$ (the steps are independent). The number of such paths is the number of ways to choose which $u$ of the $n$ steps go up, which is $\binom{n}{u}$ ([2.3](../../02-COUNTING/2.3-Combinations.md)). Multiplying gives the formula.

**The mean and variance.** One step has

$$
E[\xi_i] = (+1) p + (-1) q = p - q = 2p - 1
$$

$$
E[\xi_i^2] = 1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\xi_i) = 1 - (2p - 1)^2 = 4p(1 - p) = 4pq
$$

(Check: $1 - (2p-1)^2 = 1 - 4p^2 + 4p - 1 = 4p(1-p)$.) The steps are independent, so by [18.1](../18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md) the mean and variance of the sum are $n$ times those of one step.

**Why $\sqrt{n}$ and not $n$.** For the fair walk, $E[S_n] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n$, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{n}$. The Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) says the shape is a bell curve:

$$
\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

So for large $n$, $P(\lvert S_n \rvert \le c\sqrt{n}) \approx P(\lvert Z \rvert \le c)$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. In particular the typical size is $E\lvert S_n \rvert \approx \sqrt{2n/\pi} \approx 0.8\sqrt{n}$, because $E\lvert Z \rvert = \sqrt{2/\pi}$.

**A biased walk goes away linearly.** For $p \ne \frac{1}{2}$ the Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](../../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) gives $\frac{S_n}{n} \to 2p - 1$. The drift $n(2p-1)$ grows like $n$, while the noise grows only like $\sqrt{n}$. So a small bias wins in the long run.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Exact Distribution of a Short Walk

**Problem.** For the fair walk, find the full distribution of $S_6$ and the probability that $S_6 \ge 2$.

**Solution.**

1. After 6 steps $S_6$ is even, and $S_6 = 2u - 6$ where $u$ is the number of up-steps, $u = 0, \dots, 6$. So $S_6 \in \lbrace -6, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$.
2. Each of the $2^6 = 64$ paths has probability $\frac{1}{64}$, and $P(S_6 = 2u - 6) = \binom{6}{u} \frac{1}{64}$:

```text
  k = -6 | #                       1/64
  k = -4 | ######                  6/64
  k = -2 | ###############        15/64
  k =  0 | ####################   20/64
  k = +2 | ###############        15/64
  k = +4 | ######                  6/64
  k = +6 | #                       1/64        (each # is 1/64)
```

3. The event $S_6 \ge 2$ means $S_6 \in \lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$:

$$
P(S_6 \ge 2) = \frac{15 + 6 + 1}{64} = \frac{22}{64} = 0.34375
$$

**Answer.** $P(S_6 = 0) = \frac{20}{64} = 0.3125$ and $P(S_6 \ge 2) = 0.34375$.

**Check.** The probabilities add to $\frac{1 + 6 + 15 + 20 + 15 + 6 + 1}{64} = 1$. By symmetry $P(S_6 \le -2)$ is also $0.34375$, and $2 \times 0.34375 + 0.3125 = 1$.

### Example 2: A Share Price With a Small Edge

**Problem.** Every minute the price of a share goes up by 1 taka with probability $0.55$ and down by 1 taka with probability $0.45$. Over 400 minutes, find the mean and standard deviation of the total change $S_{400}$, and the chance that the price ends higher than it started.

**Solution.**

1. Here $p = 0.55$, $q = 0.45$, $n = 400$. The mean is $E[S_{400}] = 400 (2 \cdot 0.55 - 1) = 400 \times 0.1 = 40$ taka.
2. The variance is $4npq = 4 \times 400 \times 0.55 \times 0.45 = 396$, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{396} \approx 19.9$ taka.
3. The price ends higher when $S_{400} \gt 0$. Since $S_{400} = 2K - 400$, this means $K \ge 201$ up-steps, where $K \sim \text{Bin}(400, 0.55)$ has mean $220$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{400 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} = \sqrt{99} \approx 9.95$.
4. Use the normal approximation with the continuity correction ([17.6](../../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md)), $P(K \ge 201) = P(K \gt 200.5)$:

$$
z = \frac{200.5 - 220}{9.95} \approx -1.96, \qquad P(K \ge 201) \approx 1 - \Phi(-1.96) = 0.975
$$

**Answer.** The mean change is $40$ taka, the standard deviation is about $19.9$ taka, and the price ends higher with probability about $0.975$ (the exact binomial value is $0.9748$).

**Check.** The mean $40$ is two standard deviations above 0. Such a small edge (a 55% chance each minute) turns into a very likely gain over 400 minutes: the drift grows like $n$, the noise like $\sqrt{n}$. The simulation below gives the same numbers.

### Example 3: Being Back at the Start

**Problem.** For the fair walk, find the probability of being at 0 at time 20, and compare it with the approximation $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi m}}$ for time $2m$.

**Solution.**

1. At time $2m = 20$ we need $u = 10$ up-steps out of 20, so $m = 10$:

$$
P(S_{20} = 0) = \binom{20}{10} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{20} = \frac{184{,}756}{1{,}048{,}576} \approx 0.1762
$$

2. The approximation is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi \cdot 10}} \approx 0.1784$.

**Answer.** $P(S_{20} = 0) \approx 0.1762$, close to the approximation $0.1784$.

**Check.** This is consistent with the bell-curve picture. The walk is spread over about $\sqrt{20} \approx 4.5$ in each direction, over the even values only, so a central point has probability about $\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 20}} \approx 0.178$. The decay $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi m}}$ matters a lot in [18.2.4](18.2.4-Recurrence-and-Transience.md).

## Simulate It

The code checks the distribution, the $\sqrt{n}$ spread and the biased walk of Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_walks = 200_000

# Fair walk: 100 steps of +1 or -1.
walk = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(n_walks, 100)).cumsum(axis=1)
s10, s100 = walk[:, 9], walk[:, 99]
within = binom.cdf(55, 100, 0.5) - binom.cdf(44, 100, 0.5)      # |S_100| <= 10
print(f"P(S_10 = 2):    simulation {np.mean(s10 == 2):.4f}, exact {comb(10, 6) / 2**10:.4f}")
print(f"Var(S_100):     simulation {s100.var():.2f}, exact 100")
print(f"E|S_100|:       simulation {np.abs(s100).mean():.3f}, exact {100 * comb(100, 50) / 4**50:.3f}")
print(f"P(|S_100|<=10): simulation {np.mean(np.abs(s100) <= 10):.4f}, exact {within:.4f}")

# Walk with drift: up with probability 0.55, 400 steps. S_n = 2K - n.
p, n = 0.55, 400
s400 = 2 * (rng.random((50_000, n)) < p).sum(axis=1) - n
print(f"E[S_400]:       simulation {s400.mean():.2f}, exact {n * (2 * p - 1):.2f}")
print(f"SD(S_400):      simulation {s400.std():.2f}, exact {(4 * n * p * (1 - p)) ** 0.5:.2f}")
print(f"P(S_400 > 0):   simulation {np.mean(s400 > 0):.4f}, exact {binom.sf(200, n, p):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(S_10 = 2):    simulation 0.2045, exact 0.2051
Var(S_100):     simulation 100.54, exact 100
E|S_100|:       simulation 7.979, exact 7.959
P(|S_100|<=10): simulation 0.7276, exact 0.7287
E[S_400]:       simulation 39.88, exact 40.00
SD(S_400):      simulation 19.94, exact 19.90
P(S_400 > 0):   simulation 0.9738, exact 0.9748
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Expecting the walk to be about $n$ away.** After $n$ fair steps the typical distance is about $\sqrt{n}$. For $n = 10{,}000$ it is about 100 metres, not 10,000.
- **Forgetting parity.** $S_n$ and $n$ always have the same parity, so $P(S_5 = 2) = 0$. Check that $\frac{n+k}{2}$ is a whole number before using the formula.
- **Using the wrong variance of a step.** One step $\pm 1$ has variance $4pq$, which is $1$ for the fair walk. It is not the $pq$ of a $0/1$ coin, because the steps are $2$ apart.
- **Thinking a fair walk spends half its time on each side of 0.** The path in the Picture never came back to 0 after step 12. This is typical: fair walks have long one-sided stretches.
- **Treating a tiny bias as harmless.** With $p = 0.55$ the walk ends above 0 with probability $0.975$ after 400 steps, even though each single step looks almost like a fair coin.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** random-walk Metropolis, a basic MCMC algorithm, proposes a random step from the current point ([19.7.1](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/19.7.1-Metropolis-Hastings.md)). The noise in stochastic gradient descent behaves like a random walk around the true gradient path.
- **Computer science:** a random walk on a graph (move to a random neighbour) is the idea behind PageRank and many randomized search algorithms ([18.3](../18.3-Markov-Chains/README.md)).
- **Physics:** a particle bumped around by molecules is a random walk, and the $\sqrt{n}$ law is the law of **diffusion** ([21.8](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.8-Physics.md)).
- **Finance:** the tick-by-tick movement of a price is modelled by a walk with a small drift ([21.5](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Everyday life:** the lead in a long series of games between two equal teams is a random walk. It often stays on one side for a very long time.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** For the fair walk, find $P(S_8 = 0)$ and $P(S_8 = 4)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$S_8 = 0$ needs 4 up-steps, and $S_8 = 4$ needs 6 up-steps:

$$P(S_8 = 0) = \binom{8}{4} \frac{1}{2^8} = \frac{70}{256} \approx 0.2734, \qquad P(S_8 = 4) = \binom{8}{6} \frac{1}{2^8} = \frac{28}{256} \approx 0.1094$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Can the fair walk have $S_7 = 2$? Find $P(S_7 = 3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No: $7 + 2 = 9$ is odd, so $\frac{n+k}{2}$ is not a whole number and $P(S_7 = 2) = 0$. For $k = 3$ we need $u = \frac{7+3}{2} = 5$ up-steps:

$$P(S_7 = 3) = \binom{7}{5} \frac{1}{2^7} = \frac{21}{128} \approx 0.164$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A walk has $p = 0.7$. Find $E[S_{50}]$, $\operatorname{Var}(S_{50})$, the standard deviation, and $P(S_3 = 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[S_{50}] = 50(2 \times 0.7 - 1) = 50 \times 0.4 = 20, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_{50}) = 4 \times 50 \times 0.7 \times 0.3 = 42$$

The standard deviation is $\sqrt{42} \approx 6.48$. For $S_3 = 1$ we need $u = 2$ up-steps:

$$P(S_3 = 1) = \binom{3}{2} (0.7)^2 (0.3) = 3 \times 0.49 \times 0.3 = 0.441$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A fair walk takes $n = 10{,}000$ steps. Use the normal approximation (with the continuity correction) to estimate the chance that it ends within 100 of the start, $P(\lvert S_n \rvert \le 100)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The standard deviation is $\sqrt{10{,}000} = 100$. Since $S_n$ takes only even values, $\lvert S_n \rvert \le 100$ is the same as $\lvert S_n \rvert \lt 101$, and the correction is 1 on each side, i.e. $z = \frac{101}{100} = 1.01$:

$$P(\lvert S_n \rvert \le 100) \approx 2\Phi(1.01) - 1 = 2 \times 0.8438 - 1 = 0.6875$$

So the walk is within one standard deviation of the start about 69% of the time.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For the fair walk, prove that $E[S_n^2] = n$ and use it to show $E\lvert S_n \rvert \le \sqrt{n}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand the square and use independence ($E[\xi_i \xi_j] = E[\xi_i] E[\xi_j] = 0$ for $i \ne j$, and $\xi_i^2 = 1$):

$$E[S_n^2] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[\xi_i^2] + \sum_{i \ne j} E[\xi_i \xi_j] = n \cdot 1 + 0 = n$$

The function $x \mapsto x^2$ is convex, so Jensen's inequality ([16.3](../../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)) gives $(E\lvert S_n \rvert)^2 \le E[\lvert S_n \rvert^2] = n$. Taking square roots:

$$E\lvert S_n \rvert \le \sqrt{n}$$

The true value is about $\sqrt{2n/\pi} \approx 0.8\sqrt{n}$, a little smaller.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 by simulation, using 4,000 walks of 10,000 steps.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom, norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, n_walks, chunk = 10_000, 4_000, 500

# S_n = 2K - n, where K counts the up-steps of each walk.
inside = 0
for _ in range(n_walks // chunk):
    k = (rng.random((chunk, n)) < 0.5).sum(axis=1)
    inside += np.sum(np.abs(2 * k - n) <= 100)
exact = binom.cdf(5050, n, 0.5) - binom.cdf(4949, n, 0.5)
print(f"P(|S_10000| <= 100): simulation {inside / n_walks:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}, "
      f"normal {2 * norm.cdf(1.01) - 1:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(|S_10000| <= 100): simulation 0.6875, exact 0.6875, normal 0.6875
```

</details>

## Summary

- A simple random walk adds i.i.d. steps of $+1$ (probability $p$) and $-1$ (probability $q$).
- Its position is $S_n = 2K_n - n$ with $K_n \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$, so $P(S_n = k) = \binom{n}{(n+k)/2} p^{(n+k)/2} q^{(n-k)/2}$, and $S_n$ always has the parity of $n$.
- $E[S_n] = n(2p-1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = 4npq$.
- A fair walk is typically about $\sqrt{n}$ from the start; a biased walk drifts away at speed $2p - 1$, which beats the $\sqrt{n}$ noise.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{S_n = 2K_n - n, \qquad E[S_n] = n(2p-1), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = 4npq}
$$
