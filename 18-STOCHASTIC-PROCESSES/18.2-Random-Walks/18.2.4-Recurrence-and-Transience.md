# 18.2.4 Recurrence and Transience

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](../README.md) / [18.2 Random Walks](README.md) / 18.2.4 Recurrence and Transience

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [18.2.3 Reflection Principle](18.2.3-Reflection-Principle.md)

> **Big idea:** A fair random walk on a line or on a plane comes back to its starting point with probability 1, again and again, but a biased walk, or a fair walk in three dimensions, may leave forever: the walk is recurrent exactly when the expected number of visits to the start is infinite.

---

## Why It Matters

A walker who wanders at random: will it ever come home? This is the question of **recurrence**. It decides whether a random search eventually revisits a state, whether a queue empties again and again, and whether a Markov chain keeps returning to the same place. The answer depends on the **dimension** and on **bias**, and it is surprising. The same ideas are made general for Markov chains in [18.3.3 Classification of States](../18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.3-Classification-of-States.md).

## Intuition

A drunk person leaves a pub and takes a step to the left or to the right with equal chance, again and again, on a long straight street. Will they ever come back to the pub door? Yes: with probability 1. In fact they come back not once, but infinitely many times.

Now let the drunk person walk on a flat city grid, taking a random step north, south, east or west. Surprisingly, they still come back to the starting corner with probability 1. But a **bird** flying at random in three dimensions (up, down, north, south, east, west) comes back with probability only about $0.34$. With probability $0.66$ it never sees its nest again. A famous saying puts it this way: a drunk man will find his way home, but a drunk bird may get lost forever.

What changes between 2 and 3 dimensions? In higher dimensions there is more room to wander off, so the walk is less likely to meet the same point. We will count visits to the starting point and see exactly where the line is crossed.

## Picture

```text
          U = u_1 + u_2 + u_3 + ...   (expected visits to the start)
                                |
                 +--------------+--------------+
                 |                             |
          U is infinite                  U is finite
                 |                             |
          f = 1                          f = 1 - 1/U  < 1
          RECURRENT                      TRANSIENT
          returns again and again        may leave for ever
                 |                             |
     fair walk on a line (u_n ~ n^-1/2)   biased walk on a line
     fair walk on a plane (u_n ~ 1/n)     fair walk in space (u_n ~ n^-3/2)
```

Count the expected number of visits $U$ to the start. If it is infinite the walk is recurrent, and if it is finite the walk is transient.

## Definition

Let $S_n$ be a random walk that starts at its **origin** $S_0 = 0$. The **first return time** is

$$
R = \min \lbrace n \ge 1 : S_n = 0 \rbrace
$$

and the **return probability** is $f = P(R \lt \infty)$, the chance that the walk ever comes back to 0. The walk is called

- **recurrent** if $f = 1$, and
- **transient** if $f \lt 1$.

**In words:** a walk is recurrent if it is certain to return to where it started, and transient if there is a positive chance that it never does.

Let $u_n = P(S_{2n} = 0)$ be the chance of being at the origin at time $2n$ (the walk on a line can only be at 0 at even times), and let $V$ be the total number of visits to the origin, counting the start at time 0. Define

$$
U = E[V] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} u_n \qquad (u_0 = 1)
$$

**The main criterion.**

$$
U = \frac{1}{1 - f}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \text{recurrent} \iff U = \infty
$$

**In words:** a walk is recurrent exactly when the expected number of visits to the origin is infinite. If it is transient, the expected number of visits is $\frac{1}{1 - f}$.

Results for the simple walk (each step goes to a random neighbour):

| Walk | $u_n$ for large $n$ | $f$ | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Line, fair ($p = \frac{1}{2}$) | about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$ | $1$ | recurrent |
| Line, biased ($p \ne \frac{1}{2}$) | about $\frac{(4pq)^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$ | $1 - \lvert p - q \rvert$ | transient |
| Plane, fair | about $\frac{1}{\pi n}$ | $1$ | recurrent |
| Space, fair | about $0.233 \thinspace n^{-3/2}$ | about $0.3405$ | transient |

> **Note:** Even a recurrent walk can take a very long time to return. For the fair walk on a line the mean return time $E[R]$ is infinite. In Markov chain language this is **null recurrence** ([18.3.3](../18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.3-Classification-of-States.md)).

## Why It Is True

**The main criterion.** Each time the walk is at 0, its future steps are fresh independent steps, so the walk starts over. The chance of one more return is $f$ each time, so $P(V \ge j + 1) = f^j$ (at least $j$ returns). Then $V$ is a geometric variable and

$$
E[V] = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} P(V \ge j + 1) = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} f^j = \frac{1}{1 - f} \quad (f \lt 1), \qquad E[V] = \infty \quad (f = 1)
$$

On the other hand, $V$ is the sum of indicators $I_{\lbrace S_m = 0 \rbrace}$ over all times $m \ge 0$. By linearity of expectation ([6.2](../../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)), $E[V] = \sum_m P(S_m = 0) = \sum_n u_n = U$. Comparing the two expressions gives $U = \frac{1}{1 - f}$. If $f = 1$, then $P(V \ge j + 1) = 1$ for every $j$, so the walk visits 0 infinitely often with probability 1.

**The size of $u_n$ on a line.** For a walk with $P(+1) = p$, being at 0 at time $2n$ needs exactly $n$ up-steps ([18.2.1](18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md)):

$$
u_n = \binom{2n}{n} p^n q^n
$$

Stirling's formula $n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n$ (a standard fact) gives

$$
\binom{2n}{n} = \frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2} \approx \frac{\sqrt{4\pi n} \left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2n}}{2\pi n \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{2n}} = \frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}
$$

so $u_n \approx \frac{(4pq)^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$.

**Fair walk on a line is recurrent.** For $p = q = \frac{1}{2}$ we have $4pq = 1$ and $u_n \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$. The series $\sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ diverges (its partial sums grow like $2\sqrt{N}$), so $U = \infty$, and by the criterion $f = 1$.

**Biased walk on a line is transient.** For $p \ne q$ we have $4pq = 1 - (p - q)^2 \lt 1$, so $u_n$ decays geometrically and $U$ is finite. The binomial series $\sum_{n \ge 0} \binom{2n}{n} x^n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 4x}}$ for $\lvert x \rvert \lt \frac{1}{4}$, with $x = pq$, gives

$$
U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 4pq}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(p - q)^2}} = \frac{1}{\lvert p - q \rvert}
$$

(because $1 - 4pq = (p + q)^2 - 4pq = (p - q)^2$). By the criterion,

$$
f = 1 - \frac{1}{U} = 1 - \lvert p - q \rvert
$$

There is also a quick reason without computation. By the Law of Large Numbers ([17.4](../../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)), $\frac{S_n}{n} \to 2p - 1 \ne 0$ with probability 1, so $\lvert S_n \rvert \to \infty$ and the walk is at 0 only finitely often.

**The plane.** The simple walk on the grid has four equally likely steps $(\pm 1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm 1)$. Turn the grid by $45$ degrees: use the new coordinates $A_n = X_n + Y_n$ and $B_n = X_n - Y_n$. The four steps become

$$
(1, 0) \mapsto (1, 1), \quad (-1, 0) \mapsto (-1, -1), \quad (0, 1) \mapsto (1, -1), \quad (0, -1) \mapsto (-1, 1)
$$

These are the four equally likely pairs of signs, so $A_n$ and $B_n$ are **independent** fair walks on a line. The walk is at the origin exactly when both $A_n = 0$ and $B_n = 0$, so

$$
u_n^{\text{plane}} = u_n^2 \approx \frac{1}{\pi n}
$$

The harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges, so $U = \infty$ and the walk on the plane is recurrent.

**Space.** In three dimensions one can show $u_n \approx 2\left(\frac{3}{4\pi n}\right)^{3/2} \approx 0.233 \thinspace n^{-3/2}$. The series $\sum n^{-3/2}$ converges, so $U$ is finite and the walk is transient. Summing the exact values gives $U \approx 1.5164$, hence $f = 1 - \frac{1}{U} \approx 0.3405$. This number is called **Polya's constant**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Walk With a Small Drift

**Problem.** A walk goes up with probability $p = 0.6$ and down with probability $q = 0.4$. Find the chance that it ever returns to 0, the expected number of returns, and the chance of exactly 2 returns.

**Solution.**

1. Here $\lvert p - q \rvert = 0.2$. The expected number of visits (counting time 0) is

$$
U = \frac{1}{\lvert p - q \rvert} = \frac{1}{0.2} = 5
$$

2. The return probability is $f = 1 - \frac{1}{U} = 1 - \frac{1}{5} = 0.8$. (Same as $1 - \lvert p - q \rvert = 1 - 0.2$.)
3. The number of returns is $V - 1$. Its mean is $U - 1 = 4$. (Check with geometric: $\frac{f}{1 - f} = \frac{0.8}{0.2} = 4$.)
4. Exactly 2 returns means two returns and then never again:

$$
P(\text{exactly 2 returns}) = f^2 (1 - f) = 0.64 \times 0.2 = 0.128
$$

**Answer.** $f = 0.8$, the expected number of returns is $4$, and $P(\text{exactly 2 returns}) = 0.128$.

**Check.** The chance of returning in the first two steps alone is $2pq = 0.48$, which is below $0.8$, as it must be. The simulation below gives $0.8018$ and $4.029$.

### Example 2: How Long Until a Return?

**Problem.** For the fair walk on a line, the chance of no return within $2n$ steps is $u_n = \binom{2n}{n} 4^{-n}$ (proved in Practice 5). How many steps are needed to be 90% sure of a return? And 99% sure?

**Solution.**

1. We need $u_n \le 0.1$. Use $u_n \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \le 0.1$ means $n \ge \frac{100}{\pi} \approx 31.8$. Exact values: $u_{31} \approx 0.1009$ and $u_{32} \approx 0.0993$. So $n = 32$, which is $64$ steps.
2. For 99%: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \le 0.01$ means $n \ge \frac{10{,}000}{\pi} \approx 3183$. The exact values agree: $u_{3183} \approx 0.0099998$. This is $2 \times 3183 = 6366$ steps.

**Answer.** About 64 steps for 90% and 6,366 steps for 99%.

**Check.** The number of steps grows like $\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}$ for failure chance $\varepsilon$: a hundred times more steps for ten times more certainty. This is the slow $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ decay that makes the mean return time infinite.

### Example 3: Line, Plane and Space

**Problem.** Compare how many times a fair walk is expected to visit the origin within 100 steps and within 1,000 steps in dimensions 1, 2 and 3.

**Solution.** The expected number of visits at times $2, 4, \dots$ up to $2n_{\max}$ is the sum $\sum_{n=1}^{n_{\max}} u_n$. We use $u_n$ (line), $u_n^2$ (plane) and the exact three-dimensional values, and add them by computer.

| Steps | Line | Plane | Space |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | $7.04$ | $1.32$ | $0.45$ |
| 1000 | $24.25$ | $2.04$ | $0.50$ |

**Answer.** On the line the number of visits grows like $\sqrt{\text{steps}}$ without bound ($7 \to 24$). On the plane it grows very slowly, like $\ln(\text{steps})$. In space it stops growing: it stays below $U - 1 \approx 0.516$.

**Check.** The 3D limit $U - 1 = 0.516$ matches $f = 0.3405$, since $\frac{1}{1 - f} - 1 = \frac{f}{1 - f} = 0.516$. Going from 100 to 1000 steps (ten times more time) multiplies the visits by about 3.4 on the line, by about 1.5 on the plane, and by about 1.1 in space.

<details>
<summary>Code for this table (optional)</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb, lgamma, log

log_fact = np.array([lgamma(i + 1) for i in range(1001)])

def u_1d(n):                       # P(1D walk at 0 at step 2n)
    return comb(2 * n, n) / 4**n

def u_3d(n):                       # P(3D walk at 0 at step 2n), summed over i+j+k = n
    i, j = np.meshgrid(np.arange(n + 1), np.arange(n + 1), indexing="ij")
    k = n - i - j
    ok = k >= 0
    k = np.where(ok, k, 0)
    log_terms = log_fact[2 * n] - n * log(4) - 2 * n * log(3) \
        - 2 * (log_fact[i] + log_fact[j] + log_fact[k])
    return np.exp(log_terms[ok]).sum()

for steps in (100, 1000):
    n_max = steps // 2
    visits_1d = sum(u_1d(n) for n in range(1, n_max + 1))
    visits_2d = sum(u_1d(n) ** 2 for n in range(1, n_max + 1))
    visits_3d = sum(u_3d(n) for n in range(1, n_max + 1))
    print(f"steps {steps:4d}: visits to origin  1D {visits_1d:6.2f}  "
          f"2D {visits_2d:5.2f}  3D {visits_3d:5.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
steps  100: visits to origin  1D   7.04  2D  1.32  3D  0.45
steps 1000: visits to origin  1D  24.25  2D  2.04  3D  0.50
```

</details>

## Simulate It

The code checks the no-return formula for the fair line, the return probability and the mean number of returns for $p = 0.6$, and the plane at step 100.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_walks, n_steps = 40_000, 2000

def paths(up_tenths):
    # Positions S_1..S_2000; each step is +1 with probability up_tenths / 10.
    up = rng.integers(0, 10, size=(n_walks, n_steps), dtype=np.int8) < up_tenths
    return np.cumsum(up.astype(np.int8) * 2 - 1, axis=1, dtype=np.int16)

fair = paths(5)
for n in (5, 50, 1000):                                    # no return in 2n steps
    sim = 1 - np.mean((fair[:, :2 * n] == 0).any(axis=1))
    print(f"fair, P(no return by {2 * n:4d}): simulation {sim:.4f}, "
          f"exact {comb(2 * n, n) / 4**n:.4f}")

biased = paths(6)                                          # p = 0.6, so f = 1 - |p - q| = 0.8
print(f"p=0.6, P(ever returns): simulation {np.mean((biased == 0).any(axis=1)):.4f}, exact 0.8000")
print(f"p=0.6, mean returns:    simulation {(biased == 0).sum(axis=1).mean():.3f}, exact 4.000")

# Simple walk on the plane: 4 directions. P(at origin at step 100) = u_50 squared.
d = rng.integers(0, 4, size=(200_000, 100), dtype=np.int8)
x = np.cumsum(np.array([1, -1, 0, 0], dtype=np.int16)[d], axis=1)[:, -1]
y = np.cumsum(np.array([0, 0, 1, -1], dtype=np.int16)[d], axis=1)[:, -1]
print(f"2D, P(origin at 100):   simulation {np.mean((x == 0) & (y == 0)):.5f}, "
      f"exact {(comb(100, 50) / 4**50) ** 2:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
fair, P(no return by   10): simulation 0.2471, exact 0.2461
fair, P(no return by  100): simulation 0.0798, exact 0.0796
fair, P(no return by 2000): simulation 0.0178, exact 0.0178
p=0.6, P(ever returns): simulation 0.8018, exact 0.8000
p=0.6, mean returns:    simulation 4.029, exact 4.000
2D, P(origin at 100):   simulation 0.00631, exact 0.00633
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking "recurrent" means "returns quickly".** The fair walk on a line returns with probability 1, but its mean return time is infinite. In Example 2, 64 steps give only 90% certainty.
- **Believing a fair walk must be recurrent in every dimension.** It is recurrent on the line and on the plane, and transient from dimension 3 upwards.
- **Forgetting that bias destroys recurrence.** Even a mild bias ($p = 0.55$) makes the line walk transient. Then $f = 1 - \lvert p - q \rvert = 0.9$ is high, but not 1.
- **Using the series test on the wrong quantity.** The sum is over $u_n = P(S_{2n} = 0)$ (the chance to be at 0), not over the first-return probabilities.
- **Mixing up "returns once" and "returns infinitely often".** If $f \lt 1$ the walk returns only finitely many times; if $f = 1$ it returns infinitely often, with probability 1.

## Where It Is Used

- **Markov chains:** the same test (expected number of visits to a state) classifies every state of every chain ([18.3.3](../18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.3-Classification-of-States.md)). An irreducible chain with a stationary distribution is positive recurrent ([18.3.5](../18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md)).
- **Queues:** a queue with arrival rate equal to service rate behaves like a fair walk reflected at 0. It empties again and again but with infinite mean time between empties ([18.7](../18.7-Queues-Intuition.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** a random search or a sampling chain that is not recurrent can drift away and never revisit good regions. MCMC needs recurrence ([19.7](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/README.md)).
- **Physics and networks:** whether a diffusing particle returns, and whether a random walk on an infinite network (a grid or a tree) is recurrent, tells us about electrical resistance and about how information spreads.
- **Everyday life:** random searching for a lost item on a line or a flat floor will certainly revisit a spot, while a search in open space may miss it forever.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** For the fair walk, find $P(S_4 = 0)$ and compare it with the probability that the walk does not return to 0 in the first 4 steps.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(S_4 = 0) = \binom{4}{2} \frac{1}{2^4} = \frac{6}{16} = 0.375$$

The paths with no return are those with $S_1, \dots, S_4 \ne 0$. The first two steps must agree (2 ways), and then of the 4 continuations, UUDD (and its mirror image) comes back to 0 at step 4, while UUUU, UUUD, UUDU do not. So there are $2 \times 3 = 6$ such paths out of 16, again $\frac{6}{16} = 0.375$. The two probabilities are equal, as Practice 5 proves in general.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A walk goes up with probability $0.8$. Find $f$ and the expected number of returns to 0.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f = 1 - \lvert p - q \rvert = 1 - \lvert 0.8 - 0.2 \rvert = 0.4, \qquad E[\text{returns}] = \frac{f}{1 - f} = \frac{0.4}{0.6} = \frac{2}{3}$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A walk goes up with probability $0.55$. Find $f$, the chance that it never returns, and the chance of exactly 2 returns.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$f = 1 - \lvert 0.55 - 0.45 \rvert = 0.9, \qquad P(\text{never returns}) = 1 - f = 0.1$$

$$P(\text{exactly 2 returns}) = f^2 (1 - f) = 0.81 \times 0.1 = 0.081$$

The expected number of returns is $\frac{0.9}{0.1} = 9$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Use the rotation trick to find the probability that the simple walk on the plane is at the origin after 4 steps. Check it by counting closed paths.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The rotated coordinates are independent fair walks on a line, and a fair line walk is at 0 after 4 steps with probability $\frac{6}{16} = \frac{3}{8}$:

$$P = \left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{64} = 0.140625$$

Counting: there are $4^4 = 256$ paths of 4 steps. A closed path uses either 2 steps in one direction and 2 in the opposite one (for example R, R, L, L: $2 \times \frac{4!}{2!2!} = 12$ paths), or 1 right, 1 left, 1 up and 1 down ($4! = 24$ paths). Total $12 + 24 = 36$, and $\frac{36}{256} = 0.140625$. They agree.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for the fair walk on a line, $P(S_1 \ne 0, \dots, S_{2n} \ne 0) = u_n = \binom{2n}{n} 4^{-n}$. Deduce that the walk is recurrent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By symmetry, the probability is $2 P(S_1 \gt 0, \dots, S_{2n} \gt 0)$. Condition on $S_1 = 1$ (probability $\frac{1}{2}$). Let $A(d)$ be the number of paths of $2n - 1$ steps with total displacement $d$. A path that starts at height 1 and ends at an even height $h \ge 2$ either stays at least 1, or touches 0. Reflecting the part before the first touch of 0 shows that the bad paths correspond to paths from height $-1$ to $h$, so there are $A(h + 1)$ of them. The number of good paths ending at $h$ is $A(h - 1) - A(h + 1)$. Summing over $h = 2, 4, 6, \dots$ the sum telescopes:

$$\sum_{h = 2, 4, \dots} \big( A(h - 1) - A(h + 1) \big) = A(1) = \binom{2n - 1}{n}$$

So $P(S_1 \gt 0, \dots, S_{2n} \gt 0) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\binom{2n-1}{n}}{2^{2n-1}} = \frac{\binom{2n-1}{n}}{4^n}$. Double it and use $\binom{2n}{n} = 2 \binom{2n-1}{n}$:

$$P(S_1 \ne 0, \dots, S_{2n} \ne 0) = \frac{2 \binom{2n-1}{n}}{4^n} = \frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n} = u_n$$

Since $u_n \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \to 0$, the chance of never returning is $\lim_{n \to \infty} u_n = 0$. So $f = 1$ and the walk is recurrent. Also $P(R \gt 2n) = u_n \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$, so $E[R] = \sum_m P(R \gt m)$ diverges: the mean return time is infinite.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the return probability $f = 0.6$ and the mean number of returns $1.5$ for $p = 0.7$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $p = 0.7$, $f = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$ and the mean number of returns is $\frac{0.6}{0.4} = 1.5$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_walks, n_steps = 40_000, 1000
up = rng.integers(0, 10, size=(n_walks, n_steps), dtype=np.int8) < 7      # p = 0.7
walk = np.cumsum(up.astype(np.int8) * 2 - 1, axis=1, dtype=np.int16)
at_zero = walk == 0
print(f"P(ever returns): simulation {at_zero.any(axis=1).mean():.4f}, exact 0.6000")
print(f"mean returns:    simulation {at_zero.sum(axis=1).mean():.3f}, exact 1.500")
```

```text

mean returns:    simulation 1.499, exact 1.500
```

</details>

## Summary

- A walk is **recurrent** if it returns to the origin with probability 1 ($f = 1$), and **transient** if $f \lt 1$.
- The number of visits to the origin is geometric, and $U = \sum_n u_n = \frac{1}{1 - f}$, so recurrence means $U = \infty$.
- The fair walk is recurrent on the line ($u_n \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$) and on the plane ($u_n \approx \frac{1}{\pi n}$), but transient in space ($f \approx 0.3405$).
- A biased walk on the line is transient, with $f = 1 - \lvert p - q \rvert$.
- Recurrent does not mean fast: the fair line walk has infinite mean return time.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{U = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} P(S_{2n} = 0) = \frac{1}{1 - f}}
$$
