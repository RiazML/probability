# 18.8 Brownian Motion

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](README.md) / 18.8 Brownian Motion

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 55 minutes · **Before this:** [18.7 Queues Intuition](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md)

> **Big idea:** Brownian motion is what a random walk becomes when the steps get tiny and fast: a process with continuous paths, independent increments, and $B(t) - B(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)$.

---

## Why It Matters

The random walk of [18.2](18.2-Random-Walks/README.md) takes steps at ticks $1, 2, 3, \dots$. Many real quantities do not tick: the position of a dust particle in water, the noise on a sensor, the log-price of a share. They move a little at every instant. The limit of the random walk with tiny, quick steps is **Brownian motion**. It is the main continuous-time, continuous-state process (see the table in [18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)). It is the basis of diffusion models in AI, of the Black-Scholes model in finance, and of the theory of stochastic differential equations.

## Intuition

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown looked at pollen grains in water under a microscope. They jittered without stopping. The reason is that the grain is hit by a huge number of water molecules, from all sides, every instant. Each hit is tiny and random. The grain's position after some time is the sum of a very large number of tiny independent pushes.

Now think about the coin game of [18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md): $S_n$ is the sum of $n$ steps $\pm 1$. Let us speed it up. In every unit of time we make $n$ steps, and each step has size $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. Why this size? After time $t$ we have made $nt$ steps. The variance of the position is $nt \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \right)^2 = t$, whatever $n$ is. A larger step would make the variance blow up, and a smaller step would make the particle stand still. The size $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ is the only one that gives a limit.

By the Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)), the position at time $t$ is nearly normal with variance $t$, and for large $n$ this is exact in the limit. The limit process is Brownian motion.

## Definition

A **standard Brownian motion** (also called a **Wiener process**) is a stochastic process $\lbrace B(t) : t \ge 0 \rbrace$ in continuous time and continuous state ([18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)) with these four properties:

1. $B(0) = 0$.
2. **Independent increments:** for times $0 \le t_0 \lt t_1 \lt \dots \lt t_k$, the increments $B(t_1) - B(t_0), \dots, B(t_k) - B(t_{k-1})$ are independent.
3. **Normal increments:** for $0 \le s \lt t$,

$$
B(t) - B(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)
$$

4. **Continuous paths:** with probability 1, the function $t \mapsto B(t)$ is continuous.

Here the second parameter of $\mathcal{N}$ is the variance, so the increment over a time span of length $u$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{u}$.

**In words:** Brownian motion starts at 0, makes independent normal changes over separate time spans, the variance of a change equals the length of the time span, and it never jumps.

**Consequences.** From the definition we get at once:

- $B(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)$, so $E[B(t)] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(B(t)) = t$.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t)) = \min(s, t)$ (proved below).
- Any vector $(B(t_1), \dots, B(t_k))$ is jointly normal, because it is a sum of independent normal increments ([12.9](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md)).
- **Scaling:** for any $c \gt 0$, the process $\frac{B(ct)}{\sqrt{c}}$ is again a standard Brownian motion. Zooming in by time $c$ and by space $\sqrt{c}$ gives the same picture.
- **Brownian motion with drift and volatility:** $X(t) = \mu t + \sigma B(t)$ has $X(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu t, \sigma^2 t)$. Here $\mu$ is the drift (the average change per unit time) and $\sigma$ is the volatility (the size of the noise).

**Paths are continuous but very rough.** The paths of Brownian motion are continuous, but they are **nowhere differentiable**: at no time does the path have a slope. This is a deep theorem (Wiener, Paley and Zygmund), and we do not prove it here. We only give the idea in the next section.

## Why It Is True

### The Random Walk Scales to Brownian Motion

Let $S_k$ be the simple random walk with steps $\pm 1$ ([18.2.1](18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md)), and define the speeded-up walk

$$
B_n(t) = \frac{S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

where $\lfloor nt \rfloor$ is $nt$ rounded down. We check each property of the definition in the limit.

**Normal increments.** For $s \lt t$, the increment $B_n(t) - B_n(s)$ is the sum of the steps number $\lfloor ns \rfloor + 1$ to $\lfloor nt \rfloor$, divided by $\sqrt{n}$. That is $k = \lfloor nt \rfloor - \lfloor ns \rfloor \approx n(t - s)$ steps, each with mean 0 and variance 1. Write

$$
B_n(t) - B_n(s) = \sqrt{\frac{k}{n}} \cdot \frac{\text{sum of } k \text{ steps}}{\sqrt{k}}
$$

By the Central Limit Theorem the last fraction tends to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ in distribution, and $\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}} \to \sqrt{t - s}$. So

$$
B_n(t) - B_n(s) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)
$$

**Independent increments.** Increments over non-overlapping time spans use different steps, and the steps are independent, so the increments are independent for every $n$.

These two facts show that all finite-dimensional distributions ([18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md)) of $B_n$ converge to those of Brownian motion. A stronger theorem (Donsker's theorem, stated without proof) says that the whole path of $B_n$ converges, and that the limit has continuous paths. This is the sense in which Brownian motion is the limit of the random walk.

### The Covariance Is the Smaller Time

Take $s \le t$ and split $B(t)$ into $B(s)$ plus a later increment, as in [18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t)) &= \operatorname{Cov}\big(B(s), B(s) + (B(t) - B(s))\big) && \text{(split } B(t) \text{)} \\
&= \operatorname{Var}(B(s)) + \operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t) - B(s)) && \text{(bilinearity)} \\
&= s + 0 = \min(s, t) && \text{(independent increments)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**In words:** two values of Brownian motion share the part of the path before the earlier time, and nothing else.

### Why the Paths Are Rough

Cut the time span $[0, 1]$ into $m$ pieces of length $\frac{1}{m}$. The squared increments are $(\Delta B_k)^2$ with $\Delta B_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{1}{m})$, so $E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \frac{1}{m}$ and $\operatorname{Var}((\Delta B_k)^2) = \frac{2}{m^2}$ (the fourth moment of a normal variable with variance $v$ is $3v^2$). The sum of the $m$ independent squared increments has

$$
E\Big[ \sum_{k=1}^{m} (\Delta B_k)^2 \Big] = m \cdot \frac{1}{m} = 1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}\Big( \sum_{k=1}^{m} (\Delta B_k)^2 \Big) = m \cdot \frac{2}{m^2} = \frac{2}{m} \to 0
$$

So the sum of the squared increments tends to 1, not to 0. This number is called the **quadratic variation** of $B$ on $[0, 1]$, and it equals the length of the time span.

Now compare with a smooth path. If a path had slope at most $C$ everywhere, every increment would be at most $\frac{C}{m}$, and the sum of squares would be at most $m \cdot \frac{C^2}{m^2} = \frac{C^2}{m} \to 0$. A Brownian path cannot be like this. Its increment over a short time $h$ is of size $\sqrt{h}$, and so the slope $\frac{B(h)}{h}$ is of size $\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}$, which grows without bound when $h \to 0$. This is the idea behind "continuous but nowhere differentiable". The simulation below shows the slope growing.

### The Reflection Principle for Brownian Motion

The mirror argument of [18.2.3](18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.3-Reflection-Principle.md) passes to the limit. For a level $a \gt 0$: every path that touches $a$ before time $t$ and ends below $a$ has a mirror path, reflected after the first touch, that ends above $a$. After the first touch the path starts afresh, and the normal law is symmetric, so both have the same probability. Therefore the maximum $M(t) = \max_{0 \le s \le t} B(s)$ satisfies

$$
P(M(t) \ge a) = 2 \thinspace P(B(t) \ge a) = 2\left(1 - \Phi\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. (We state this without a full proof.)

**In words:** the chance that the path ever reaches a level is twice the chance that it ends above that level.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Probabilities for Brownian Motion

**Problem.** Let $B$ be a standard Brownian motion. Find (a) $P(B(4) \gt 3)$, (b) $P(B(5) - B(2) \lt -1)$, (c) the correlation of $B(2)$ and $B(5)$, (d) the mean and variance of $B(1)$ given $B(4) = 2$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) $B(4) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$, so the standard deviation is 2:

$$
P(B(4) \gt 3) = P\left(Z \gt \frac{3}{2}\right) = 1 - \Phi(1.5) = 0.0668
$$

2. (b) The increment over a span of 3 has variance 3, so its standard deviation is $\sqrt{3} = 1.732$:

$$
P(B(5) - B(2) \lt -1) = \Phi\left(\frac{-1}{1.732}\right) = \Phi(-0.5774) = 0.2819
$$

3. (c) $\operatorname{Cov}(B(2), B(5)) = \min(2, 5) = 2$, with $\operatorname{Var}(B(2)) = 2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(B(5)) = 5$:

$$
\rho = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2 \cdot 5}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} = 0.632
$$

4. (d) The pair $(B(1), B(4))$ is jointly normal with means 0, variances 1 and 4, and covariance 1. By the conditional law of a bivariate normal ([12.7](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.7-Conditionals.md)):

$$
E[B(1) \mid B(4) = 2] = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}}{\operatorname{Var}(B(4))} \cdot 2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 0.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(B(1) \mid B(4) = 2) = 1 - \frac{1^2}{4} = 0.75
$$

**Answer.** (a) $0.0668$. (b) $0.2819$. (c) $0.632$. (d) mean $0.5$, variance $0.75$.

**Check.** In (d) the best guess of $B(1)$ is the straight-line value: a quarter of the way from 0 to 2. The correlation in (c) is the same $\sqrt{\frac{s}{t}}$ pattern as for the random walk in [18.1](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md): $\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}} = 0.632$.

### Example 2: A Share Price with Drift

**Problem.** The log of a share price is modelled as $X(t) = 0.05t + 0.2B(t)$, with $t$ in years (drift 5% a year, volatility 20% a year). Find the probability that the share has a higher price after one year, and the probability that it falls by more than 10% within 3 months.

**Solution.**

1. At $t = 1$: $X(1) \sim \mathcal{N}(0.05, 0.2^2)$ with standard deviation $0.2$. The price is higher when $X(1) \gt 0$:

$$
P(X(1) \gt 0) = P\left(Z \gt \frac{0 - 0.05}{0.2}\right) = P(Z \gt -0.25) = \Phi(0.25) = 0.5987
$$

2. At $t = 0.25$: the mean is $0.05 \times 0.25 = 0.0125$ and the variance is $0.2^2 \times 0.25 = 0.01$, so the standard deviation is $0.1$.
3. A fall of more than 10% means that the price ratio is below $0.9$, so $X(0.25) \lt \ln 0.9 = -0.10536$:

$$
P(X(0.25) \lt -0.10536) = \Phi\left(\frac{-0.10536 - 0.0125}{0.1}\right) = \Phi(-1.1786) = 0.1193
$$

**Answer.** About 59.9% for a higher price after one year, and about 11.9% for a fall of more than 10% at the end of 3 months.

**Check.** With a small drift and a large volatility, the price goes up a bit more often than down, but not by much (just under 60%). Volatility dominates over short times, because the noise grows like $\sqrt{t}$ and the drift like $t$.

### Example 3: Will the Path Ever Reach a Level?

**Problem.** For a standard Brownian motion, find the probability that the path reaches the level 2 at some time in $[0, 4]$. How does this change on $[0, 16]$?

**Solution.**

1. Use the reflection principle with $a = 2$ and $t = 4$, so $\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$:

$$
P(M(4) \ge 2) = 2(1 - \Phi(1)) = 2 \times 0.1587 = 0.3173
$$

2. Compare with the probability of being above 2 at time 4: $P(B(4) \ge 2) = 0.1587$, which is exactly half.
3. For $t = 16$, $\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}} = \frac{2}{4} = 0.5$:

$$
P(M(16) \ge 2) = 2(1 - \Phi(0.5)) = 2 \times 0.3085 = 0.6171
$$

**Answer.** $0.3173$ on $[0, 4]$ and $0.6171$ on $[0, 16]$.

**Check.** As $t \to \infty$, $\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}} \to 0$ and the probability tends to $2(1 - \Phi(0)) = 1$. So Brownian motion reaches every level sooner or later, as the fair random walk does ([18.2.4](18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.4-Recurrence-and-Transience.md)). The simulation below gives $0.3181$.

## Simulate It

The code checks three things. First, a scaled $\pm 1$ random walk has the variance, covariance and independent increments of Brownian motion. Second, squared increments add up to the time span while the slope $\frac{B(h)}{h}$ grows. Third, the maximum follows the reflection principle.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Part 1: a scaled random walk, B_n(t) = S_(nt) / sqrt(n), behaves like Brownian motion.
n, n_paths = 1_000, 20_000
walk = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(n_paths, n)).cumsum(axis=1) / np.sqrt(n)
b_half, b_one = walk[:, n // 2 - 1], walk[:, -1]    # B(0.5) and B(1)
print(f"Var B(1) = {b_one.var():.3f} (exact 1), P(B(1) <= 1) = {np.mean(b_one <= 1):.4f} (exact {norm.cdf(1):.4f})")
print(f"Cov(B(0.5), B(1)) = {np.cov(b_half, b_one)[0, 1]:.3f} (exact 0.5)")
print(f"Corr(B(0.5), B(1) - B(0.5)) = {np.corrcoef(b_half, b_one - b_half)[0, 1]:.4f} (exact 0)")

# Part 2: the squared increments add up to the time (quadratic variation), while the slope B(h)/h explodes.
for m in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000]:
    dB = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / m), size=(2_000, m))          # increments over time h = 1/m
    qv = (dB ** 2).sum(axis=1)
    print(f"h = 1/{m:<6} sum of squares = {qv.mean():.3f} (sd {qv.std():.3f}),  mean |B(h)/h| = {np.abs(dB[:, 0] * m).mean():7.1f}")

# Part 3: reflection principle, P(max of B on [0, 4] >= 2) = 2 P(B(4) >= 2).
hits = 0
for _ in range(10):                                              # 10 batches keep the memory small
    path = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(0.001), size=(2_000, 4_000)).cumsum(axis=1)   # steps of 0.001
    hits += np.sum(path.max(axis=1) >= 2)
print(f"P(max >= 2) = {hits / 20_000:.4f} (exact {2 * (1 - norm.cdf(1)):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Var B(1) = 1.003 (exact 1), P(B(1) <= 1) = 0.8380 (exact 0.8413)
Cov(B(0.5), B(1)) = 0.504 (exact 0.5)
Corr(B(0.5), B(1) - B(0.5)) = -0.0074 (exact 0)
h = 1/10     sum of squares = 1.012 (sd 0.458),  mean |B(h)/h| =     2.5
h = 1/100    sum of squares = 0.997 (sd 0.135),  mean |B(h)/h| =     7.9
h = 1/1000   sum of squares = 1.000 (sd 0.045),  mean |B(h)/h| =    25.7
h = 1/10000  sum of squares = 1.000 (sd 0.014),  mean |B(h)/h| =    80.5
P(max >= 2) = 0.3181 (exact 0.3173)
```

The sum of squares stays at 1 while its spread shrinks like $\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}}$ (that is $0.447$ for $m = 10$ and $0.0141$ for $m = 10{,}000$). The mean slope grows like $\frac{0.8}{\sqrt{h}}$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing variance and standard deviation.** The increment over a span $u$ has **variance** $u$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{u}$. Over a span of 4 the standard deviation is 2, not 4.
- **Adding standard deviations.** For independent spans the variances add: $\operatorname{Var}(B(3) - B(1)) + \operatorname{Var}(B(5) - B(3)) = 2 + 2 = 4$, so the standard deviation is 2, not $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2}$.
- **Treating $B(s)$ and $B(t)$ as independent.** Only increments over non-overlapping spans are independent. The values are correlated, with $\operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t)) = \min(s, t)$.
- **Scaling time wrongly.** $B(4t)$ is not $4B(t)$. It has the same law as $2B(t)$, because the standard deviation grows like $\sqrt{t}$. This is the scaling property.
- **Using ordinary calculus on the path.** A Brownian path has no slope, and $(B(t + h) - B(t))^2$ is of size $h$, not $h^2$. Rules such as "the square of a small change is negligible" fail. This is why stochastic calculus exists (it is outside this book).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** diffusion models add noise to an image step by step, and this forward process is a discretized Brownian motion; Langevin sampling and stochastic gradient noise are also built from it ([21.1.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** Gaussian processes use the covariance $\min(s, t)$ as a simple smoothness model for functions; random walks on large networks look like Brownian motion when seen from far away.
- **Finance:** the log of a share price is modelled as Brownian motion with drift, which leads to the Black-Scholes formula for the price of an option ([21.5](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Physics and biology:** diffusion of particles, heat and molecules in a cell ([21.8](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.8-Physics.md)).
- **Everyday life:** smoke spreading in a still room and the jitter of a noisy sensor reading.

**1. (Easy)** For a standard Brownian motion, find $P(B(9) \gt 6)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$B(9) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 9)$, with standard deviation 3. So

$$P(B(9) \gt 6) = P(Z \gt 2) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 0.0228$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Find $\operatorname{Var}(B(7) - B(3))$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(B(3), B(7))$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The increment over a span of $7 - 3 = 4$ has variance 4. The covariance is the smaller time:

$$\operatorname{Var}(B(7) - B(3)) = 4, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(B(3), B(7)) = \min(3, 7) = 3$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X(t) = 2t + 3B(t)$. Find the law of $X(4)$ and $P(X(4) \gt 14)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X(4) = 8 + 3B(4)$, and $B(4) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$, so $3B(4) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 36)$. Hence $X(4) \sim \mathcal{N}(8, 36)$, with standard deviation 6:

$$P(X(4) \gt 14) = P\left(Z \gt \frac{14 - 8}{6}\right) = P(Z \gt 1) \approx 0.1587$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Find $\operatorname{Var}(B(1) + B(2) + B(3))$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write the sum with independent increments $A = B(1)$, $C = B(2) - B(1)$, $D = B(3) - B(2)$. Then $B(1) = A$, $B(2) = A + C$, $B(3) = A + C + D$, and

$$B(1) + B(2) + B(3) = 3A + 2C + D$$

Each of $A, C, D$ has variance 1 and they are independent, so the variance is $9 + 4 + 1 = 14$. Check with covariances: $1 + 2 + 3 + 2(1 + 1 + 2) = 6 + 8 = 14$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $T_a$ be the first time that $B$ reaches the level $a \gt 0$. Use the reflection principle to show that $P(T_a \le t) = 2(1 - \Phi(\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}}))$. Then find the median of $T_1$, and show that $E[T_a] = \infty$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The event $T_a \le t$ is the same as $M(t) \ge a$ (the path reaches $a$ by time $t$), so the reflection principle gives

$$P(T_a \le t) = 2\left(1 - \Phi\left(\tfrac{a}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)$$

For the median of $T_1$ solve $2(1 - \Phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}})) = \frac{1}{2}$, that is $\Phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}) = 0.75$. The 75% point of the standard normal is $0.6745$, so $\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} = 0.6745$ and $t = \frac{1}{0.6745^2} \approx 2.198$.

For the mean, $E[T_a] = \int_0^{\infty} P(T_a \gt t) \thinspace dt$ (the tail formula for the mean, [Module 06](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/README.md)) with $P(T_a \gt t) = 2\Phi(\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}}) - 1$. For large $t$ the argument $x = \frac{a}{\sqrt{t}}$ is small, and $2\Phi(x) - 1 \approx 2 x \phi(0) = \frac{2a}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}$. Since $\int^{\infty} \frac{dt}{\sqrt{t}}$ diverges, $E[T_a] = \infty$. The path reaches every level for sure, but the mean waiting time is infinite, like the fair random walk of [18.2.2](18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md) against unlimited money.

</details>

## Summary

- Brownian motion is the limit of the random walk with $n$ steps of size $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ per unit time. The size $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ keeps the variance equal to $t$.
- It is defined by $B(0) = 0$, independent increments, $B(t) - B(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)$ and continuous paths. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t)) = \min(s, t)$.
- Its paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable: squared increments add up to the time span, so the slope $\frac{B(h)}{h}$ grows like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}$.
- The reflection principle gives $P(\max_{s \le t} B(s) \ge a) = 2P(B(t) \ge a)$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{B(t) - B(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s), \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t)) = \min(s, t)}
$$
