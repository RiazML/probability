## How to Use This Set

- Each problem says which topic it uses. If you get stuck, reread that topic first.
- Try every problem on paper before opening the answer. Write each step, as in the worked examples.
- **Warm-Up** problems check the basic formulas. **Core** problems mix two or three ideas. **Challenge** problems include proofs. **Simulation Projects** ask you to write code.
- The first question to ask is: **what is the state, and what is the time?** Discrete or continuous time, and a small set of states or a number line, tell you which tool to use.
- After solving a problem by hand, check the number with a short simulation. The code in the earlier files is a good starting point.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy)** *Uses [18.2.1 Simple Random Walk](18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.1-Simple-Random-Walk.md) and [18.1 What is a Stochastic Process](18.1-What-is-a-Stochastic-Process.md).*
A fair walk takes steps $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, starting at $S_0 = 0$. Find $E[S_{16}]$, $\operatorname{Var}(S_{16})$, and $P(S_4 = 2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The steps have mean 0 and variance 1, so

$$E[S_{16}] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_{16}) = 16 \cdot 1 = 16$$

The standard deviation is 4. After 4 steps, $S_4 = 2K - 4$ where $K$ is the number of up-steps. $S_4 = 2$ means $K = 3$:

$$P(S_4 = 2) = \binom{4}{3} \left( \tfrac{1}{2} \right)^4 = \tfrac{4}{16} = \tfrac{1}{4}$$

</details>

**W2. (Easy)** *Uses [18.2.2 Gambler's Ruin](18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md).*
You have 3 taka. Each round you win 1 taka or lose 1 taka with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. You stop when you reach 10 taka or 0 taka. Find the probability that you reach 10, and the expected number of rounds.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a fair game started at $k$ with target $N$, the chance to reach $N$ is $\frac{k}{N}$ and the expected duration is $k(N - k)$:

$$P(\text{reach } 10) = \frac{3}{10} = 0.3, \qquad E[\text{rounds}] = 3 \cdot (10 - 3) = 21$$

</details>

**W3. (Easy)** *Uses [18.3.2 Transition Matrix and N-Step Transitions](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.2-Transition-Matrix-and-N-Step-Transitions.md).*
Each day is Dry or Wet. After a Dry day, the next day is Dry with probability $0.8$. After a Wet day, the next day is Dry with probability $0.4$. Today is Wet. Find the probability that the day after tomorrow is Wet.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The rows of $P$ (order Dry, Wet) are $(0.8, 0.2)$ and $(0.4, 0.6)$. We need the entry (Wet, Wet) of $P^2$: go Wet to Dry to Wet, or Wet to Wet to Wet:

$$p^{(2)}_{WW} = 0.4 \times 0.2 + 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.08 + 0.36 = 0.44$$

</details>

**W4. (Easy)** *Uses [18.3.5 Stationary Distribution](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md).*
For the chain of W3, find the stationary distribution and the mean number of days between two Wet days.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Balance for Wet: the flow Dry to Wet equals the flow Wet to Dry, so $0.2 \pi_D = 0.4 \pi_W$. With $\pi_D + \pi_W = 1$ we get $\pi_D = 2 \pi_W$ and

$$\pi = \left( \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3} \right)$$

The mean return time to Wet is $\frac{1}{\pi_W} = 3$ days.

</details>

**W5. (Easy)** *Uses [18.8 Brownian Motion](18.8-Brownian-Motion.md).*
For a standard Brownian motion $B$, find $P(-1 \le B(1) \le 1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(B(6) - B(2))$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$B(1) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, so the probability is the chance that a standard normal is within one standard deviation of its mean:

$$P(-1 \le B(1) \le 1) = \Phi(1) - \Phi(-1) \approx 0.6827$$

The increment over a span of $6 - 2 = 4$ has variance $4$.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium)** *Uses [18.3.7 Absorbing Chains](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.7-Absorbing-Chains.md).*
You flip a fair coin again and again until two heads appear in a row (HH). What is the expected number of flips?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the states $0$ (no useful progress, the last flip was a tail or we have just started), $H$ (the last flip was a head), and HH (done). Let $t_0$ and $t_H$ be the expected numbers of flips still needed. First-step equations:

$$t_0 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_0 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_H, \qquad t_H = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} t_0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0$$

The second gives $t_H = 1 + \frac{t_0}{2}$. Put this in the first: $t_0 = 1 + \frac{t_0}{2} + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{t_0}{4}$, so $\frac{t_0}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$ and

$$t_0 = 6, \qquad t_H = 4$$

Check with the fundamental matrix ([18.3.7](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.7-Absorbing-Chains.md)). The transient part of the matrix has the rows $(0.5, 0.5)$ and $(0.5, 0)$. Then $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$ has the rows $(4, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$, and its row sums are $6$ and $4$, the same expected numbers of flips.

</details>

**C2. (Medium)** *Uses [18.3.6 Detailed Balance and Reversibility](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.6-Detailed-Balance-and-Reversibility.md).*
Four computers A, B, C, D are connected by the cables A-B, B-C, C-D, D-A and A-C. A packet moves at each step along a uniformly chosen cable of its current computer. Find the long-run share of time at each computer, and the mean number of steps between two visits to B.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The degrees (number of cables) are $3, 2, 3, 2$ for A, B, C, D, and there are $5$ cables. For a walk on an undirected graph, $\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{2E}$ with $2E = 10$:

$$\pi = (0.3, 0.2, 0.3, 0.2)$$

The mean return time to B is $\frac{1}{\pi_B} = \frac{1}{0.2} = 5$ steps.

</details>

**C3. (Medium)** *Uses [18.4 Continuous-Time Markov Chains](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md).*
A job cycles through three stages. It leaves stage A at rate 1 (going to B), leaves B at rate 2 (going to C), and leaves C at rate 4 (going back to A). Find the long-run share of time in each stage, and the mean time of one full cycle.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Balance equations (rate out equals rate in): $1 \cdot \pi_A = 4 \pi_C$, $2 \pi_B = 1 \cdot \pi_A$ and $4 \pi_C = 2 \pi_B$. So $\pi_C = \frac{\pi_A}{4}$ and $\pi_B = \frac{\pi_A}{2}$. The sum is $\pi_A (1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}) = \frac{7}{4} \pi_A = 1$:

$$\pi = \left( \tfrac{4}{7}, \tfrac{2}{7}, \tfrac{1}{7} \right)$$

The mean cycle time is the sum of the mean holding times, $1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{7}{4}$. The share of time in A is the mean time in A divided by the cycle time, $\frac{1}{7/4} = \frac{4}{7}$, which agrees.

</details>

**C4. (Medium)** *Uses [18.5 Poisson Process](18.5-Poisson-Process.md).*
Customers enter a shop as a Poisson process at rate 20 per hour. Each customer is a child with probability $0.3$, independently. (a) Find the probability that at least one child enters in 15 minutes. (b) Given that exactly 4 customers entered during the hour, find the probability that exactly one of them came in the first 15 minutes.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Children are a thinned Poisson process with rate $0.3 \times 20 = 6$ per hour. In $0.25$ hour the mean is $1.5$:

$$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - e^{-1.5} \approx 0.7769$$

(b) Given 4 arrivals in the hour, they are 4 independent uniform points. Each is in the first 15 minutes with probability $\frac{1}{4}$, so the count is $\text{Bin}(4, \frac{1}{4})$:

$$P(\text{exactly one}) = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \left( \tfrac{3}{4} \right)^3 = \tfrac{27}{64} \approx 0.4219$$

</details>

**C5. (Medium)** *Uses [18.6 Branching Processes](18.6-Branching-Processes.md).*
A cell has 0, 1 or 2 daughter cells with probabilities $0.1$, $0.4$, $0.5$. Find the mean number of daughters, the extinction probability of a line started from one cell and from three cells, and $E[Z_5]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $m = 0.4 + 2 \cdot 0.5 = 1.4$, which is above 1. The PGF is $G(s) = 0.1 + 0.4 s + 0.5 s^2$. Solve $s = G(s)$, that is $0.5 s^2 - 0.6 s + 0.1 = 0$, or $5 s^2 - 6 s + 1 = 0$:

$$s = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 20}}{10} = \frac{6 \pm 4}{10} \in \lbrace 1, 0.2 \rbrace$$

The smallest root is $q = 0.2$. From three cells all three lines must die, so the probability is $q^3 = 0.008$. The mean size is $E[Z_5] = 1.4^5 = 5.378$.

</details>

**C6. (Medium)** *Uses [18.7 Queues Intuition](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md).*
Customers reach a single-server counter at rate $\lambda = 6$ per minute, and the server has rate $\mu = 8$ per minute. Find $\rho$, the mean number in the system, the mean time in the system, the mean number waiting in the queue, and $P(N \ge 4)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The load is $\rho = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75$. Then:

$$L = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} = 3, \qquad W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{1}{2} \text{ minute}, \qquad L_q = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho} = \frac{0.5625}{0.25} = 2.25$$

$$P(N \ge 4) = \rho^4 = 0.75^4 \approx 0.3164$$

Check with Little's law: $\lambda W = 6 \times 0.5 = 3 = L$.

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [18.5 Poisson Process](18.5-Poisson-Process.md).*
Cars pass a point as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$. You arrive at a fixed time $t$. Let $A$ be the time since the last car (the age, taken as $t$ if no car has passed) and $R$ the time until the next car (the residual). Show that the gap that covers your arrival has mean

$$E[A + R] = \frac{2 - e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}$$

and explain why this is almost twice the mean gap $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ for large $t$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Residual.** The next car is later than $r$ after time $t$ exactly when there is no car in the window $(t, t + r]$. This window has length $r$, so

$$P(R \gt r) = e^{-\lambda r}$$

and $R \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, with $E[R] = \frac{1}{\lambda}$.

**Age.** The age is more than $a$ (for $0 \le a \lt t$) exactly when there is no car in $(t - a, t]$, so $P(A \gt a) = e^{-\lambda a}$. The age is never more than $t$, so $P(A \gt a) = 0$ for $a \ge t$. Then

$$E[A] = \int_0^{t} P(A \gt a) \thinspace da = \int_0^{t} e^{-\lambda a} \thinspace da = \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}$$

Add the two means (linearity of expectation needs no independence):

$$E[A + R] = \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda} + \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{2 - e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}$$

For large $t$ this tends to $\frac{2}{\lambda}$. This is the **inspection paradox**: a long gap has more room to cover the fixed time $t$ than a short gap, so the gap that you land in is longer than a typical gap. Looking forward, you wait the full mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ again, because of memorylessness; looking back, the age is also about $\frac{1}{\lambda}$.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [18.4 Continuous-Time Markov Chains](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md) and [18.5 Poisson Process](18.5-Poisson-Process.md).*
The count $N(t)$ of a Poisson process is a continuous-time Markov chain on $0, 1, 2, \dots$ with $q_{n, n+1} = \lambda$ and no other jumps. Write the forward equations for $p_n(t) = P(N(t) = n)$ with $N(0) = 0$, and check that $p_n(t) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^n}{n!}$ solves them.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The generator has $q_{nn} = -\lambda$ and $q_{n, n+1} = \lambda$. The forward equation $p'(t) = p(t) Q$ for the row vector $p(t) = (p_0(t), p_1(t), \dots)$ says that the entry $n$ changes by the flow in from $n - 1$ minus the flow out of $n$:

$$p_0'(t) = -\lambda p_0(t), \qquad p_n'(t) = -\lambda p_n(t) + \lambda p_{n-1}(t) \quad (n \ge 1)$$

with $p_0(0) = 1$ and $p_n(0) = 0$ for $n \ge 1$. The equations can be solved one after the other (first $p_0$, then $p_1$, and so on), and each step is a linear equation with a unique solution, so the whole solution is unique. Now test the formula. For $n = 0$ we get $p_0 = e^{-\lambda t}$, with derivative $-\lambda e^{-\lambda t} = -\lambda p_0$. For $n \ge 1$, by the product rule:

$$\frac{d}{dt} \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^n}{n!} = -\lambda \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^n}{n!} + \lambda \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} = -\lambda p_n(t) + \lambda p_{n-1}(t)$$

The initial values also fit ($p_0(0) = 1$, and $p_n(0) = 0$ for $n \ge 1$). So $N(t) \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda t)$, found from the generator alone.

</details>

**H3. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [18.7 Queues Intuition](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md) and [15.1 Moment Generating Function](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md).*
In the M/M/1 queue with $\rho \lt 1$, an arriving customer finds $n$ customers with probability $(1 - \rho) \rho^n$, and its time in the system $W$ is the sum of $n + 1$ independent $\text{Exp}(\mu)$ times. Show that $W \sim \text{Exp}(\mu - \lambda)$. Then, for $\lambda = 8$ and $\mu = 10$, find $P(W \gt 1)$ and the 95th percentile of $W$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $\theta \lt \mu - \lambda$, the MGF of one $\text{Exp}(\mu)$ time is $u = \frac{\mu}{\mu - \theta}$, so the MGF of the sum of $n + 1$ of them is $u^{n+1}$. Average over $n$ with the geometric weights (note $\rho u \lt 1$ here):

$$E[e^{\theta W}] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (1 - \rho) \rho^n u^{n+1} = \frac{(1 - \rho) u}{1 - \rho u} = \frac{(1 - \rho) \mu}{(\mu - \theta) - \rho \mu} = \frac{(1 - \rho) \mu}{(1 - \rho) \mu - \theta}$$

Since $(1 - \rho) \mu = \mu - \lambda$, this is $\frac{\mu - \lambda}{(\mu - \lambda) - \theta}$, the MGF of $\text{Exp}(\mu - \lambda)$. By uniqueness of the MGF ([15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)), $W \sim \text{Exp}(\mu - \lambda)$, with mean $\frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$, as in 18.7.

For $\lambda = 8$ and $\mu = 10$ the rate is $2$:

$$P(W \gt 1) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$$

The 95th percentile solves $e^{-2w} = 0.05$, so $w = \frac{\ln 20}{2} \approx 1.498$ seconds. The mean is only $0.5$ seconds, but 5% of the customers wait more than 1.5 seconds.

</details>

**H4. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [18.8 Brownian Motion](18.8-Brownian-Motion.md).*
Let $B$ be a standard Brownian motion and define the **Brownian bridge** $X(t) = B(t) - t B(1)$ for $0 \le t \le 1$. Show that $\operatorname{Cov}(X(s), X(t)) = s(1 - t)$ for $s \le t$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X(t))$ and explain the values at $t = 0$ and $t = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand the covariance by bilinearity and use $\operatorname{Cov}(B(u), B(v)) = \min(u, v)$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X(s), X(t)) = \operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(t)) - t \operatorname{Cov}(B(s), B(1)) - s \operatorname{Cov}(B(1), B(t)) + st \operatorname{Var}(B(1))$$

For $s \le t \le 1$ the four terms are $s$, $-ts$, $-st$ and $st$. They add up to

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X(s), X(t)) = s - st = s(1 - t)$$

Taking $s = t$ gives $\operatorname{Var}(X(t)) = t(1 - t)$. At $t = 0$ and at $t = 1$ the variance is 0: the bridge starts at 0 and returns to 0 ($X(1) = B(1) - B(1) = 0$). The variance is largest in the middle, $\operatorname{Var}(X(\frac{1}{2})) = \frac{1}{4}$.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Poisson process and the inspection paradox)** *Uses [18.5 Poisson Process](18.5-Poisson-Process.md) and H1.*
Simulate a Poisson process with rate $\lambda = 1$ by adding exponential gaps. For $t = 0.5, 2, 10$, find the gap that covers the fixed time $t$ and average its length over 100,000 runs. Compare with the exact value $\frac{2 - e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}$ of H1 and with the mean gap.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs, rate = 100_000, 1.0

for t in [0.5, 2.0, 10.0]:
    arrivals = rng.exponential(1 / rate, size=(n_runs, 60)).cumsum(axis=1)
    before = np.concatenate([np.zeros((n_runs, 1)), arrivals], axis=1)   # S_0 = 0, S_1, S_2, ...
    k = (arrivals <= t).sum(axis=1)                                      # N(t)
    start = before[np.arange(n_runs), k]                                 # last arrival at or before t
    end = before[np.arange(n_runs), k + 1]                               # first arrival after t
    length = end - start
    print(f"t = {t:>4}: mean covering interval {length.mean():.4f}, exact {(2 - np.exp(-rate * t)) / rate:.4f}")
print("mean ordinary gap: 1.0000")
```

Output:

```text
t =  0.5: mean covering interval 1.3956, exact 1.3935
t =  2.0: mean covering interval 1.8636, exact 1.8647
t = 10.0: mean covering interval 2.0027, exact 2.0000
mean ordinary gap: 1.0000
```

The covering gap is almost twice as long as an ordinary gap for large $t$, as H1 says.

</details>

**S2. (An M/M/1 queue as a continuous-time chain)** *Uses [18.4 Continuous-Time Markov Chains](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md) and [18.7 Queues Intuition](18.7-Queues-Intuition.md).*
Simulate the number in an M/M/1 queue with $\lambda = 3$ and $\mu = 4$ using only the recipe of 18.4: wait an exponential time with the total leaving rate, then jump up with probability $\frac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}$ (or up with probability 1 from state 0). Find the share of time spent in each state and compare it with $(1 - \rho) \rho^n$ and with $L = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
lam, mu = 3.0, 4.0                                    # arrival and service rates
k_max, n_events = 60, 3_000_000
spent = np.zeros(k_max + 1)                           # time spent with n customers
n = 0
for _ in range(n_events):
    rate_down = mu if n > 0 else 0.0
    total = lam + rate_down                           # total rate of leaving state n
    spent[n] += rng.exponential(1 / total)            # holding time ~ Exp(total)
    n += 1 if rng.random() < lam / total else -1      # jump up with probability lam / total

share = spent / spent.sum()
rho = lam / mu
print("n    simulation   exact (1 - rho) rho^n")
for i in range(5):
    print(f"{i}    {share[i]:.4f}       {(1 - rho) * rho ** i:.4f}")
print(f"mean number in system: simulation {(share * np.arange(k_max + 1)).sum():.3f}, exact {rho / (1 - rho):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
n    simulation   exact (1 - rho) rho^n
0    0.2505       0.2500
1    0.1877       0.1875
2    0.1413       0.1406
3    0.1067       0.1055
4    0.0794       0.0791
mean number in system: simulation 2.969, exact 3.000
```

The time shares match the geometric law. The mean is a little low because the rare long excursions are hard to see in a finite run: the load $\rho = 0.75$ is high.

</details>
