# 18.7 Queues Intuition

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 18: Stochastic Processes](README.md) / 18.7 Queues Intuition

**Level:** 5 (Hero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [18.6 Branching Processes](18.6-Branching-Processes.md)

> **Big idea:** A queue with random arrivals and random service times builds a line even when the server is not overloaded; for the M/M/1 queue the number in the system is geometric with parameter $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}$, its mean $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ explodes as $\rho$ nears 1, and Little's law $L = \lambda W$ links the line length to the waiting time.

---

## Why It Matters

Requests wait for a server. Patients wait for a doctor. Packets wait in a router buffer. Jobs wait for a GPU. In all of these, work arrives at random and is done at random speed, and a line forms. How long is the line? How long does one customer wait? When must we buy a faster server? A queue is the best example of the ideas of this module together: Poisson arrivals ([18.5](18.5-Poisson-Process.md)), a continuous-time Markov chain ([18.4](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md)) and a stationary distribution ([18.3.5](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.5-Stationary-Distribution.md)). The answers are simple formulas, and they hold a surprise.

## Intuition

A web server can process 10 requests per second. Requests arrive at a rate of 8 per second. The server is busy 80% of the time and idle 20% of the time. You might expect a short line. The truth is different.

If requests arrived exactly every $0.125$ seconds and each took exactly $0.1$ seconds, there would never be a line at all. But real arrivals are random. Sometimes 3 requests arrive in a short burst. The server needs 0.3 seconds to clear them, and during this time the next ones join the line. The server can only catch up in the quiet moments, and these come later. The result is that at 80% load the average number of requests in the system is **4**, and the average request spends 0.5 seconds in the system, five times the 0.1 seconds of pure work.

The effect gets very strong when the load is near 100%. At 95% load the average number in the system is 19, and at 99% load it is 99. A server that is 99% busy is almost unusable, even though it is "not overloaded". Randomness, not the average, makes the line.

## Picture

```text
  arrivals (rate λ)        waiting line         server (rate μ)
  ----------------->    [ c ][ c ][ c ]  --->  ( c )  ----------> done

  The number of customers in the system, N(t), as a chain:

         λ          λ          λ          λ
      +---+ ---> +---+ ---> +---+ ---> +---+ ---> ...
      | 0 |      | 1 |      | 2 |      | 3 |
      +---+ <--- +---+ <--- +---+ <--- +---+ <--- ...
         μ          μ          μ          μ
```

New customers come at rate $\lambda$ and push the number up. The server finishes customers at rate $\mu$ and pushes the number down (but only when somebody is there).

## Definition

The **M/M/1 queue** is the following model. (The first M says that arrivals are Markov, that is Poisson. The second M says that service times are Markov, that is exponential. The 1 is the number of servers.)

- Customers arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$ ([18.5](18.5-Poisson-Process.md)).
- There is **one server**. Customers are served in the order of arrival.
- The service times are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\mu)$, independent of the arrivals. Here $\mu$ is the **service rate**, so the mean service time is $\frac{1}{\mu}$.
- The waiting room has unlimited space.

Let $N(t)$ be the **number in the system** (the ones waiting plus the one being served). Because exponential times are memoryless, $N(t)$ is a continuous-time Markov chain ([18.4](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md)): from state $n$ it goes to $n + 1$ at rate $\lambda$, and, if $n \ge 1$, to $n - 1$ at rate $\mu$ (the remaining service time of the customer at the counter is again $\text{Exp}(\mu)$, whatever time has passed).

The **utilization** (or **traffic intensity**) is

$$
\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}
$$

**In words:** $\rho$ is the fraction of time the server is busy, when it can keep up. It is also the mean amount of work that arrives per unit time, measured in server-seconds.

**Theorem 1 (stationary law).** If $\rho \lt 1$, the chain $N(t)$ has the stationary distribution

$$
\pi_n = (1 - \rho) \rho^n, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

If $\rho \ge 1$ there is no stationary distribution: the line grows without limit.

**Theorem 2 (mean number).** For $\rho \lt 1$, the mean number in the system and in the queue (not counting the one in service) are

$$
L = E[N] = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}, \qquad L_q = L - \rho = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}
$$

**Theorem 3 (Little's law).** In any stable queue (not only M/M/1), the mean number in the system equals the arrival rate times the mean time in the system:

$$
L = \lambda W
$$

where $W$ is the mean time a customer spends in the system. The same law holds for the queue alone: $L_q = \lambda W_q$.

**In words:** the number of people in a shop equals the rate at which they come in times how long each one stays. For the M/M/1 queue the mean times are

$$
W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}, \qquad W_q = W - \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{\rho}{\mu - \lambda}
$$

## Why It Is True

**The stationary law.** In the stationary state, the chain crosses each cut between $n$ and $n + 1$ upward as often as downward. The upward flow is $\pi_n \lambda$ (in state $n$, a customer arrives at rate $\lambda$). The downward flow is $\pi_{n+1} \mu$ (in state $n + 1$, a service ends at rate $\mu$). This is detailed balance for a birth-death chain ([18.3.6](18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.6-Detailed-Balance-and-Reversibility.md)), and it follows from the balance equations of [18.4](18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md): state 0 gives $\lambda \pi_0 = \mu \pi_1$, and by induction the balance equation of state $n$, $(\lambda + \mu) \pi_n = \lambda \pi_{n-1} + \mu \pi_{n+1}$, gives $\lambda \pi_n = \mu \pi_{n+1}$. So

$$
\pi_{n+1} = \rho \thinspace \pi_n \implies \pi_n = \rho^n \pi_0
$$

The probabilities must add to 1. For $\rho \lt 1$ the geometric series sums to $\frac{1}{1 - \rho}$:

$$
1 = \pi_0 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \rho^n = \frac{\pi_0}{1 - \rho} \implies \pi_0 = 1 - \rho
$$

For $\rho \ge 1$ the series $\sum \rho^n$ diverges, so the only way to have a finite total is $\pi_0 = 0$, and then all $\pi_n = 0$. There is no stationary distribution. Note also $\pi_0 = 1 - \rho$ says: the server is idle with probability $1 - \rho$, so busy with probability $\rho$.

**The mean.** Use the series $\sum_{n \ge 0} n \rho^n = \frac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2}$ (differentiate the geometric series):

$$
L = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n (1 - \rho) \rho^n = (1 - \rho) \cdot \frac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2} = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}
$$

The server holds one customer with probability $\rho$ (and none otherwise), so the mean number in service is $\rho$ and $L_q = L - \rho = \frac{\rho (1 - (1 - \rho))}{1 - \rho} = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}$.

**Little's law (the idea).** Watch the queue for a long time $T$. Suppose $A$ customers arrive and all of them finish. Add up the area under the curve $N(t)$ in two ways. By columns, the area is $\int_0^T N(t) \thinspace dt$. By customers, each customer $i$ contributes the height 1 for as long as it stays, which is its time in the system $W_i$. So

$$
\int_0^T N(t) \thinspace dt \approx \sum_{i=1}^{A} W_i
$$

The two sides differ only by the customers who are still inside at time $T$, which is a small part when $T$ is large. Divide by $T$ and write $\frac{\sum W_i}{T} = \frac{A}{T} \cdot \frac{\sum W_i}{A}$:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{1}{T} \int_0^T N(t) \thinspace dt}_{L} \approx \underbrace{\frac{A}{T}}_{\lambda} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sum W_i}{A}}_{W}
$$

So $L = \lambda W$. No assumption about the arrival process or the service times was needed.

**The waiting time, as a check.** A customer who arrives finds $n$ customers in the system with probability $\pi_n$ (Poisson arrivals see the system as it is on average; we state this without proof). Its time in the system is the service of those $n$ customers (the one at the counter has $\text{Exp}(\mu)$ time left, by memorylessness) plus its own service: $n + 1$ service times in all. So

$$
W = \frac{E[N] + 1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{\mu} \left( \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} + 1 \right) = \frac{1}{\mu (1 - \rho)} = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}
$$

This is the formula above, found without Little's law. Now check Little: $\lambda W = \frac{\lambda}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} = L$. The two views agree.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Server at 80% Load

**Problem.** Requests arrive at rate $\lambda = 8$ per second at a server with rate $\mu = 10$ per second. Find $\rho$, the probability that the server is idle, the mean number in the system and in the queue, the mean time in the system and in the queue, and $P(N \ge 5)$.

**Solution.**

1. $\rho = \frac{8}{10} = 0.8 \lt 1$, so the queue is stable.
2. The server is idle with probability $\pi_0 = 1 - \rho = 0.2$.
3. The mean number in the system is $L = \frac{0.8}{1 - 0.8} = 4$. In the queue: $L_q = L - \rho = 4 - 0.8 = 3.2$.
4. The mean time in the system is $W = \frac{1}{10 - 8} = 0.5$ seconds. In the queue: $W_q = W - \frac{1}{\mu} = 0.5 - 0.1 = 0.4$ seconds.
5. The tail of a geometric law is $P(N \ge k) = \sum_{n \ge k} (1 - \rho) \rho^n = \rho^k$, so

$$
P(N \ge 5) = 0.8^5 = 0.32768
$$

**Answer.** $\rho = 0.8$, idle 20% of the time, $L = 4$, $L_q = 3.2$, $W = 0.5$ s, $W_q = 0.4$ s, and $P(N \ge 5) \approx 0.328$.

**Check.** Little's law: $\lambda W = 8 \times 0.5 = 4 = L$ and $\lambda W_q = 8 \times 0.4 = 3.2 = L_q$. Both agree. The simulation below gives the same numbers.

### Example 2: Why the Last 10% of Load Is Expensive

**Problem.** The server of Example 1 has $\mu = 10$ per second. Find the mean number in the system and the mean time in the system when $\lambda = 5, 8, 9, 9.5, 9.9$. Then find what happens at $\lambda = 8$ if the server is made twice as fast.

**Solution.**

1. Use $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{10}$, $L = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ and $W = \frac{1}{10 - \lambda}$:

| $\lambda$ | $\rho$ | $L$ | $W$ (seconds) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.5 | 1 | 0.2 |
| 8 | 0.8 | 4 | 0.5 |
| 9 | 0.9 | 9 | 1 |
| 9.5 | 0.95 | 19 | 2 |
| 9.9 | 0.99 | 99 | 10 |

2. For a server twice as fast, $\mu = 20$ and $\lambda = 8$: $\rho = 0.4$, $L = \frac{0.4}{0.6} = 0.667$ and $W = \frac{1}{20 - 8} = 0.0833$ seconds.

**Answer.** Going from 80% to 95% load multiplies the mean waiting time by 4. Doubling the server speed at $\lambda = 8$ cuts the time in the system from $0.5$ to $0.083$ seconds, a factor of 6.

**Check.** The time in the system is $\frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$, so it depends on the gap $\mu - \lambda$, not on the ratio. The gap is 2 at $\lambda = 8$, $0.5$ at $\lambda = 9.5$, and 12 for the fast server. Spare capacity is what keeps the line short.

### Example 3: Little's Law Without a Model

**Problem.** (a) A coffee shop has on average 30 customers inside, and 45 customers walk in per hour. How long does a customer stay? (b) A web service handles 1000 requests per second, and the average latency is $0.2$ seconds. How many requests are in progress at a typical moment?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Use $L = \lambda W$ with $L = 30$ and $\lambda = 45$ per hour:

$$
W = \frac{L}{\lambda} = \frac{30}{45} = \frac{2}{3} \text{ hour} = 40 \text{ minutes}
$$

2. (b) With $\lambda = 1000$ per second and $W = 0.2$ seconds:

$$
L = \lambda W = 1000 \times 0.2 = 200 \text{ requests}
$$

**Answer.** (a) 40 minutes. (b) 200 requests in progress.

**Check.** In (b), the answer tells an engineer that the service needs room for at least 200 requests at once (threads, connections or memory), on average, and more for the peaks. Neither part needed Poisson arrivals or exponential times: Little's law holds for any stable queue.

## Simulate It

The code simulates 2 million customers of the M/M/1 queue with $\lambda = 8$ and $\mu = 10$. A customer's waiting time follows the recursion "wait for the work that is left over by the customer before". Then it counts the number in the system at random times and compares everything with the formulas and with Little's law.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
lam, mu, n = 8.0, 10.0, 2_000_000              # arrival rate, service rate, customers
gap = rng.exponential(1 / lam, n)                # time between arrivals
service = rng.exponential(1 / mu, n)             # service times
arrive = gap.cumsum()

# Lindley recursion: a customer waits for the work the previous customers left behind.
wait = np.zeros(n)
for i in range(1, n):
    wait[i] = max(0.0, wait[i - 1] + service[i - 1] - gap[i])
depart = arrive + wait + service                 # one server, first come first served

# Count the customers in the system at random times (skip the start-up period).
t = rng.uniform(0.1 * arrive[-1], 0.9 * arrive[-1], 400_000)
in_system = np.searchsorted(arrive, t, side="right") - np.searchsorted(depart, t, side="right")

rho = lam / mu
print(f"L  (mean number in system): simulation {in_system.mean():.3f}, exact {rho / (1 - rho):.3f}")
print(f"W  (mean time in system):   simulation {(wait + service).mean():.4f}, exact {1 / (mu - lam):.4f}")
print(f"Wq (mean wait in queue):    simulation {wait.mean():.4f}, exact {rho / (mu - lam):.4f}")
print(f"P(N = 0): simulation {np.mean(in_system == 0):.4f}, exact {1 - rho:.4f}")
print(f"P(N >= 5): simulation {np.mean(in_system >= 5):.4f}, exact {rho ** 5:.4f}")
rate_seen = n / arrive[-1]                       # measured arrival rate
print(f"Little: rate x W = {rate_seen * (wait + service).mean():.3f}, L = {in_system.mean():.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
L  (mean number in system): simulation 3.963, exact 4.000
W  (mean time in system):   simulation 0.4973, exact 0.5000
Wq (mean wait in queue):    simulation 0.3973, exact 0.4000
P(N = 0): simulation 0.2001, exact 0.2000
P(N >= 5): simulation 0.3260, exact 0.3277
Little: rate x W = 3.981, L = 3.963
```

> **Tip:** At 80% load a queue needs a very long run to settle, because a line, once built, lasts long. With 500,000 customers the estimate of $L$ was $4.064$; with 2 million it is $3.963$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that load below 100% means no line.** At $\rho = 0.8$ the mean number in the system is 4. Lines come from random bursts, not only from overload.
- **Mixing up rate and mean time.** The service rate $\mu = 10$ per second means a mean service time of $0.1$ seconds. The rates $\lambda$ and $\mu$ must use the same time unit.
- **Expecting a linear effect.** The mean time $\frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$ is small for a long time and then shoots up: $\lambda = 8, 9, 9.5, 9.9$ give $0.5, 1, 2, 10$ seconds.
- **Confusing "in the system" with "in the queue".** $L$ counts the customer in service, and $L_q$ does not: $L = L_q + \rho$. The same holds for $W$ and $W_q$.
- **Using the formulas when $\rho \ge 1$.** Then there is no stationary law, and the line grows without limit. The formulas give negative or infinite nonsense.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** an inference server with a request queue, a GPU job scheduler, and batching decisions: more load per server means a longer wait for every request ([21.1](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/README.md)).
- **Computer science:** packet buffers in routers, thread pools, disk and database queues, and sizing a system with Little's law ([21.3.4](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
- **Engineering and operations:** call centres, hospitals, factory lines and airport check-in desks ([21.7](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)).
- **Everyday life:** supermarket checkouts, bus stops and road traffic. One fast checkout is better than the same speed split into slower ones, and a little spare capacity helps a lot.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Customers arrive at a counter at rate $\lambda = 3$ per minute, and the server has rate $\mu = 5$ per minute. Find $\rho$, $L$, $W$ and the probability that the server is idle.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\rho = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$. The mean number is $L = \frac{0.6}{0.4} = 1.5$. The mean time is $W = \frac{1}{5 - 3} = 0.5$ minute. The server is idle with probability $1 - \rho = 0.4$. Check with Little: $\lambda W = 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5 = L$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A restaurant has on average 12 customers inside. Each customer stays on average 20 minutes. At what rate do customers arrive?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Little's law $L = \lambda W$, so with $W = 20$ minutes:

$$\lambda = \frac{L}{W} = \frac{12}{20} = 0.6 \text{ per minute} = 36 \text{ per hour}$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A clinic has one doctor. Patients arrive at rate 4 per hour and the doctor sees 5 per hour. Find $P(N \ge 3)$, $L_q$, and the mean waiting time before the visit starts.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\rho = 0.8$. Then:

$$P(N \ge 3) = \rho^3 = 0.512, \qquad L_q = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho} = \frac{0.64}{0.2} = 3.2$$

The mean wait before the visit is $W_q = \frac{\rho}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{0.8}{1} = 0.8$ hour, which is 48 minutes. Check with Little: $\lambda W_q = 4 \times 0.8 = 3.2 = L_q$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Requests arrive at rate 8 per second. The mean time in the system must be at most $0.1$ seconds. What service rate $\mu$ is needed? What is the load then?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need $W = \frac{1}{\mu - 8} \le 0.1$, so $\mu - 8 \ge 10$ and $\mu \ge 18$ per second. At $\mu = 18$ the load is $\rho = \frac{8}{18} = 0.444$, and the mean number in the system is $L = \frac{0.444}{0.556} = 0.8$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For the M/M/1 queue show by direct summation that the mean number waiting (not in service) is $L_q = \sum_{n \ge 1} (n - 1) \pi_n = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}$, and check that $L_q = \lambda W_q$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Substitute $k = n - 1$ and use $\sum_{k \ge 0} k \rho^k = \frac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2}$:

$$L_q = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (n - 1)(1 - \rho) \rho^n = (1 - \rho) \rho \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k \rho^k = (1 - \rho) \rho \cdot \frac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2} = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}$$

Now $W_q = \frac{\rho}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{\rho}{\mu (1 - \rho)}$, so

$$\lambda W_q = \lambda \cdot \frac{\rho}{\mu (1 - \rho)} = \frac{\rho \cdot \rho}{1 - \rho} = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho} = L_q$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the clinic of Problem 3 (rate 4 and 5 per hour) and estimate the mean time in the system, the mean wait, and the fraction of patients who do not wait.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The exact values are $W = 1$ hour, $W_q = 0.8$ hour, and a patient does not wait with probability $1 - \rho = 0.2$ (arrivals see an idle server with probability $\pi_0$).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
lam, mu, n = 4.0, 5.0, 2_000_000
gap = rng.exponential(1 / lam, n)
service = rng.exponential(1 / mu, n)
wait = np.zeros(n)
for i in range(1, n):
    wait[i] = max(0.0, wait[i - 1] + service[i - 1] - gap[i])
print(f"W:  simulation {(wait + service).mean():.4f}, exact {1 / (mu - lam):.4f}")
print(f"Wq: simulation {wait.mean():.4f}, exact {(lam / mu) / (mu - lam):.4f}")
print(f"fraction of customers who do not wait: {np.mean(wait == 0):.4f}, exact {1 - lam / mu:.4f}")
```

```text

Wq: simulation 0.7946, exact 0.8000
fraction of customers who do not wait: 0.2003, exact 0.2000
```

</details>

## Summary

- The M/M/1 queue has Poisson arrivals (rate $\lambda$), one server with $\text{Exp}(\mu)$ service times, and the number in the system is a birth-death chain.
- With $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} \lt 1$, the stationary law is geometric: $\pi_n = (1 - \rho) \rho^n$, and $P(N \ge k) = \rho^k$. With $\rho \ge 1$ the queue is unstable.
- The mean number is $L = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ and the mean time in the system is $W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$. Both explode as $\rho \to 1$.
- Little's law $L = \lambda W$ holds for every stable queue and links the line length to the waiting time.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{L = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}, \qquad W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}, \qquad L = \lambda W}
$$
