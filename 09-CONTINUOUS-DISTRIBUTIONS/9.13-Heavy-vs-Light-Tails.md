# 9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [9.12 Dirichlet](9.12-Dirichlet.md)

> **Big idea:** How fast the tail probability $P(X \gt x)$ shrinks decides almost everything about extreme events: light tails shrink exponentially so extremes are negligible, while heavy tails shrink like a power so one huge value can dominate everything.

---

## Why It Matters

Two distributions can have the same mean, the same median and nearly the same
shape near the center, and still behave completely differently in the
**tail**: the region of rare, extreme values. Tails decide whether averages
are stable, whether the Central Limit Theorem works, how big the largest
value in your data will be, and how much risk you are really taking. Network
traffic, file sizes, wealth, insurance losses, earthquakes and city sizes are
all heavy-tailed. Treating them as normal is one of the most expensive
mistakes in engineering and finance.

## Intuition

Think about two questions.

**Heights.** The average adult man is about 170 cm tall. Could someone be ten
times the average, 17 meters tall? Never. Could the tallest person in a room of
1000 people be twice the average? No. Heights have **light tails**: values far
from the center are not just rare, they are impossible in practice.

**Wealth.** The average wealth in a country might be a few thousand dollars.
Is someone worth ten thousand times the average? Yes, every country has
billionaires. In a room of 1000 people with one billionaire, that one person
holds almost all the wealth in the room. Wealth has a **heavy tail**: huge
values are rare, but not rare enough to ignore.

The difference is the **speed** at which $P(X \gt x)$ goes to 0. For a normal it
falls like $e^{-x^2/2}$: from 3 to 6 standard deviations the chance drops by a
factor of about a million. For a power law like $\frac{1}{x^2}$, doubling $x$
only divides the chance by 4. Big values keep showing up.

flat: at $x = 10$ they are millions of times larger than the normal's.

## Definition

The **tail** (or **survival function**) of a random variable is
$\bar{F}(x) = P(X \gt x)$. We compare tails by how fast they go to 0 as $x \to \infty$.

A distribution has a **light tail** if the tail is at most exponential: there
are numbers $C \gt 0$ and $t \gt 0$ with

$$
P(X \gt x) \le C e^{-tx} \quad \text{for all } x
$$

A distribution has a **heavy tail** if its tail is slower than **every**
exponential:

$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} e^{tx} P(X \gt x) = \infty \quad \text{for every } t \gt 0
$$

**In words:** light means "shrinks at least as fast as some exponential";
heavy means "shrinks more slowly than any exponential".

An equivalent test uses the moment generating function: the tail is light
exactly when $E[e^{tX}]$ is finite for some $t \gt 0$
([15.1 MGF](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)).

A **power-law tail** (also called a **fat tail**) is a heavy tail of the form
$P(X \gt x) \approx C x^{-\alpha}$ for large $x$. The number $\alpha \gt 0$ is the **tail
index**: the smaller $\alpha$, the heavier the tail.

### The Pareto Distribution

The simplest power law is the **Pareto distribution** with **minimum** $x_m \gt 0$
and **tail index** $\alpha \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)$:

$$
P(X \gt x) = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha}, \qquad f(x) = \frac{\alpha \thinspace x_m^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha + 1}}, \qquad x \ge x_m
$$

$$
E[X] = \frac{\alpha \thinspace x_m}{\alpha - 1} \ (\alpha \gt 1), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha \thinspace x_m^2}{(\alpha - 1)^2 (\alpha - 2)} \ (\alpha \gt 2)
$$

**In words:** each time $x$ is multiplied by 10, the chance of exceeding it is
divided by $10^{\alpha}$. For $\alpha \le 1$ the mean is infinite, and for $\alpha \le 2$ the
variance is infinite.

### Where Each Distribution Sits

| Distribution | Tail $P(X \gt x)$ behaves like | Type |
|---|---|---|
| [Uniform](9.1-Uniform.md), [Beta](9.5-Beta.md) | exactly 0 beyond the support | bounded |
| [Normal](9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md) | $e^{-x^2/2}$ (times a slower factor) | very light |
| [Exponential](9.3-Exponential.md), [Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md), [Laplace](9.7-Laplace.md), [Logistic, Gumbel](9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md) | $e^{-cx}$ (times a power for Gamma) | light |
| [Weibull](9.11-Weibull.md) with $k \lt 1$ | $e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$ | heavy, all moments finite |
| [Log-normal](9.8-Log-Normal.md) | about $e^{-(\ln x)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ | heavy, all moments finite |
| [Student t](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md) with $\nu$ df | $x^{-\nu}$ | power law, index $\nu$ |
| Pareto | $x^{-\alpha}$ | power law, index $\alpha$ |
| [Cauchy](9.9-Cauchy.md) | $\frac{1}{\pi x}$ | power law, index 1 |

## Why It Is True

### Pareto Moments

For $x \ge x_m$ the CDF is $1 - (x_m/x)^{\alpha}$; its derivative is the PDF above.
For any power $p \gt 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^p] &= \int_{x_m}^{\infty} x^p \cdot \frac{\alpha x_m^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha + 1}} \thinspace dx = \alpha x_m^{\alpha} \int_{x_m}^{\infty} x^{p - \alpha - 1} \thinspace dx && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= \alpha x_m^{\alpha} \cdot \frac{x_m^{p - \alpha}}{\alpha - p} = \frac{\alpha \thinspace x_m^{p}}{\alpha - p} \quad \text{if } p \lt \alpha && \text{(power rule; the integral converges only if } p - \alpha - 1 \lt -1 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

If $p \ge \alpha$ the integral is infinite. So **a power law with index $\alpha$ has
finite moments only below $\alpha$**. With $p = 1$ we get the mean
$\frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1}$, and with $p = 2$:
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha x_m^2}{\alpha - 2} - \frac{\alpha^2 x_m^2}{(\alpha - 1)^2} = \frac{\alpha x_m^2}{(\alpha - 1)^2(\alpha - 2)}$.

### Why Light Tails Have Small Extremes

If $P(X \gt x) \le C e^{-tx}$, then $P(X \gt 2x) \le C e^{-2tx}$: doubling the
threshold **squares** the (small) exponential factor. Extreme values become
absurdly rare very fast. For a power law, $P(X \gt 2x) \approx 2^{-\alpha} P(X \gt x)$: doubling
only divides by a fixed number.

### The Longer It Lasts, the Longer It Will Last

Look at the **mean excess**: how much longer $X$ goes on, given it has passed $t$.

- **Exponential:** by memorylessness ([9.3](9.3-Exponential.md#memorylessness)),
  $E[X - t \mid X \gt t] = \frac{1}{\lambda}$, a constant.
- **Pareto:** for $x \ge t \ge x_m$,
  $P(X \gt x \mid X \gt t) = \frac{(x_m/x)^{\alpha}}{(x_m/t)^{\alpha}} = \left( \frac{t}{x} \right)^{\alpha}$, so given $X \gt t$,
  $X$ is $\text{Pareto}(t, \alpha)$. Its mean is $\frac{\alpha t}{\alpha - 1}$, so

$$
E[X - t \mid X \gt t] = \frac{\alpha t}{\alpha - 1} - t = \frac{t}{\alpha - 1} \qquad (\alpha \gt 1)
$$

**In words:** for a power law, the expected remaining amount **grows** in
proportion to how far you have already gone. A job that has run for 10 hours
is expected to run much longer than a fresh job. For the normal, the mean
excess shrinks toward 0.

### Maximums Grow Very Differently

For $n$ independent values, $P(\max \le x) = F(x)^n$.

- For $\text{Exp}(1)$, the maximum grows like $\ln n$ (see
  [9.10](9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md#why-maximums-become-gumbel)).
- For $\text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)$, put $x = c \thinspace n^{1/\alpha}$:

$$
P\left( \max \le c \thinspace n^{1/\alpha} \right) = \left( 1 - \frac{x_m^{\alpha}}{c^{\alpha} n} \right)^n \to \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x_m}{c} \right)^{\alpha} \right)
$$

So the maximum grows like $n^{1/\alpha}$, a power of $n$, and the limit shape is the
**Frechet distribution** instead of the Gumbel. With $\alpha = 1.5$, a sample 1000
times bigger has a maximum about $1000^{1/1.5} = 100$ times bigger.

### Key Properties

- **One big jump.** For heavy tails, a large sum $X_1 + \dots + X_n$ usually comes
  from **one** huge term, not from many moderately large ones. For light tails
  it is the other way round.
- **Unstable averages.** With $\alpha \le 2$ (infinite variance) the sample mean
  converges very slowly and the classical CLT fails; with $\alpha \le 1$ there is no
  mean at all ([17.9 When the CLT Fails](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).
- **Bounds need light tails.** Chernoff and Hoeffding bounds
  ([16.6](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.6-Chernoff-Bounds.md)) use $E[e^{tX}]$, which is
  infinite for heavy tails. Only weaker bounds such as Chebyshev's remain.
- **Heavy is not the same as power law.** The log-normal and the Weibull with
  $k \lt 1$ are heavy (no finite $E[e^{tX}]$) but still have all moments finite.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: How Long Will This Job Run

**Problem.** Job durations on a computing cluster are $\text{Pareto}(1, 2.5)$ in hours.
(a) Find the mean and variance. (b) What fraction of jobs run more than 10
hours? (c) A job has already run 10 hours. How much longer do you expect it to
run? (d) Compare with exponential durations that have the same mean.

**Solution.**

1. (a) Mean and variance with $x_m = 1$, $\alpha = 2.5$:

$$
E[X] = \frac{2.5 \times 1}{1.5} \approx 1.667 \text{ hours}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{2.5}{1.5^2 \times 0.5} = \frac{2.5}{1.125} \approx 2.222
$$

2. (b) The tail:

$$
P(X \gt 10) = \left( \frac{1}{10} \right)^{2.5} = 10^{-2.5} \approx 0.00316
$$

3. (c) The mean excess with $t = 10$:

$$
E[X - 10 \mid X \gt 10] = \frac{10}{2.5 - 1} = \frac{10}{1.5} \approx 6.67 \text{ hours}
$$

4. (d) An exponential with mean $1.667$ has rate $0.6$. Then
   $P(X \gt 10) = e^{-6} \approx 0.0025$ (similar!), but by memorylessness the expected
   remaining time is always $1.667$ hours.

**Answer.** (a) Mean about 1.67 hours, variance about 2.22. (b) About 0.3% of
jobs run over 10 hours. (c) About 6.67 more hours. (d) An exponential model
would predict only 1.67 more hours.

**Check.** This is why schedulers that favor short jobs work well on real
clusters: a job that has already run long is likely to run much longer.

### Example 2: The 80/20 Rule

**Problem.** Wealth is $\text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)$ with $\alpha \gt 1$. Show that the richest
fraction $q$ of people own the share $q^{1 - 1/\alpha}$ of all wealth. Which $\alpha$ gives
"the top 20% own 80%"?

**Solution.**

1. The richest fraction $q$ are those above the level $t$ with
   $(x_m/t)^{\alpha} = q$, that is $t = x_m q^{-1/\alpha}$.
2. Their total wealth per person in the population is:

$$
E[X \cdot I_{X \gt t}] = \int_t^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{\alpha x_m^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha + 1}} \thinspace dx = \frac{\alpha x_m^{\alpha} \thinspace t^{1 - \alpha}}{\alpha - 1}
$$

3. Divide by the mean $\frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1}$:

$$
\text{share} = \left( \frac{t}{x_m} \right)^{1 - \alpha} = \left( q^{-1/\alpha} \right)^{1 - \alpha} = q^{(\alpha - 1)/\alpha} = q^{1 - 1/\alpha}
$$

4. For the 80/20 rule, solve $0.2^{1 - 1/\alpha} = 0.8$. Take logs:

$$
1 - \frac{1}{\alpha} = \frac{\ln 0.8}{\ln 0.2} = \frac{-0.22314}{-1.60944} \approx 0.13865 \implies \alpha = \frac{1}{1 - 0.13865} \approx 1.161
$$

**Answer.** The top fraction $q$ own $q^{1 - 1/\alpha}$, and $\alpha \approx 1.16$ gives the 80/20
rule.

**Check.** With $\alpha = 1.5$ the top 20% would own the share $0.2^{1/3} \approx 0.58$ (58%). A heavier tail
(smaller $\alpha$) concentrates more wealth at the top, as expected.

## Simulate It

This code shows the "one big jump" effect and checks Example 1. numpy's
`rng.pareto(a)` returns a **shifted** Pareto that starts at 0, so add 1 and
multiply by $x_m$ to get $\text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)$ as defined here.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Share of the largest value in a sum of 1000 values, averaged over 2000 repeats.
n, reps = 1000, 2000
light = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(reps, n))       # Exp(1): scale = 1/rate
heavy = 1 + rng.pareto(1.5, size=(reps, n))              # Pareto(1, 1.5)
for name, x in [("Exp(1)", light), ("Pareto(1, 1.5)", heavy)]:
    share = (x.max(axis=1) / x.sum(axis=1)).mean()
    print(f"{name:15s} largest value's share of the sum: {share:.4f}")

# Example 1: job durations Pareto(1, 2.5).
jobs = 1 + rng.pareto(2.5, size=10_000_000)
print(f"P(X > 10):             simulation {np.mean(jobs > 10):.5f}, exact {10**-2.5:.5f}")
long = jobs[jobs > 10]
print(f"E[X - 10 | X > 10]:    simulation {np.mean(long - 10):.3f}, exact {10 / 1.5:.3f}")
print(f"E[X]:                  simulation {jobs.mean():.4f}, exact {2.5 / 1.5:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Exp(1)          largest value's share of the sum: 0.0075
Pareto(1, 1.5)  largest value's share of the sum: 0.0684
P(X > 10):             simulation 0.00315, exact 0.00316
E[X - 10 | X > 10]:    simulation 6.708, exact 6.667
E[X]:                  simulation 1.6666, exact 1.6667
```

Out of 1000 exponential values, the largest one is under 1% of the sum. Out of
1000 Pareto values, the largest one is about 7% of the sum on average, nine
times more, and in some samples far more than that.

## Common Mistakes

- **Judging the tail from the middle of a histogram.** Tails are invisible on a
  normal plot. Plot $P(X \gt x)$ on a log scale, or a log-log scale for power laws.
- **Trusting the sample variance of heavy-tailed data.** If $\alpha \le 2$, the true
  variance is infinite, and the sample variance just keeps growing as you add data.
- **Using "3 sigma" rules everywhere.** "Beyond 3 standard deviations is
  0.3%" is a normal fact. For a $t_3$ or a Pareto it can be much larger.
- **Calling every skewed distribution "heavy".** The Gamma is skewed but light;
  the Laplace has heavier tails than the normal but is still light.
- **Assuming a past maximum is a safe limit.** For heavy tails, the next record
  can be far bigger than all previous values combined.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** file sizes, web page sizes, job run times, and network
  traffic bursts are heavy-tailed; this shapes caching, load balancing and
  scheduling ([21.3.4 Networks and Queues](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** word frequencies follow Zipf's law (a power
  law); gradient noise in deep learning is often heavy-tailed; robust losses
  (Huber, Student t) are used when data has outliers.
- **Finance and insurance:** stock market crashes and catastrophic insurance
  claims; risk models built on the normal underestimate them
  ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Nature and society:** earthquake energies, city sizes, wealth and the number
  of followers on social networks.
- **Everyday life:** knowing when one rare event can matter more than all the
  normal days together ([22.13 Fat Tails](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.13-Fat-Tails.md)).

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Pareto}(2, 3)$. Find $P(X \gt 4)$ and $E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \gt 4) = \left( \frac{2}{4} \right)^3 = 0.125, \qquad E[X] = \frac{3 \times 2}{3 - 1} = 3$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Sort these from lightest to heaviest tail: $\text{Exp}(1)$, $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$,
$\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$, $t_5$, $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ (bounded), then $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (like $e^{-x^2/2}$), then $\text{Exp}(1)$ (like $e^{-x}$), then $t_5$ (power law with index 5), then $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$ (power law with index 1).

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For $X \sim \text{Pareto}(1, \alpha)$, for which $\alpha$ is $E[X^3]$ finite? For
$t_4$, which moments are finite?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From the moment formula, $E[X^p]$ is finite only for $p \lt \alpha$, so $E[X^3]$ needs $\alpha \gt 3$. The $t_4$ tail is a power law with index 4, so $E[\lvert T \rvert^p]$ is finite only for $p \lt 4$: the mean, variance and third moment exist, but the fourth moment (kurtosis) does not.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Wealth is Pareto with tail index $\alpha$. Which $\alpha$ makes the richest
1% own half of all wealth?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the share formula from Example 2 with $q = 0.01$:

$$0.01^{1 - 1/\alpha} = 0.5 \implies 1 - \frac{1}{\alpha} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln 0.01} \approx 0.15051 \implies \alpha \approx 1.177$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that the log-normal is heavy-tailed: $E[e^{tX}] = \infty$ for every
$t \gt 0$ when $X \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma \gt 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $x \gt 0$ and any whole number $n$, $e^{tx} \ge \frac{(tx)^n}{n!}$ (one term of the power series). With $E[X^n] = e^{n\mu + n^2\sigma^2/2}$ from [9.8](9.8-Log-Normal.md):

$$E[e^{tX}] \ge \frac{t^n E[X^n]}{n!} = \frac{t^n e^{n\mu + n^2\sigma^2/2}}{n!} \ge \frac{e^{n \ln t + n\mu + n^2\sigma^2/2}}{e^{n \ln n}}$$

using $n! \le n^n$. The exponent $n^2\sigma^2/2$ grows faster than $n \ln n$, so the bound goes to infinity as $n \to \infty$. Hence $E[e^{tX}] = \infty$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Show that sample means of $\text{Pareto}(1, 1.5)$ (finite mean 3, infinite
variance) are much less stable than those of an exponential with the same mean.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
reps, n = 2000, 1000
pareto_means = (1 + rng.pareto(1.5, size=(reps, n))).mean(axis=1)    # true mean 3
exp_means = rng.exponential(scale=3.0, size=(reps, n)).mean(axis=1)  # mean 3 (rate 1/3)
for name, m in [("Pareto(1, 1.5)", pareto_means), ("Exp(1/3)", exp_means)]:
    print(f"{name:15s} 1% / 50% / 99% of sample means: "
          f"{np.quantile(m, 0.01):.3f} / {np.median(m):.3f} / {np.quantile(m, 0.99):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
Pareto(1, 1.5)  1% / 50% / 99% of sample means: 2.465 / 2.869 / 5.662
Exp(1/3)        1% / 50% / 99% of sample means: 2.798 / 2.996 / 3.210
```

The exponential sample means stay in a narrow band around 3. The Pareto ones are usually a bit below 3 and occasionally far above it, because of single huge values.

</details>

## Summary

- The tail $P(X \gt x)$ decides how extreme values behave.
- Light tails shrink at least exponentially (normal, exponential, Gamma, Laplace);
  heavy tails shrink more slowly than any exponential (log-normal, Pareto, t, Cauchy).
- A power law $P(X \gt x) \approx C x^{-\alpha}$ has finite moments only of order below $\alpha$.
- For heavy tails, one big value dominates sums, maximums grow like a power of
  $n$, and the expected remaining time grows the longer you wait.
- In numpy: `x_m * (1 + rng.pareto(alpha, size=n))` gives $\text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X \gt x) = \left( \frac{x_m}{x} \right)^{\alpha} \implies E[X^p] \lt \infty \text{ only for } p \lt \alpha}
$$
