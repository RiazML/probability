# 9.11 Weibull

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.11 Weibull

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [9.10 Logistic and Gumbel](9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md)

> **Big idea:** The Weibull distribution models lifetimes whose risk of failure can fall, stay flat or rise with age, controlled by one shape number, which makes it the standard tool for reliability and survival.

---

## Why It Matters

The exponential distribution assumes things never age: a 10-year-old machine
is as good as new. That is wrong for most real things. New electronics often
fail early from factory defects, then settle down; car parts, bones and
batteries wear out and fail more as they get older. The **Weibull
distribution** handles all of these with a single shape parameter. Engineers
use it for hard drives, bearings and wind turbines, doctors for survival
times, and meteorologists for wind speeds.

## Intuition

Think about the **hazard**: the chance per unit time that a working item fails
right now, given that it has survived so far.

- A new phone has a small chance of a hidden factory defect. If it survives the
  first month, the defect probably is not there, so its hazard **drops** with
  age. This is called **infant mortality**.
- A radioactive atom never ages: its hazard is **constant**. That is the
  exponential distribution.
- A car tire wears down: the older it is, the more likely it is to fail next
  week. Its hazard **rises** with age. This is **wear-out**.

The Weibull uses a power of time for the hazard: $h(x) \propto x^{k-1}$. A shape
$k \lt 1$ gives a falling hazard, $k = 1$ a flat one, and $k \gt 1$ a rising one.
Put the three phases together over a product's life and you get the famous
**bathtub curve**.

## Picture

```text
  hazard h(x)
    3 + a                                      cccc
      | a                                  cccc
      | a                              cccc         a: k = 0.5
      |  a                          ccc                (infant mortality)
    2 +  a                      cccc                b: k = 1
      |   a                 cccc                       (exponential)
      |    a            cccc                        c: k = 2
    1 +bbbbbaaabbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbbb  (wear-out)
      |        aaaaaaa
      |      cccc     aaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaa
      |  cccc                                    aaaaaaaaaaaaaa
    0 +cc
      +-------------------------------------------------------- x
       0             0.5          1             1.5           2
```

With scale $\lambda = 1$, the Weibull hazard falls for $k \lt 1$, stays flat for
$k = 1$, and grows for $k \gt 1$.

## Definition

A random variable $X$ has the **Weibull distribution** with **shape** $k \gt 0$
and **scale** $\lambda \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$, if its CDF is:

$$
F(x) = 1 - \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right), \qquad x \ge 0
$$

and so its survival function and PDF are:

$$
P(X \gt x) = \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right), \qquad f(x) = \frac{k}{\lambda} \left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^{k-1} \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right)
$$

where:

- $k$ is the **shape**: it decides whether the hazard falls, stays flat or rises;
- $\lambda$ is the **scale**: a typical lifetime, in the same units as $x$.
  Careful: here $\lambda$ is a **scale**, not a rate as in $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$;
- the support is $[0, \infty)$.

**In words:** the chance of surviving past $x$ decays like $e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$: an
exponential decay in the stretched time $(x/\lambda)^k$.

The **hazard rate** $h(x) = \frac{f(x)}{P(X \gt x)}$ is a simple power:

$$
h(x) = \frac{k}{\lambda} \left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^{k-1}
$$

The mean and variance use the [Gamma function](9.4-Gamma.md#the-gamma-function):

$$
E[X] = \lambda \thinspace \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{1}{k} \right), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda^2 \left[ \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{2}{k} \right) - \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{1}{k} \right)^2 \right]
$$

**In words:** the mean is the scale times a number that depends only on the
shape; for $k = 1$ that number is $\Gamma(2) = 1$.

## Why It Is True

**The hazard.** Divide the PDF by the survival function; the exponential
factors cancel and leave $\frac{k}{\lambda}(\frac{x}{\lambda})^{k-1}$. For $k = 1$ this is
$\frac{1}{\lambda}$, a constant, and the CDF becomes $1 - e^{-x/\lambda}$: the exponential
with **rate** $\frac{1}{\lambda}$.

**A Weibull is a stretched exponential.** Let $E \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and
$X = \lambda E^{1/k}$. For $x \ge 0$:

$$
P(X \gt x) = P\left( E^{1/k} \gt \frac{x}{\lambda} \right) = P\left( E \gt \left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right) = \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right)
$$

So every Weibull is $\lambda E^{1/k}$. This gives the moments in one line each.

**The moments.** For any $r \gt 0$, use LOTUS for $E$ and the Gamma function:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^r] &= \lambda^r E\left[ E^{r/k} \right] = \lambda^r \int_0^{\infty} u^{r/k} e^{-u} \thinspace du && \text{(}X = \lambda E^{1/k}\text{, LOTUS)} \\
&= \lambda^r \thinspace \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{r}{k} \right) && \text{(definition of } \Gamma \text{ with } \alpha = 1 + r/k \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $r = 1$ we get the mean. With $r = 2$,
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \lambda^2 \Gamma(1 + \frac{2}{k}) - \lambda^2 \Gamma(1 + \frac{1}{k})^2$.

**The quantiles.** Solving $F(x) = p$ gives
$x_p = \lambda \left( -\ln(1 - p) \right)^{1/k}$. In particular, the median is
$\lambda (\ln 2)^{1/k}$, and $F(\lambda) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$ for **every** shape: 63.2%
of items have failed by time $\lambda$, which is why $\lambda$ is called the
**characteristic life**.

### The Weakest Link

**Claim.** A chain fails when its weakest link fails. If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are
independent $\text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$, then
$\min(X_1, \dots, X_n) \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda n^{-1/k})$.

**Proof.** The minimum survives past $x$ only if every $X_i$ does:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\min \gt x) &= \prod_{i=1}^{n} \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right) = \exp\left( -n \left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right) && \text{(independence)} \\
&= \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda n^{-1/k}} \right)^k \right) && \text{(}n = (n^{1/k})^k\text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a Weibull with the same shape and a smaller scale. Weibull himself
introduced the distribution in 1939 to explain the strength of materials this way.

### Key Properties

- **Special cases.** $k = 1$ is $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{\lambda})$. $k = 2$ is the **Rayleigh
  distribution**: the distance from the target in the drone example of
  [9.6](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md#example-1-how-far-does-the-drone-land)
  is $\text{Weibull}(2, \sigma\sqrt{2})$, since $P(R \gt r) = e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}$.
- **Not memoryless (unless $k = 1$).** For $k \gt 1$, an old item is more likely to
  fail soon than a new one: $P(X \gt s + t \mid X \gt s) \lt P(X \gt t)$.
- **Shape of the density.** For $k \le 1$ the density is highest at 0. For $k \gt 1$
  it has a hump. Around $k \approx 3.6$ it looks almost like a normal.
- **Scaling.** If $X \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$ then $cX \sim \text{Weibull}(k, c\lambda)$ for $c \gt 0$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Hard Drive Lifetimes

**Problem.** A hard drive's lifetime is $\text{Weibull}(1.5, 5)$ in years. (a) What
fraction survives 3 years? (b) What are the mean and median lifetimes? (c) A
drive has already worked for 3 years. What is the chance it works 3 more years?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Use the survival function with $\frac{3}{5} = 0.6$:

$$
P(X \gt 3) = \exp\left( -0.6^{1.5} \right) = \exp(-0.46476) \approx 0.6283
$$

2. (b) The mean, with $\Gamma(1 + \frac{1}{1.5}) = \Gamma(\frac{5}{3}) \approx 0.90275$:

$$
E[X] = 5 \times 0.90275 \approx 4.514 \text{ years}
$$

3. The median, with $(\ln 2)^{1/1.5} = 0.69315^{0.6667} \approx 0.78322$:

$$
\text{median} = 5 \times 0.78322 \approx 3.916 \text{ years}
$$

4. (c) Conditional survival, with $\frac{6}{5} = 1.2$ and $1.2^{1.5} \approx 1.31453$:

$$
P(X \gt 6 \mid X \gt 3) = \frac{P(X \gt 6)}{P(X \gt 3)} = \frac{e^{-1.31453}}{e^{-0.46476}} = e^{-0.84977} \approx 0.4275
$$

**Answer.** (a) About 62.8% survive 3 years. (b) The mean is about 4.51 years
and the median about 3.92 years. (c) Only about 42.8%: an old drive is less
likely than a new one (62.8%) to last 3 more years.

**Check.** The shape $k = 1.5 \gt 1$ means wear-out, so (c) should be smaller than
(a). It is. With $k = 1$ they would be equal (memorylessness).

### Example 2: The Weakest Link of a Chain

**Problem.** Each link of a chain has a breaking strength $\text{Weibull}(3, 100)$ in
kilonewtons (kN), independently. A chain has 10 links. What is the chance
that a single link holds a 30 kN load? That the whole chain holds it?

**Solution.**

1. One link: $\frac{30}{100} = 0.3$ and $0.3^3 = 0.027$:

$$
P(\text{link holds}) = P(X \gt 30) = e^{-0.027} \approx 0.9734
$$

2. The chain's strength is the minimum of 10 links:
   $\text{Weibull}(3, 100 \times 10^{-1/3}) = \text{Weibull}(3, 46.42)$. Then:

$$
P(\text{chain holds}) = \exp\left( -\left( \frac{30}{46.42} \right)^3 \right) = \exp(-10 \times 0.027) = e^{-0.27} \approx 0.7634
$$

**Answer.** A single link holds with probability about $0.973$, but the
10-link chain only with probability about $0.763$.

**Check.** Directly: the chain holds if all 10 links hold, so
$0.9734^{10} = e^{-0.27} \approx 0.763$. Same answer. Longer chains (and bigger pieces
of material) are weaker, which is the "size effect" seen in real experiments.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 1. numpy's `rng.weibull(k)` draws $\text{Weibull}(k, 1)$ (scale
1), so multiply by $\lambda$ to get scale $\lambda$.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import gamma, log

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
k, lam = 1.5, 5.0

# numpy gives scale 1; multiplying by lam gives Weibull(k, lam).
life = lam * rng.weibull(k, size=n_trials)

print(f"P(X > 3):         simulation {np.mean(life > 3):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-0.6**1.5):.4f}")
print(f"mean:             simulation {life.mean():.4f}, exact {lam * gamma(1 + 1 / k):.4f}")
print(f"median:           simulation {np.median(life):.4f}, exact {lam * log(2)**(1 / k):.4f}")
old = life[life > 3]
print(f"P(X > 6 | X > 3): simulation {np.mean(old > 6):.4f}, "
      f"exact {np.exp(-1.2**1.5 + 0.6**1.5):.4f}")

# The stretched-exponential story: lam * E^(1/k) with E ~ Exp(1).
e = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=n_trials)
print(f"mean of lam*E^(1/k): simulation {np.mean(lam * e**(1 / k)):.4f}, exact {lam * gamma(1 + 1 / k):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X > 3):         simulation 0.6279, exact 0.6283
mean:             simulation 4.5133, exact 4.5137
median:           simulation 3.9144, exact 3.9161
P(X > 6 | X > 3): simulation 0.4280, exact 0.4275
mean of lam*E^(1/k): simulation 4.5107, exact 4.5137
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating $\lambda$ as a rate.** In $\text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$, $\lambda$ is a scale: bigger $\lambda$ means
  longer lives. With $k = 1$ it is $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{\lambda})$, not $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$.
- **Forgetting to rescale numpy's output.** `rng.weibull(k)` has scale 1. Multiply
  by $\lambda$.
- **Using the exponential for things that age.** If old items fail more often
  than new ones, the exponential underestimates late failures. Fit a Weibull
  and check whether $k \gt 1$.
- **Mixing up the two parameter orders.** Some software writes the scale first,
  or uses $\frac{1}{\lambda^k}$ as a parameter. Always check which number is the shape.
- **Thinking a mean lifetime of 4.5 years means most drives last 4.5 years.**
  The median (3.9 years here) and the spread matter too.

## Where It Is Used

- **Reliability engineering:** lifetimes of bearings, hard drives, LEDs and
  batteries; warranty planning; the bathtub curve
  ([21.7 Engineering and Reliability](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)).
- **Computer science:** time to failure of servers and disks in data centers,
  which drives backup and replacement schedules.
- **Medicine:** survival analysis of patients after a treatment (the Weibull is a
  common parametric survival model).
- **Weather and energy:** wind speeds are close to Weibull with $k \approx 2$, which
  is used to estimate the energy a wind turbine will produce.
- **Materials science:** the strength of brittle materials such as glass and
  ceramics, through the weakest-link argument.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Weibull}(2, 10)$. Find $P(X \gt 5)$ and $P(X \le 10)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \gt 5) = e^{-(5/10)^2} = e^{-0.25} \approx 0.7788, \qquad P(X \le 10) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Find the mean of $\text{Weibull}(1, 4)$ and of $\text{Weibull}(2, 4)$.
(Use $\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \approx 0.8862$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[\text{Weibull}(1, 4)] = 4\Gamma(2) = 4, \qquad E[\text{Weibull}(2, 4)] = 4\Gamma(1.5) \approx 4 \times 0.8862 = 3.545$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For $X \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$, find the time by which 10% of items
have failed (the "B10 life", used in industry).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $F(x) = 0.1$:

$$1 - e^{-(x/\lambda)^k} = 0.1 \implies \left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k = -\ln 0.9 \approx 0.10536 \implies x = \lambda (0.10536)^{1/k}$$

For the hard drive of Example 1, $x = 5 \times 0.10536^{2/3} \approx 5 \times 0.2231 \approx 1.115$ years.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that for $k \gt 1$ the Weibull is "new better than used":
$P(X \gt s + t \mid X \gt s) \lt P(X \gt t)$ for $s, t \gt 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In units of $\lambda$, the claim is $e^{-(s+t)^k + s^k} \lt e^{-t^k}$, that is $(s + t)^k \gt s^k + t^k$. For $k \gt 1$ write $(s + t)^k = (s + t)^{k-1}(s + t) = (s + t)^{k-1} s + (s + t)^{k-1} t$:

$$(s + t)^k \gt s^{k-1} s + t^{k-1} t = s^k + t^k$$

because $(s + t)^{k-1} \gt s^{k-1}$ and $(s + t)^{k-1} \gt t^{k-1}$ when $k - 1 \gt 0$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that if $X \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$, then $(X/\lambda)^k \sim \text{Exp}(1)$. Use
this to explain how to sample a Weibull from a uniform random number.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \ge 0$, $P((X/\lambda)^k \gt y) = P(X \gt \lambda y^{1/k}) = e^{-y}$, the $\text{Exp}(1)$ survival function. To sample, take $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, set $E = -\ln U \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ ([9.3](9.3-Exponential.md)), then:

$$X = \lambda E^{1/k} = \lambda (-\ln U)^{1/k}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2: simulate 10-link chains and estimate the chance
the chain holds 30 kN.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
links = 100 * rng.weibull(3, size=(1_000_000, 10))   # scale 100 kN
chain = links.min(axis=1)                             # weakest link
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(chain > 30):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {np.exp(-0.27):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.7635
Exact:      0.7634
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$ has survival $e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$; $k$ is the shape and $\lambda$ is a **scale**.
- Its hazard $\frac{k}{\lambda}(\frac{x}{\lambda})^{k-1}$ falls ($k \lt 1$), stays flat ($k = 1$, the
  exponential) or rises ($k \gt 1$).
- $X = \lambda E^{1/k}$ with $E \sim \text{Exp}(1)$, so $E[X^r] = \lambda^r\Gamma(1 + \frac{r}{k})$.
- The minimum of $n$ iid Weibulls is Weibull with scale $\lambda n^{-1/k}$ (weakest link).
- In numpy: `lam * rng.weibull(k, size=n)`. numpy's Weibull has scale 1.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X \gt x) = \exp\left( -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda} \right)^k \right)}
$$
