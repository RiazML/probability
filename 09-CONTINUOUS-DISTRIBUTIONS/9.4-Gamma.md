# 9.4 Gamma

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.4 Gamma

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [9.3 Exponential](9.3-Exponential.md)

> **Big idea:** The Gamma distribution is the waiting time until the $\alpha$-th event when events happen at rate $\beta$, so it is a sum of exponential waits and a flexible model for any positive, right-skewed quantity.

---

## Why It Matters

The exponential tells you how long until the **next** event. Often you need
more: how long until the 3rd customer, the 10th server failure, or the 5th
goal? The answer is a Gamma distribution. It is also a general-purpose model
for positive skewed data (rainfall, insurance claims, service times), it
contains the exponential and the [chi-square](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)
as special cases, and it is a standard prior for rates in Bayesian machine
learning. Along the way you meet the **Gamma function**, which shows up all
over probability.

## Intuition

A call center gets calls at rate 2 per minute. The wait for the first call is
$\text{Exp}(2)$, with mean half a minute. After the first call, the wait for
the second call starts fresh (memorylessness), and it is again $\text{Exp}(2)$.
So the time until the 3rd call is the sum of three independent exponential
waits, with mean $0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.5$ minutes.

What does this sum look like? One exponential wait is most likely to be
short: its density is highest at 0. But a sum of three waits is very unlikely
to be near 0, because all three waits would have to be tiny. So the density
of the sum starts at 0, rises to a hump, then has a long right tail. Add more
waits and the hump moves right and becomes more symmetric, like a bell (that
is the [Central Limit Theorem](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)
at work).

## Picture

      |    a                                starts at height 1
      |    a                             b: α = 2
  0.4 +     a                            c: α = 5      (all with β = 1)
      |   bbabb

With shape $\alpha = 1$ the Gamma is the exponential; as the shape grows, the
peak moves right and the curve looks more and more like a bell.

## Definition

### The Gamma Function

The **Gamma function** extends the factorial to non-integer numbers. For
$\alpha \gt 0$:

$$
\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x} \thinspace dx
$$

It has three facts you will use again and again:

$$
\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha \thinspace \Gamma(\alpha), \qquad \Gamma(n) = (n-1)! \text{ for } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, \qquad \Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \sqrt{\pi}
$$

**In words:** the Gamma function is a smooth version of the factorial, shifted
by one: $\Gamma(5) = 4! = 24$.

### The Gamma Distribution

A random variable $X$ has the **Gamma distribution** with **shape** $\alpha \gt 0$
and **rate** $\beta \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$, if its PDF is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x}, \qquad x \gt 0
$$

where:

- $\alpha$ is the **shape**: for a whole number $\alpha$, the number of events you wait for;
- $\beta$ is the **rate**: events per unit time, so the mean of each single wait is $\frac{1}{\beta}$;
- the support is $(0, \infty)$, and $f(x) = 0$ for $x \le 0$.

**In words:** a power of $x$ that pushes the mass away from 0, times an
exponential decay that pulls it back, scaled so the total area is 1.

The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}
$$

**In words:** the mean is "number of events times mean wait per event", and
the variance is "number of events times variance per event".

For a whole-number shape $\alpha = n$ (also called the **Erlang distribution**),
the CDF has a closed form:

$$
P(X \le x) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{-\beta x} \frac{(\beta x)^k}{k!}
$$

**In words:** the $n$-th event has happened by time $x$ unless fewer than $n$
events happened in $[0, x]$, and that count is $\text{Pois}(\beta x)$. For a
non-integer shape there is no closed form; use `scipy.stats.gamma.cdf`.

## Why It Is True

### Facts About the Gamma Function

**$\Gamma(1) = 1$:** $\int_0^{\infty} e^{-x} \thinspace dx = 1$.

**$\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha \Gamma(\alpha)$:** integrate by parts with
$u = x^{\alpha}$ and $dv = e^{-x} dx$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma(\alpha + 1) &= \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha} e^{-x} \thinspace dx && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \Big[ -x^{\alpha} e^{-x} \Big]_0^{\infty} + \alpha \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x} \thinspace dx && \text{(integration by parts)} \\
&= 0 + \alpha \thinspace \Gamma(\alpha) && \text{(boundary terms vanish)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)(n-2) \cdots 1 \cdot \Gamma(1) = (n-1)!$.

**$\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}) = \sqrt{\pi}$:** substitute $x = z^2/2$, so $dx = z \thinspace dz$
and $x^{-1/2} = \sqrt{2}/z$. Then use the Gaussian integral from
[9.2 Normal (Gaussian)](9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md#why-it-is-true), whose half is
$\int_0^{\infty} e^{-z^2/2} dz = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}$:

$$
\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \int_0^{\infty} x^{-1/2} e^{-x} \thinspace dx = \int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{2} \thinspace e^{-z^2/2} \thinspace dz = \sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2} = \sqrt{\pi}
$$

### The PDF Is Valid, and the Key Integral

Substitute $u = \beta x$ in the Gamma function to get the **key integral** of
this topic. For any $a \gt 0$:

$$
\int_0^{\infty} x^{a - 1} e^{-\beta x} \thinspace dx = \frac{\Gamma(a)}{\beta^{a}}
$$

With $a = \alpha$ this shows the PDF integrates to
$\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\beta^{\alpha}} = 1$.

### Mean and Variance

Each moment is the key integral with a bigger power:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha} e^{-\beta x} \thinspace dx && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + 1)}{\beta^{\alpha + 1}} && \text{(key integral with } a = \alpha + 1 \text{)} \\
&= \frac{\alpha \thinspace \Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha) \thinspace \beta} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} && \text{(}\Gamma(\alpha+1) = \alpha\Gamma(\alpha)\text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^2] &= \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + 2)}{\beta^{\alpha + 2}} && \text{(key integral with } a = \alpha + 2 \text{)} \\
&= \frac{(\alpha + 1) \alpha \thinspace \Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha) \thinspace \beta^2} = \frac{\alpha(\alpha + 1)}{\beta^2} && \text{(use the recursion twice)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \frac{\alpha^2 + \alpha}{\beta^2} - \frac{\alpha^2}{\beta^2} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}
$$

### Sum of Exponentials Is Gamma

**Claim.** If $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, then
$S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n \sim \text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$.

**Proof by induction.** For $n = 1$: $\text{Gamma}(1, \lambda)$ has PDF
$\frac{\lambda}{\Gamma(1)} x^0 e^{-\lambda x} = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, the exponential.
Now assume $S_n \sim \text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$. The density of a sum of two
independent variables is the **convolution**
$f_{S+X}(s) = \int f_S(x) f_X(s - x) \thinspace dx$
([13.4 Convolution](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md)).
Both densities are zero for negative inputs, so $x$ runs from $0$ to $s$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{S_{n+1}}(s) &= \int_0^{s} \frac{\lambda^n x^{n-1} e^{-\lambda x}}{(n-1)!} \cdot \lambda e^{-\lambda (s - x)} \thinspace dx && \text{(convolution)} \\
&= \frac{\lambda^{n+1} e^{-\lambda s}}{(n-1)!} \int_0^{s} x^{n-1} \thinspace dx && \text{(}e^{-\lambda x} e^{\lambda x} = 1\text{)} \\
&= \frac{\lambda^{n+1} e^{-\lambda s}}{(n-1)!} \cdot \frac{s^n}{n} = \frac{\lambda^{n+1}}{n!} s^{n} e^{-\lambda s} && \text{(integrate)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the $\text{Gamma}(n + 1, \lambda)$ density, since $\Gamma(n + 1) = n!$.

### Key Properties

- **Special cases.** $\text{Gamma}(1, \lambda) = \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, and the
  chi-square with $k$ degrees of freedom is $\text{Gamma}(\frac{k}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$
  ([9.6](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)).
- **Adding Gammas.** If $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_1, \beta)$ and
  $Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_2, \beta)$ are independent with the **same rate**,
  then $X + Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta)$. The shapes add. (A
  short proof with moment generating functions is in
  [15.3 MGF of Sums](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md).)
- **Scaling.** If $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $c \gt 0$, then
  $cX \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta / c)$.
- **Shape.** For $\alpha \lt 1$ the density shoots up to infinity at 0. For
  $\alpha = 1$ it is the exponential. For $\alpha \gt 1$ it is a hump with its
  peak (mode) at $\frac{\alpha - 1}{\beta}$, and for large $\alpha$ it is close to
  $\mathcal{N}(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2})$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Waiting for the Third Call

**Problem.** Calls arrive at a call center at rate 2 per minute. Let $T$ be the
time until the 3rd call. Find $E[T]$, the standard deviation of $T$, and
$P(T \le 1)$.

**Solution.**

1. $T$ is a sum of 3 independent $\text{Exp}(2)$ waits, so $T \sim \text{Gamma}(3, 2)$.
2. Mean and variance:

$$
E[T] = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5 \text{ minutes}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(T) = \frac{3}{2^2} = 0.75, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.866
$$

3. $T \le 1$ means at least 3 calls arrive in the first minute. The count in one
   minute is $\text{Pois}(2 \times 1) = \text{Pois}(2)$:

$$
P(T \le 1) = 1 - \left( e^{-2} \frac{2^0}{0!} + e^{-2} \frac{2^1}{1!} + e^{-2} \frac{2^2}{2!} \right) = 1 - e^{-2}(1 + 2 + 2) = 1 - 5e^{-2}
$$

4. With $e^{-2} \approx 0.13534$:

$$
P(T \le 1) \approx 1 - 5 \times 0.13534 = 1 - 0.67668 = 0.3233
$$

**Answer.** The 3rd call comes after 1.5 minutes on average, give or take
about 0.87 minutes, and it comes within the first minute about 32% of the time.

**Check.** The mean is three times the mean of one wait, as it should be. The
simulation below agrees with $0.3233$.

### Example 2: A Machine with Spare Parts

**Problem.** A sensor fails after an $\text{Exp}(0.5)$ time in years (mean 2
years). When it fails, it is replaced at once by a spare. You have the
sensor plus 4 spares, all independent. How long does the system last on
average? What is the chance it lasts more than 12 years?

**Solution.**

1. The total life is a sum of 5 independent $\text{Exp}(0.5)$ times, so
   $L \sim \text{Gamma}(5, 0.5)$.
2. Mean and standard deviation:

$$
E[L] = \frac{5}{0.5} = 10 \text{ years}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{5}{0.5^2}} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47 \text{ years}
$$

3. $L \gt 12$ means fewer than 5 failures in 12 years. The failure count in 12
   years is $\text{Pois}(0.5 \times 12) = \text{Pois}(6)$:

$$
P(L \gt 12) = e^{-6} \left( 1 + 6 + \frac{6^2}{2} + \frac{6^3}{6} + \frac{6^4}{24} \right) = e^{-6}(1 + 6 + 18 + 36 + 54) = 115 \thinspace e^{-6}
$$

4. With $e^{-6} \approx 0.0024788$:

$$
P(L \gt 12) \approx 115 \times 0.0024788 \approx 0.2851
$$

**Answer.** The system lasts 10 years on average, and lasts more than 12 years
with probability about 0.285.

**Check.** 12 years is about half a standard deviation above the mean, and the
distribution is skewed right, so a chance a bit under one third is sensible.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 1 in two ways: by adding exponentials and by using
numpy's Gamma sampler. Note that numpy's `rng.gamma` takes `shape` and a
**scale** $= \frac{1}{\beta}$, not the rate.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
shape, rate = 3, 2.0

# Way 1: the sum of 3 independent Exp(2) waits (numpy scale = 1/rate).
waits = rng.exponential(scale=1 / rate, size=(n_trials, shape))
t_sum = waits.sum(axis=1)

# Way 2: numpy's Gamma sampler, which also wants scale = 1/rate.
t_gamma = rng.gamma(shape=shape, scale=1 / rate, size=n_trials)

exact = 1 - 5 * np.exp(-2)
print(f"P(T <= 1): sum of exps {np.mean(t_sum <= 1):.4f}, "
      f"gamma {np.mean(t_gamma <= 1):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"E[T]:      sum of exps {t_sum.mean():.4f}, "
      f"gamma {t_gamma.mean():.4f}, exact {shape / rate:.4f}")
print(f"Var(T):    sum of exps {t_sum.var():.4f}, "
      f"gamma {t_gamma.var():.4f}, exact {shape / rate**2:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(T <= 1): sum of exps 0.3236, gamma 0.3231, exact 0.3233
E[T]:      sum of exps 1.4993, gamma 1.5016, exact 1.5000
Var(T):    sum of exps 0.7483, gamma 0.7511, exact 0.7500
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Passing the rate to numpy.** `rng.gamma(3, 2)` has scale 2, so its mean is
  $3 \times 2 = 6$. For $\text{Gamma}(3, 2)$ with rate 2, write
  `rng.gamma(shape=3, scale=1/2)`.
- **Mixing the two parameterizations.** Some books write $\text{Gamma}(k, \theta)$
  with a scale $\theta$ and mean $k\theta$. In this book the second parameter is
  the **rate** and the mean is $\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$.
- **Adding Gammas with different rates.** $\text{Gamma}(2, 1) + \text{Gamma}(3, 5)$
  is **not** a Gamma. The shapes add only when the rates are equal.
- **Off-by-one with the Gamma function.** $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$, not $n!$. So
  $\Gamma(4) = 6$.
- **Expecting a closed-form CDF for every shape.** It exists only for whole-number
  shapes (via the Poisson sum). Otherwise use software.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the Gamma is the standard prior for a Poisson
  rate or for the precision $\frac{1}{\sigma^2}$ of a normal in Bayesian models
  (see [10.7 Bayes for Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md));
  sampling Gammas is also how Beta and Dirichlet variables are made
  ([9.12 Dirichlet](9.12-Dirichlet.md)).
- **Computer science:** the time until the $n$-th request, packet or failure;
  total service time of $n$ jobs in a queue.
- **Insurance and finance:** sizes of insurance claims and total losses over a
  year.
- **Science:** rainfall amounts, and the waiting time for several mutations or
  radioactive decays.
- **Everyday life:** how long until the 5th bus passes, or until you have
  collected 10 customer reviews.

<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\Gamma(5) = 4! = 24, \qquad \Gamma(1) = 0! = 1, \qquad \Gamma\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \approx 0.8862$$

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Gamma}(4, 0.5)$. Find $E[X]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = \frac{4}{0.5} = 8, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{4}{0.5^2} = 16$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Goals in a football match come at rate 3 per 90 minutes. What
is the probability that the second goal comes before the 30th minute?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In 30 minutes the expected number of goals is $3 \times \frac{30}{90} = 1$. The second goal comes before minute 30 if at least 2 goals happen in that window, where the count is $\text{Pois}(1)$:

$$P(T_2 \le 30) = 1 - e^{-1}(1 + 1) = 1 - 2e^{-1} \approx 1 - 0.7358 = 0.2642$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Find the mode (the peak) of the $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ density
for $\alpha \gt 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Maximize $\ln f(x) = \text{const} + (\alpha - 1)\ln x - \beta x$. Set its derivative to zero:

$$\frac{\alpha - 1}{x} - \beta = 0 \implies x = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\beta}$$

For $\text{Gamma}(3, 2)$ the mode is $1$ minute, less than the mean $1.5$, because of the long right tail.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that if $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ with $\alpha \gt 1$, then
$E[\frac{1}{X}] = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the key integral with $a = \alpha - 1$ (which needs $\alpha \gt 1$):

$$E\left[\frac{1}{X}\right] = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha - 2} e^{-\beta x} \thinspace dx = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha - 1)}{\beta^{\alpha - 1}} = \frac{\beta \thinspace \Gamma(\alpha - 1)}{(\alpha - 1)\Gamma(\alpha - 1)} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1}$$

Note that $E[\frac{1}{X}] \ne \frac{1}{E[X]} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$. This result is used for the Student t in [9.6](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2: simulate $\text{Gamma}(5, 0.5)$ and estimate

## Summary

- $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x} dx$ extends the factorial:
  $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$, $\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha\Gamma(\alpha)$, $\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}) = \sqrt{\pi}$.
- $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ has shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$; for whole $\alpha$ it
  is the waiting time for the $\alpha$-th event of a stream of events with rate $\beta$.
- Mean $\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$, variance $\frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}$; a sum of $n$
  independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ is $\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$.
- The key integral $\int_0^{\infty} x^{a-1} e^{-\beta x} dx = \frac{\Gamma(a)}{\beta^a}$
  gives every moment.
- In numpy: `rng.gamma(shape=alpha, scale=1/beta, size=n)`. The scale is $\frac{1}{\beta}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f(x) = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x}, \quad E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}}
$$
