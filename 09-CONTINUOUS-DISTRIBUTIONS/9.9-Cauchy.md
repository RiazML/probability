# 9.9 Cauchy

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.9 Cauchy

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [9.8 Log-Normal](9.8-Log-Normal.md)

> **Big idea:** The Cauchy distribution looks like a harmless bell curve, but its tails are so heavy that it has no mean and no variance, and the average of many Cauchy values is just as wild as a single one.

---

## Why It Matters

Almost every result you will learn later, such as the Law of Large Numbers
and the Central Limit Theorem, assumes that a mean exists. The Cauchy
distribution is the classic example where that assumption fails. It looks
symmetric and centered, so you would guess its mean is the center, but
averaging Cauchy data **never** settles down. It is a warning label for heavy
tails in finance, physics and machine learning, and a useful tool too: it
describes resonance in physics and is a popular prior in Bayesian models.

## Intuition

Stand a laser pointer 2 meters in front of a very long wall. Spin it so that
it points in a completely random direction toward the wall: any angle from
$-90$ to $+90$ degrees, all equally likely. Where does the red dot land?

Most angles are moderate, so the dot is usually within a few meters of the
spot straight ahead. But when the angle is close to $\pm 90$ degrees, the
laser points almost along the wall and the dot lands extremely far away:
100 m, 1 km, 1000 km. These huge values are rare, but not rare enough.

Now try to estimate "where the dot lands on average" by repeating the spin
many times and averaging. Every so often, one spin sends the dot so far that
it drags the whole average with it. However many spins you do, the average
keeps jumping. In fact the average of 1000 spins has **exactly the same**
distribution as a single spin.

## Picture

```text
                x0                    X = x0 + γ tan θ
   -------------+---------------------*----------------- the wall (x-axis)
                |                   .
                |                 .
                |               .
                |  γ          .
                |           .
                |         .
                |  θ    .
                |     .
                |   .
                | .
                *  the laser, at distance γ from the wall
```

The laser is spun to a uniform random angle $\theta$ between $-90$ and $+90$
degrees from the straight-ahead line; the dot lands at $x_0 + \gamma \tan\theta$,
which has a Cauchy distribution.

## Definition

A random variable $X$ has the **Cauchy distribution** with **location** $x_0$
and **scale** $\gamma \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Cauchy}(x_0, \gamma)$, if its PDF is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi \gamma \left( 1 + \left( \frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} \right)^2 \right)}, \qquad -\infty \lt x \lt \infty
$$

where:

- $x_0$ is the center: the median and the mode (but **not** a mean);
- $\gamma$ is the **scale**: half the width of the curve at half its peak height. The
  quartiles are $x_0 - \gamma$ and $x_0 + \gamma$;
- the support is the whole real line.

The **standard Cauchy** is $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$, with PDF $\frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$.

**In words:** a bell-shaped curve whose height falls off only like
$\frac{1}{x^2}$, far more slowly than the normal's $e^{-x^2/2}$.

The CDF has a closed form:

$$
F(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{arctan}\left( \frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} \right)
$$

**In words:** the chance of landing left of $x$ is the fraction of angles that
point left of $x$.

The mean and variance:

$$
E[X] \text{ does not exist}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) \text{ does not exist}
$$

## Why It Is True

### The Laser Story Gives the CDF

Let $\theta \sim \text{Unif}(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2})$ (angles in radians) and
$X = x_0 + \gamma \tan\theta$. The tangent is increasing on this interval, so:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(x) &= P\left( \tan\theta \le \frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} \right) = P\left( \theta \le \operatorname{arctan} \frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} \right) && \text{(}\tan\text{ is increasing)} \\
&= \frac{\operatorname{arctan}\frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} + \frac{\pi}{2}}{\pi} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{arctan}\left( \frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} \right) && \text{(uniform CDF on a length-}\pi\text{ interval)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Differentiate, using $\frac{d}{du} \operatorname{arctan} u = \frac{1}{1 + u^2}$ and the chain rule
(inner derivative $\frac{1}{\gamma}$):

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \left( \frac{x - x_0}{\gamma} \right)^2} \cdot \frac{1}{\gamma}
$$

That is the Cauchy PDF. As $x \to \pm\infty$, $F(x) \to \frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{1}{2}$, so the total area is 1.

### Why There Is No Mean

A mean exists only if $E[\lvert X \rvert]$ is finite. For the standard Cauchy:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[\lvert X \rvert] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\lvert x \rvert}{\pi(1 + x^2)} \thinspace dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \thinspace dx && \text{(symmetry)} \\
&= \frac{2}{\pi} \lim_{M \to \infty} \frac{1}{2} \ln(1 + M^2) = \infty && \text{(antiderivative } \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1 + x^2) \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For large $x$ the integrand is about $\frac{1}{\pi x}$, and $\int \frac{1}{x} dx = \ln x$ grows
forever. The right half alone is $+\infty$ and the left half alone is $-\infty$, and
"infinity minus infinity" has no meaning. So the mean does not exist. Neither
does the variance: since $x^2 \ge \lvert x \rvert - 1$ for every $x$, also $E[X^2] = \infty$.

> **Warning:** It is tempting to say that the curve is symmetric, so the mean
> is $x_0$. Symmetry gives the median, not the mean. A mean must be the limit of
> long-run averages, and here no such limit exists.

### Key Properties

- **Averages do not help.** If $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Cauchy}(x_0, \gamma)$, then
  $\bar{X}_n \sim \text{Cauchy}(x_0, \gamma)$, the **same** distribution as one value. The
  proof uses characteristic functions
  ([15.6](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.6-Characteristic-Function.md)). So the Law of
  Large Numbers and the CLT fail ([17.9 When the CLT Fails](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.9-When-the-CLT-Fails.md)).
- **Ratio of normals.** If $Z_1, Z_2$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, then
  $\frac{Z_1}{Z_2} \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$
  ([13.3](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)).
  It is also the Student $t_1$ from [9.6](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md).
- **Power-law tail.** For large $x$, $P(X \gt x) \approx \frac{1}{\pi x}$ for the standard
  Cauchy. One tenth as likely needs ten times as far, not a little further.
- **Stable under linear maps.** $aX + b \sim \text{Cauchy}(a x_0 + b, \lvert a \rvert \gamma)$ for $a \ne 0$,
  and $\frac{1}{X} \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$ when $X \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Laser Dot

**Problem.** A laser stands $\gamma = 2$ m from a wall, aimed straight at the point
$x_0 = 0$, and is spun to a uniform random angle. Find (a) the probability the
dot lands within 2 m of the center, (b) the probability it lands more than 20 m
away, (c) the quartiles.

**Solution.**

1. $X \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 2)$, so $F(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{arctan}\frac{x}{2}$.
2. (a) Use the CDF at $\pm 2$, with $\operatorname{arctan} 1 = \frac{\pi}{4}$:

$$
P(\lvert X \rvert \le 2) = F(2) - F(-2) = \frac{2}{\pi} \operatorname{arctan}(1) = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

3. (b) Both tails beyond 20, with $\operatorname{arctan} 10 \approx 1.4711$:

$$
P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 20) = 1 - \frac{2}{\pi} \operatorname{arctan}(10) \approx 1 - \frac{2 \times 1.4711}{3.1416} \approx 1 - 0.9365 = 0.0635
$$

4. (c) Part (a) shows that half the mass is in $[-2, 2]$, and by symmetry a
   quarter is below $-2$ and a quarter above $2$.

**Answer.** (a) $0.5$. (b) About $0.0635$: more than 6% of dots land over 10
times the scale away. (c) The quartiles are $-2$ and $2$.

**Check.** For a normal, landing 10 standard deviations away has probability
about $10^{-23}$. The Cauchy does it 6% of the time. That is what "heavy tail" means.

### Example 2: Averaging Does Not Help

**Problem.** You average $n = 1000$ independent $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$ values. What is
$P(\lvert \bar{X} \rvert \gt 1)$? Compare with the average of 1000 independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ values.

**Solution.**

1. The average of iid Cauchy values is again $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$, whatever $n$ is.
   So, as in Example 1 with $\gamma = 1$:

$$
P(\lvert \bar{X} \rvert \gt 1) = 1 - \frac{2}{\pi} \operatorname{arctan}(1) = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = 0.5
$$

2. For the normal, $\bar{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{1}{1000})$, so its standard deviation is
   $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1000}} \approx 0.0316$, and 1 is about $31.6$ standard deviations away:

$$
P(\lvert \bar{Z} \rvert \gt 1) = 2\left( 1 - \Phi(31.6) \right) \approx 0
$$

**Answer.** The Cauchy average misses the center by more than 1 half of the
time, exactly like a single value. The normal average essentially never does.

**Check.** The simulation below shows the running mean of Cauchy data jumping
around even after a million samples.

## Simulate It

This code tracks the running mean of Cauchy and normal samples, then checks
Example 2. numpy has only the standard Cauchy, so scale and shift it yourself:
`x0 + gamma * rng.standard_cauchy(n)`.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

cauchy = rng.standard_cauchy(n)          # Cauchy(0, 1)
normal = rng.standard_normal(n)          # N(0, 1)
steps = np.arange(1, n + 1)
run_c = np.cumsum(cauchy) / steps        # running means
run_n = np.cumsum(normal) / steps

for k in [100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    print(f"n = {k:>9,}: Cauchy mean {run_c[k - 1]:>8.3f}   normal mean {run_n[k - 1]:>7.4f}")

# Averages of 1000 Cauchy values, repeated 10,000 times.
means = rng.standard_cauchy((10_000, 1_000)).mean(axis=1)
print(f"P(|mean of 1000| > 1): simulation {np.mean(np.abs(means) > 1):.4f}, exact 0.5000")
```

Output:

```text
n =       100: Cauchy mean   -0.313   normal mean -0.1213
n =     1,000: Cauchy mean    0.821   normal mean -0.0289
n =    10,000: Cauchy mean   -0.301   normal mean -0.0082
n =   100,000: Cauchy mean   -0.302   normal mean  0.0004
n = 1,000,000: Cauchy mean   -0.387   normal mean  0.0009
P(|mean of 1000| > 1): simulation 0.5017, exact 0.5000
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling $x_0$ the mean.** $x_0$ is the median and the mode. The mean does not
  exist.
- **Trusting the sample mean.** For Cauchy-like data, the sample mean is
  dominated by the single most extreme value. Use the median, which works fine
  here.
- **Thinking more data always fixes noise.** The Law of Large Numbers needs a
  finite mean. Without it, averaging 1000 values is no better than taking one.
- **Judging tails by the picture.** Near the center the Cauchy looks like a
  slightly flat normal. The difference is in the far tails, which no plot shows well.
- **Assuming "symmetric" means "the mean is the center".** Symmetry plus a
  finite $E[\lvert X \rvert]$ gives that. Without a finite $E[\lvert X \rvert]$, there is no mean at all.

## Where It Is Used

- **Physics:** the shape of spectral lines and resonance curves (called the
  Lorentzian or Breit-Wigner distribution) is Cauchy.
- **AI and machine learning:** t-SNE uses a Cauchy-shaped kernel so that far
  points stay far apart; half-Cauchy priors are a standard choice for scale
  parameters in Bayesian models; Cauchy noise is used to test robust methods.
- **Statistics and computer science:** a stress test for algorithms that
  assume finite variance, and a reason to prefer medians in robust estimation.
- **Finance and risk:** a reminder that some data may have no stable average
  ([22.13 Fat Tails](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.13-Fat-Tails.md)).
- **Geometry:** the position where a randomly aimed ray hits a line, as in the
  laser story.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Cauchy}(5, 3)$. Find the median and the two quartiles.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The median is $x_0 = 5$. The quartiles are $x_0 \pm \gamma$:

$$Q_1 = 5 - 3 = 2, \qquad Q_3 = 5 + 3 = 8$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For the standard Cauchy, find $P(X \gt 1)$ and $P(X \gt \sqrt{3})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \gt 1) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(1) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$$

$$P(X \gt \sqrt{3}) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1667$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Find the peak height of the $\text{Cauchy}(x_0, \gamma)$ density, and show
that the density equals half the peak at $x = x_0 \pm \gamma$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

At $x = x_0$ the bracket is $1$, so the peak is $\frac{1}{\pi\gamma}$. At $x = x_0 \pm \gamma$:

$$f(x_0 \pm \gamma) = \frac{1}{\pi\gamma(1 + 1)} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\pi\gamma}$$

So $\gamma$ is the "half width at half maximum".

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that if $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, then $X = \tan(\pi(U - \frac{1}{2}))$ is
standard Cauchy. (This is how to sample it by inverse transform.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\theta = \pi(U - \frac{1}{2})$ is uniform on $(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2})$, which is exactly the laser story with $x_0 = 0$ and $\gamma = 1$. Equivalently, solving $F(x) = u$ gives the inverse CDF:

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(x) = u \implies x = \tan\left(\pi\left(u - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\right)$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that if $X \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$, then $\frac{1}{X} \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the laser story: $X = \tan\theta$ with $\theta$ uniform on $(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2})$. Then $\frac{1}{X} = \frac{1}{\tan\theta} = \tan(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta)$ for $\theta \gt 0$ and $\tan(-\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta)$ for $\theta \lt 0$. Define $\psi = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$ for $\theta \gt 0$ and $\psi = -\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$ for $\theta \lt 0$:

$$\frac{1}{X} = \tan\psi$$

The first rule maps $(0, \frac{\pi}{2})$ onto itself and the second maps $(-\frac{\pi}{2}, 0)$ onto itself, each by a reflection, so $\psi$ is again uniform on $(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2})$. Hence $\frac{1}{X} = \tan\psi$ is standard Cauchy.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check that $\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}$ for independent standard normals has
$P(\lvert \frac{Z_1}{Z_2} \rvert \le 1) = \frac{1}{2}$, as a standard Cauchy should.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
ratio = rng.standard_normal(n) / rng.standard_normal(n)
print(f"P(|ratio| <= 1): simulation {np.mean(np.abs(ratio) <= 1):.4f}, exact 0.5000")
print(f"median of ratio: {np.median(ratio):.4f}, exact 0.0000")
```

Output:

```text
P(|ratio| <= 1): simulation 0.5003, exact 0.5000
median of ratio: -0.0003, exact 0.0000
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Cauchy}(x_0, \gamma)$ is where a randomly aimed laser hits a wall: $x_0 + \gamma\tan\theta$.
- PDF $\frac{1}{\pi\gamma(1 + ((x - x_0)/\gamma)^2)}$, CDF $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}\frac{x - x_0}{\gamma}$.
- Median and mode $x_0$, quartiles $x_0 \pm \gamma$; **no mean and no variance**.
- The average of $n$ iid Cauchy values is Cauchy with the same parameters, so the LLN
  and CLT fail.
- In numpy: `x0 + gamma * rng.standard_cauchy(size=n)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[\lvert X \rvert] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\lvert x \rvert}{\pi(1 + x^2)} \thinspace dx = \infty}
$$
