# 9.10 Logistic and Gumbel

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.10 Logistic and Gumbel

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [9.9 Cauchy](9.9-Cauchy.md)

> **Big idea:** The logistic distribution is the one whose CDF is the sigmoid, which is why it sits inside logistic regression, and the Gumbel distribution is the shape of the maximum of many random values, which is why it models floods and powers the Gumbel-max trick.

---

## Why It Matters

Two distributions, one family of ideas. The **logistic** distribution explains
where the sigmoid function $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ in logistic regression and neural
networks comes from: it is a CDF. The **Gumbel** distribution answers "how big
is the biggest?": the highest flood in a year, the largest load on a server in
a day, the strongest wind gust. In machine learning, Gumbel noise turns
sampling from a categorical distribution into taking a maximum (the
**Gumbel-max trick**), the basis of the Gumbel-softmax used in many models.

## Intuition

**Logistic.** A spam filter computes a score $s$ for an email. Imagine the
"true spamminess" is $s$ plus some random noise $\varepsilon$, and the email is spam
when the result is positive. Then $P(\text{spam}) = P(\varepsilon \gt -s)$. If the noise
is logistic, this probability is exactly the sigmoid of $s$. So the sigmoid is
not a random choice of curve: it is the CDF of a bell-shaped noise, slightly
heavier-tailed than the normal.

**Gumbel.** A river's water level changes every day. The level that matters for
flood defenses is the **maximum** over the year. A maximum of 365 daily values
is not centered like an average: it is pushed to the right and has a longer
right tail, because one extreme day is enough to make it large. However the
daily levels are distributed (within a wide class), the yearly maximum has
approximately the same shape, the Gumbel shape. This is like the Central Limit
Theorem, but for maximums instead of sums.

## Picture

```text
  f(x)
  0.4 +
      |                        gggg
      |                       g    g          g: Gumbel(0, 1)
      |                      g      g            peak at 0, skewed right
      |                     glllllll gg       l: Logistic(0, 1)
  0.2 +                    lg       ll g         symmetric about 0
      |                  llg          llgg
      |               lll g             llg
      |            lll   g                 ggg
      |      llllll     g                     gggggg
    0 +ggggggggggggggggg                            ggggggggggg
      +-------------------------------------------------------- x
            -4        -2        0         2         4         6
```

The logistic is a symmetric bell (a bit like a normal), while the Gumbel is
skewed to the right with a short left tail and a long right tail.

## Definition

### The Logistic Distribution

$X$ has the **logistic distribution** with **location** $\mu$ and **scale** $s \gt 0$,
written $X \sim \text{Logistic}(\mu, s)$, if its CDF is the sigmoid:

$$
F(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(x - \mu)/s}}, \qquad f(x) = \frac{e^{-(x - \mu)/s}}{s \left( 1 + e^{-(x - \mu)/s} \right)^2} = \frac{1}{s} F(x) \left( 1 - F(x) \right)
$$

with support the whole real line. Here $\mu$ is the center and $s$ is a scale
(not the standard deviation). The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\pi^2 s^2}{3}
$$

**In words:** a symmetric bell whose CDF is the sigmoid; the standard deviation
is $\frac{\pi s}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.814 \thinspace s$.

### The Gumbel Distribution

$X$ has the **Gumbel distribution** with **location** $\mu$ and **scale** $\beta \gt 0$,
written $X \sim \text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$, if its CDF is:

$$
F(x) = \exp\left( -e^{-(x - \mu)/\beta} \right), \qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{\beta} \exp\left( -z - e^{-z} \right) \text{ with } z = \frac{x - \mu}{\beta}
$$

with support the whole real line. Here $\mu$ is the mode (the peak) and $\beta$ is a
scale. The mean and variance are:

$$
E[X] = \mu + \beta\gamma, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\pi^2 \beta^2}{6}
$$

where $\gamma \approx 0.5772$ is the **Euler-Mascheroni constant** (not a parameter).

**In words:** a "double exponential" CDF; the mean sits $0.5772 \thinspace \beta$ to the
right of the peak because of the long right tail.

## Why It Is True

### The Sigmoid Inside Logistic Regression

Let the score be $s$ and the noise $\varepsilon \sim \text{Logistic}(0, 1)$. The label is 1
when $s + \varepsilon \gt 0$. By symmetry of the logistic, $P(\varepsilon \gt -s) = P(\varepsilon \lt s)$, so:

$$
P(\text{label} = 1) = P(\varepsilon \gt -s) = P(\varepsilon \lt s) = F(s) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-s}}
$$

That is exactly the logistic regression model. With normal noise instead, you
get the **probit** model, whose curve $\Phi(s)$ looks very similar.

### Logistic Mean and Variance

The PDF is symmetric about $\mu$: replacing $x - \mu$ by $-(x - \mu)$ multiplies the
top and bottom by $e^{2(x - \mu)/s}$, which leaves $f$ unchanged. The tails decay
like $e^{-\lvert x \rvert / s}$, so the mean exists, and $E[X] = \mu$.

<details>
<summary>Variance proof (optional)</summary>

Take $\mu = 0$, $s = 1$. By symmetry, $E[X^2] = 2\int_0^{\infty} x^2 \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} dx$. Expand $\frac{u}{(1 + u)^2} = \sum_{k \ge 1} (-1)^{k+1} k u^k$ with $u = e^{-x} \lt 1$, and use $\int_0^{\infty} x^2 e^{-kx} dx = \frac{2}{k^3}$:

$$E[X^2] = 2 \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k+1} k \cdot \frac{2}{k^3} = 4 \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k^2} = 4 \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{12} = \frac{\pi^2}{3}$$

The alternating sum $1 - \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{9} - \dots = \frac{\pi^2}{12}$ is a classic result. For general $s$, $X = \mu + sX_0$, so the variance is $\frac{\pi^2 s^2}{3}$.

</details>

### Gumbel as the Log of an Exponential

Let $E \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and $G = -\ln E$. For any $x$:

$$
P(G \le x) = P(-\ln E \le x) = P(E \ge e^{-x}) = e^{-e^{-x}}
$$

So $G \sim \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$. The moments then come from the Gamma function:
$E[\ln E] = \Gamma'(1) = -\gamma$, and $\operatorname{Var}(\ln E) = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$ (two classic
constants, which we state without proof). Hence $E[G] = \gamma$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(G) = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$, and for $\mu + \beta G$ we get $\mu + \beta\gamma$ and $\frac{\pi^2\beta^2}{6}$.

### Why Maximums Become Gumbel

**Claim.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(1)$ and $M_n = \max(X_1, \dots, X_n)$.
Then $M_n - \ln n$ converges in distribution to $\text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$.

**Proof.** The maximum is at most $t$ exactly when every $X_i$ is at most $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(M_n - \ln n \le x) &= P(\text{all } X_i \le x + \ln n) = \left( 1 - e^{-(x + \ln n)} \right)^n && \text{(independence)} \\
&= \left( 1 - \frac{e^{-x}}{n} \right)^n && \text{(}e^{-\ln n} = 1/n\text{)} \\
&\to \exp\left( -e^{-x} \right) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty && \text{(}(1 + a/n)^n \to e^{a}\text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the Gumbel CDF. A general theorem (the Fisher-Tippett theorem of
extreme value theory) says the same shape appears for maximums of normals,
log-normals, Gammas and many other distributions whose tails are thinner than
any power law (power-law tails lead to a different limit, see [9.13](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).

### The Gumbel-Max Trick

**Claim.** Let $p_1, \dots, p_k$ be probabilities that sum to 1, and let
$G_1, \dots, G_k \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$. Then the index $I$ of the largest
$\ln p_i + G_i$ satisfies $P(I = i) = p_i$.

**Proof.** Write $G_i = -\ln E_i$ with $E_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(1)$. Then
$\ln p_i + G_i = -\ln \frac{E_i}{p_i}$, and the largest of these is the smallest
$\frac{E_i}{p_i}$. By scaling ([9.3](9.3-Exponential.md#more-key-properties)),
$\frac{E_i}{p_i} \sim \text{Exp}(p_i)$, an exponential clock with rate $p_i$. In a race of
independent exponential clocks, clock $i$ finishes first with probability
$\frac{p_i}{p_1 + \dots + p_k} = p_i$.

### Key Properties

- **Sampling by inverse CDF.** With $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$:
  $\mu + s \ln\frac{U}{1 - U} \sim \text{Logistic}(\mu, s)$ (the inverse sigmoid is the **logit**), and
  $\mu - \beta \ln(-\ln U) \sim \text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$.
- **Gumbel minus Gumbel is logistic.** If $G_1, G_2 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$, then
  $G_1 - G_2 \sim \text{Logistic}(0, 1)$. This links the two halves of this topic and
  explains why "choose the option with the largest utility plus Gumbel noise"
  gives logistic and softmax choice probabilities.
- **Max-stability.** The maximum of $n$ iid $\text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$ is
  $\text{Gumbel}(\mu + \beta\ln n, \beta)$: the shape stays the same, it just shifts.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The 100-Year Flood

**Problem.** The highest river level each year is $\text{Gumbel}(5, 0.8)$ in meters.
(a) What is the mean yearly maximum? (b) What is the chance that next year's
maximum is above 8 m? (c) What level is exceeded only once in 100 years on
average (the **100-year flood**)?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The mean:

$$
E[X] = 5 + 0.8 \times 0.5772 = 5 + 0.4618 = 5.462 \text{ m}
$$

2. (b) Standardize: $z = \frac{8 - 5}{0.8} = 3.75$. Then:

$$
P(X \gt 8) = 1 - \exp\left( -e^{-3.75} \right) = 1 - \exp(-0.023518) \approx 1 - 0.976757 = 0.02324
$$

3. (c) We need $F(x) = 0.99$. Solve the CDF for $x$:

$$
\exp\left( -e^{-(x - 5)/0.8} \right) = 0.99 \implies e^{-(x - 5)/0.8} = -\ln 0.99 \approx 0.0100503
$$

4. Take logs again:

$$
\frac{x - 5}{0.8} = -\ln(0.0100503) \approx 4.6001 \implies x \approx 5 + 0.8 \times 4.6001 = 8.680 \text{ m}
$$

**Answer.** (a) About 5.46 m. (b) About 2.3% per year, so roughly once in 43
years. (c) About 8.68 m.

**Check.** The 100-year level (8.68 m) is above the 8 m level, which happens
once in about 43 years. Consistent. Note that "once in 100 years on average"
still gives about a $1 - 0.99^{30} \approx 0.26$ chance of at least one such flood in 30 years.

### Example 2: Chess Ratings

**Problem.** In the Elo rating system, a player rated $R_A$ beats a player rated
$R_B$ with probability $\frac{1}{1 + 10^{-(R_A - R_B)/400}}$. Show this is a logistic CDF and
find the chance that a player rated 1700 beats one rated 1500.

**Solution.**

1. Write $10^{-d/400} = e^{-d \ln 10 / 400} = e^{-d/s}$ with $s = \frac{400}{\ln 10} \approx 173.7$.
   So the win probability is $F(d)$ for $\text{Logistic}(0, 173.7)$, where $d = R_A - R_B$.
   You can read it as: each game's "performance difference" is $d$ plus logistic
   noise, and A wins when it is positive.
2. Here $d = 1700 - 1500 = 200$:

$$
P(\text{A wins}) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-200/400}} = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-0.5}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.31623} \approx 0.7597
$$

**Answer.** About 76%.

**Check.** A 0-point gap gives $\frac{1}{1 + 1} = 0.5$, a fair game. A 400-point gap
gives $\frac{1}{1 + 0.1} \approx 0.91$. The curve is the sigmoid, as expected.

## Simulate It

This code checks the flood numbers, the "maximum becomes Gumbel" claim, and the
Gumbel-max trick. numpy's `rng.gumbel(loc, scale)` and `rng.logistic(loc, scale)`
use the same location and scale as this book.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Yearly maximum river level: Gumbel(5, 0.8).
level = rng.gumbel(loc=5, scale=0.8, size=n)
print(f"E[X]:      simulation {level.mean():.4f}, exact {5 + 0.8 * np.euler_gamma:.4f}")
print(f"P(X > 8):  simulation {np.mean(level > 8):.5f}, exact {1 - np.exp(-np.exp(-3.75)):.5f}")

# Max of 1000 Exp(1) values, minus ln(1000), should be close to Gumbel(0, 1).
m = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(100_000, 1000)).max(axis=1) - np.log(1000)
print(f"P(M - ln n <= 1): simulation {np.mean(m <= 1):.4f}, Gumbel {np.exp(-np.exp(-1)):.4f}")

# Gumbel-max trick: argmax(log p + G) is a sample from p.
p = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
g = rng.gumbel(size=(n, 3))
choice = np.argmax(np.log(p) + g, axis=1)
print(f"Gumbel-max frequencies: {np.bincount(choice) / n}, target {p}")
```

Output:

```text
E[X]:      simulation 5.4615, exact 5.4618
P(X > 8):  simulation 0.02315, exact 0.02324
P(M - ln n <= 1): simulation 0.6920, Gumbel 0.6922
Gumbel-max frequencies: [0.500604 0.299511 0.199885], target [0.5 0.3 0.2]
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the scale as the standard deviation.** For $\text{Logistic}(\mu, s)$ the
  standard deviation is $1.814 \thinspace s$; for $\text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$ it is $1.283 \thinspace \beta$.
- **Calling the Gumbel's $\mu$ its mean.** $\mu$ is the mode. The mean is $\mu + 0.5772 \thinspace \beta$.
- **Using a Gumbel for minimums.** This Gumbel is for maximums. For the minimum,
  use $-X$ (the "minimum Gumbel"), or a [Weibull](9.11-Weibull.md) for positive
  lifetimes.
- **Reading a return period as a promise.** A 100-year flood can happen two
  years in a row. Each year has a 1% chance, independently.
- **Forgetting the log in the Gumbel-max trick.** Add Gumbel noise to $\ln p_i$
  (or to unnormalized log-scores), not to $p_i$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** logistic regression and the sigmoid output of
  neural networks; the Gumbel-max trick and Gumbel-softmax for sampling
  discrete choices inside differentiable models; sampling the next token from
  a language model ([21.1.3 Language Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.3-Language-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** the maximum response time or load over many requests,
  and the maximum bucket size in hashing, follow Gumbel-like laws.
- **Engineering and climate:** floods, maximum wind speeds, earthquake
  magnitudes over a period, and the design of dams and bridges.
- **Economics and games:** discrete choice models (multinomial logit) and Elo
  ratings in chess and online games.
- **Everyday life:** "the hottest day of the summer" and "the longest queue
  of the week" are maximums, so they follow Gumbel-like shapes.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Logistic}(0, 1)$. Find $P(X \le 2)$ and the standard deviation of $X$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \le 2) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2}} = \frac{1}{1.13534} \approx 0.8808, \qquad \sigma = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.814$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$. Find $P(X \le 0)$, the chance of being below the peak.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \le 0) = \exp(-e^{0}) = e^{-1} \approx 0.3679$$

Only about 37% of the mass is left of the mode, because the distribution is skewed right.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Find the median of $\text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $\exp(-e^{-(m - \mu)/\beta}) = \frac{1}{2}$, so $e^{-(m - \mu)/\beta} = \ln 2$:

$$m = \mu - \beta \ln(\ln 2) \approx \mu + 0.3665 \thinspace \beta$$

So mode $\lt$ median $\lt$ mean: $\mu \lt \mu + 0.367\beta \lt \mu + 0.577\beta$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that $f(x) = F(x)(1 - F(x))$ for the standard logistic. Why
does this make the sigmoid convenient in neural networks?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $F(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ we have $1 - F(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}$, so:

$$F(x)(1 - F(x)) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = f(x) = F'(x)$$

The derivative of the sigmoid is computed from its own output, with no extra exponentials, which makes backpropagation cheap.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that the maximum of $n$ iid $\text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$ variables is
$\text{Gumbel}(\mu + \beta \ln n, \beta)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By independence, $P(\max \le x) = F(x)^n = \exp(-n e^{-(x - \mu)/\beta})$. Write $n = e^{\ln n}$:

$$\exp\left( -e^{\ln n - (x - \mu)/\beta} \right) = \exp\left( -e^{-(x - \mu - \beta\ln n)/\beta} \right)$$

This is the CDF of $\text{Gumbel}(\mu + \beta\ln n, \beta)$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check by simulation that $G_1 - G_2$ is $\text{Logistic}(0, 1)$ when
$G_1, G_2$ are independent standard Gumbels.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
d = rng.gumbel(size=n) - rng.gumbel(size=n)
for x in [-2, 0, 2]:
    print(f"P(D <= {x:>2}): simulation {np.mean(d <= x):.4f}, logistic {1 / (1 + np.exp(-x)):.4f}")
print(f"Var(D): simulation {d.var():.4f}, exact {np.pi**2 / 3:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(D <= -2): simulation 0.1192, logistic 0.1192
P(D <=  0): simulation 0.4999, logistic 0.5000
P(D <=  2): simulation 0.8811, logistic 0.8808
Var(D): simulation 3.2913, exact 3.2899
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Logistic}(\mu, s)$ has the sigmoid as its CDF; mean $\mu$, variance $\frac{\pi^2 s^2}{3}$.
  Logistic noise on a score gives logistic regression.
- $\text{Gumbel}(\mu, \beta)$ has CDF $\exp(-e^{-(x - \mu)/\beta})$; mode $\mu$, mean $\mu + \beta\gamma$,
  variance $\frac{\pi^2\beta^2}{6}$.
- The maximum of many iid values without power-law tails is about Gumbel; for
  exponentials, $M_n - \ln n \to \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$ exactly in the limit.
- Gumbel-max trick: $\arg\max_i (\ln p_i + G_i)$ is a sample from $p$.
- In numpy: `rng.logistic(loc=mu, scale=s)` and `rng.gumbel(loc=mu, scale=beta)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{F_{\text{Logistic}}(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(x - \mu)/s}}, \qquad F_{\text{Gumbel}}(x) = \exp\left( -e^{-(x - \mu)/\beta} \right)}
$$
