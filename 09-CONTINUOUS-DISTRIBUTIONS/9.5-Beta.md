# 9.5 Beta

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.5 Beta

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [9.4 Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md)

> **Big idea:** The Beta distribution lives on the interval from 0 to 1, so it is the natural way to describe uncertainty about an unknown probability, with its two parameters acting like counts of successes and failures.

---

## Why It Matters

Many things you want to know are themselves probabilities: the click rate of
an ad, the bias of a coin, the chance that a new drug works. You never know
them exactly, so you need a distribution **over probabilities**. The Beta
distribution is that distribution. It is the engine of A/B testing, Thompson
sampling in reinforcement learning, and Bayesian updating of a proportion. It
also appears when you sort uniform random numbers, and its many-category
version is the [Dirichlet](9.12-Dirichlet.md).

## Intuition

You launch a new ad. After it is shown 10 times, it gets 2 clicks. What is the
true click rate $p$? Your best guess is near $0.2$, but $0.1$ or $0.35$ are
clearly possible with so little data. Values like $0.9$ are very unlikely. So
your belief about $p$ is a hump over the interval $[0, 1]$, peaked near $0.2$
and fairly wide.

Now suppose the ad was shown 1000 times with 200 clicks. The best guess is
still $0.2$, but now you are much more certain: the hump is tall and narrow.

The Beta distribution captures exactly this. Its two parameters $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ behave like **pseudo-counts**: roughly, $\alpha - 1$ successes and
$\beta - 1$ failures seen so far. The ratio of the two sets the center of the
hump, and their total sets how narrow it is.

The Beta family can be U-shaped, flat (the uniform), a symmetric hill, or a
skewed hump, all on the interval from 0 to 1.

## Definition

### The Beta Function

The **Beta function** is the area that makes the density integrate to 1.
For $\alpha, \beta \gt 0$:

$$
B(\alpha, \beta) = \int_0^1 x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \thinspace dx = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha) \thinspace \Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}
$$

where $\Gamma$ is the [Gamma function](9.4-Gamma.md#the-gamma-function).

**In words:** the Beta function is a normalizing number, and it can be
computed from three Gamma functions.

### The Beta Distribution

A random variable $X$ has the **Beta distribution** with shape parameters
$\alpha \gt 0$ and $\beta \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, if its
PDF is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}, \qquad 0 \lt x \lt 1
$$

where:

- $\alpha$ pulls the mass toward 1 (think "successes");
- $\beta$ pulls the mass toward 0 (think "failures");
- the support is the interval $(0, 1)$.

Here both parameters are shapes; neither one is a rate or a scale.

**In words:** a power of $x$ times a power of $1 - x$, scaled to have area 1.

The mean, variance and mode are:

$$
E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha \beta}{(\alpha + \beta)^2 (\alpha + \beta + 1)}, \qquad \text{mode} = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} \ (\alpha, \beta \gt 1)
$$

**In words:** the mean is the share of "successes" among all pseudo-counts,
and the variance shrinks like $\frac{1}{\alpha + \beta}$ as the total count grows.

The CDF has no closed form in general (software calls it the **regularized
incomplete beta function**; use `scipy.stats.beta.cdf`). When $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ are whole numbers there is a neat link to the binomial
([7.2 Binomial](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md)):

$$
P(X \le x) = P\big(\text{Bin}(\alpha + \beta - 1, x) \ge \alpha\big)
$$

**In words:** with $n = \alpha + \beta - 1$ uniform numbers, the $\alpha$-th
smallest is below $x$ exactly when at least $\alpha$ of them are below $x$.

## Why It Is True

**The Beta function from Gamma functions.** This identity needs a change of
variables in two dimensions, so the proof is optional.

<details>
<summary>Full proof (optional)</summary>

Multiply two Gamma integrals:

$$\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta) = \int_0^{\infty} \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha - 1} y^{\beta - 1} e^{-(x + y)} \thinspace dx \thinspace dy$$

Substitute $x = uv$ and $y = u(1 - v)$, with $u \gt 0$ and $0 \lt v \lt 1$. The area factor (the Jacobian, see [13.3](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)) is $u$:

$$\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta) = \int_0^{\infty} u^{\alpha + \beta - 1} e^{-u} \thinspace du \cdot \int_0^1 v^{\alpha - 1} (1 - v)^{\beta - 1} \thinspace dv = \Gamma(\alpha + \beta) \thinspace B(\alpha, \beta)$$

Divide by $\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)$. This also shows that $\frac{X}{X + Y} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ when $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, 1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta, 1)$ are independent.

</details>

**The mean.** Multiplying the integrand by $x$ just raises $\alpha$ by one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \frac{1}{B(\alpha, \beta)} \int_0^1 x^{\alpha} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \thinspace dx = \frac{B(\alpha + 1, \beta)}{B(\alpha, \beta)} && \text{(definition of } B \text{)} \\
&= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + 1)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta + 1)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} && \text{(Gamma form of } B \text{)} \\
&= \frac{\alpha \thinspace \Gamma(\alpha)}{(\alpha + \beta)\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} && \text{(}\Gamma(a + 1) = a\Gamma(a)\text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The second moment.** Raise $\alpha$ by two and use the recursion twice:

$$
E[X^2] = \frac{B(\alpha + 2, \beta)}{B(\alpha, \beta)} = \frac{(\alpha + 1)\alpha}{(\alpha + \beta + 1)(\alpha + \beta)}
$$

**The variance.** Put both over the common denominator $(\alpha + \beta)^2(\alpha + \beta + 1)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= \frac{\alpha(\alpha + 1)}{(\alpha + \beta)(\alpha + \beta + 1)} - \frac{\alpha^2}{(\alpha + \beta)^2} && \text{(shortcut formula)} \\
&= \frac{\alpha \left[ (\alpha + 1)(\alpha + \beta) - \alpha(\alpha + \beta + 1) \right]}{(\alpha + \beta)^2 (\alpha + \beta + 1)} && \text{(common denominator)} \\
&= \frac{\alpha \left[ \alpha^2 + \alpha\beta + \alpha + \beta - \alpha^2 - \alpha\beta - \alpha \right]}{(\alpha + \beta)^2 (\alpha + \beta + 1)} = \frac{\alpha \beta}{(\alpha + \beta)^2 (\alpha + \beta + 1)} && \text{(expand and cancel)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Key Properties

- **The uniform is a Beta.** $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ has PDF $\frac{x^0 (1 - x)^0}{B(1, 1)} = 1$
  on $(0, 1)$, which is $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$.
- **Mirror image.** If $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ then
  $1 - X \sim \text{Beta}(\beta, \alpha)$. Swapping successes and failures flips the picture.
- **Shapes.** Both parameters below 1: U-shaped. Both equal: symmetric about
  $\frac{1}{2}$. $\alpha \gt \beta$: mass leans toward 1. Both large: a narrow,
  nearly normal hump.
- **Sorted uniforms.** If you draw $n$ independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ numbers and
  sort them, the $k$-th smallest is $\text{Beta}(k, n + 1 - k)$
  ([13.7 Order Statistics](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.7-Order-Statistics.md)).
- **Bayesian updating.** If your belief about a coin's $p$ is $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$
  and you see $k$ heads in $n$ flips, your new belief is
  $\text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)$: just add the counts. Why this works is in
  [10.7 Bayes for Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Simple Beta Density

**Problem.** $X \sim \text{Beta}(3, 2)$. Write down its PDF, then find $E[X]$, the
standard deviation, the mode and $P(X \gt 0.5)$.

**Solution.**

1. The normalizing number:

$$
B(3, 2) = \frac{\Gamma(3)\Gamma(2)}{\Gamma(5)} = \frac{2! \cdot 1!}{4!} = \frac{2}{24} = \frac{1}{12}
$$

2. So the PDF is $f(x) = 12 x^2 (1 - x)$ for $0 \lt x \lt 1$.
3. Mean, variance and mode:

$$
E[X] = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{3 \cdot 2}{5^2 \cdot 6} = \frac{6}{150} = 0.04, \qquad \text{mode} = \frac{3 - 1}{3 + 2 - 2} = \frac{2}{3}
$$

   The standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.04} = 0.2$.

4. For the probability, first integrate from 0 to 0.5:

$$
P(X \le 0.5) = \int_0^{0.5} 12 (x^2 - x^3) \thinspace dx = 12 \left( \frac{0.5^3}{3} - \frac{0.5^4}{4} \right) = 12 \left( \frac{1}{24} - \frac{1}{64} \right) = 12 \cdot \frac{5}{192} = 0.3125
$$

5. Then take the complement:

$$
P(X \gt 0.5) = 1 - 0.3125 = 0.6875
$$

**Answer.** $f(x) = 12x^2(1-x)$, mean $0.6$, standard deviation $0.2$, mode
about $0.667$, and $P(X \gt 0.5) = 0.6875$.

**Check.** Use the binomial link: $P(X \le 0.5) = P(\text{Bin}(4, 0.5) \ge 3) = \frac{4 + 1}{16} = 0.3125$.
The two methods agree.

### Example 2: How Good Is the New Ad

**Problem.** Your belief about an ad's click rate $p$ is $\text{Beta}(2, 8)$. (a) What
are the mean and standard deviation of your belief? (b) What is the
probability that $p \gt 0.3$?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Mean and variance with $\alpha = 2$, $\beta = 8$, $\alpha + \beta = 10$:

$$
E[p] = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(p) = \frac{2 \cdot 8}{10^2 \cdot 11} = \frac{16}{1100} \approx 0.014545, \qquad \sigma \approx 0.1206
$$

2. (b) Both parameters are whole numbers, so use the binomial link with
   $n = 2 + 8 - 1 = 9$ and $x = 0.3$:

$$
P(p \le 0.3) = P\big(\text{Bin}(9, 0.3) \ge 2\big) = 1 - 0.7^9 - 9 \times 0.3 \times 0.7^8
$$

3. Compute the pieces: $0.7^8 \approx 0.057648$ and $0.7^9 \approx 0.040354$, so
   $9 \times 0.3 \times 0.057648 \approx 0.155650$:

$$
P(p \le 0.3) \approx 1 - 0.040354 - 0.155650 = 0.803996
$$

4. Take the complement:

$$
P(p \gt 0.3) \approx 1 - 0.803996 = 0.1960
$$

**Answer.** You believe the click rate is about $0.2$, give or take $0.12$, and
you give about a 19.6% chance that it is above $0.3$.

**Check.** The standard deviation is large compared with the mean, which
matches "little data so far". If you then see 10 clicks in 50 more views, your
belief becomes $\text{Beta}(12, 48)$: same mean $0.2$, but the standard deviation
drops to about $0.051$.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 2 and the "sorted uniforms" property. numpy's
`rng.beta(a, b)` uses the same two shape parameters as this book.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Belief about the click rate: Beta(2, 8).
p = rng.beta(2, 8, size=n_trials)
print(f"E[p]:        simulation {p.mean():.4f}, exact {0.2:.4f}")
print(f"SD(p):       simulation {p.std():.4f}, exact {np.sqrt(16 / 1100):.4f}")
print(f"P(p > 0.3):  simulation {np.mean(p > 0.3):.4f}, exact {beta.sf(0.3, 2, 8):.4f}")

# The 2nd smallest of 5 uniforms should be Beta(2, 4), with mean 2/6.
u = np.sort(rng.random((n_trials, 5)), axis=1)
second = u[:, 1]
print(f"2nd of 5 uniforms: mean {second.mean():.4f} (exact {2 / 6:.4f})")
print(f"P(2nd <= 0.2): simulation {np.mean(second <= 0.2):.4f}, exact {beta.cdf(0.2, 2, 4):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[p]:        simulation 0.2000, exact 0.2000
SD(p):       simulation 0.1208, exact 0.1206
P(p > 0.3):  simulation 0.1961, exact 0.1960
2nd of 5 uniforms: mean 0.3334 (exact 0.3333)
P(2nd <= 0.2): simulation 0.2626, exact 0.2627
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using a Beta for something outside 0 to 1.** The Beta is only for
  proportions and probabilities. For a positive quantity with no upper limit,
  use a [Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md) or a [log-normal](9.8-Log-Normal.md).
- **Treating $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as exact counts.** $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ acts like
  $\alpha - 1$ successes and $\beta - 1$ failures for the **mode**, but the
  **mean** is $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$. With $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ you have "seen
  nothing", and the mean is $\frac{1}{2}$.
- **Forgetting the normalizing constant.** $x^2(1 - x)$ alone has area
  $\frac{1}{12}$, not 1. Always divide by $B(\alpha, \beta)$.
- **Confusing the Beta function and the Beta distribution.** $B(\alpha, \beta)$ is a
  number; $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ is a distribution.
- **Expecting a symmetric shape.** $\text{Beta}(2, 8)$ is skewed to the right; its
  mode $0.125$, median (about $0.180$) and mean $0.2$ are all different.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Thompson sampling for multi-armed bandits keeps
  a Beta belief for each arm's success rate; Bayesian models use the Beta as
  the prior for a probability; Beta noise is used in mixup data augmentation.
- **Computer science and the web:** A/B testing of click and conversion rates;
  estimating the reliability of a server from success and failure counts.
- **Statistics:** the $k$-th smallest of $n$ uniforms is a Beta, which is used
  to build confidence intervals for medians and percentiles.
- **Project management:** the PERT method models task durations with a scaled Beta.
- **Everyday life:** judging a restaurant with 4 good reviews out of 5 against
  one with 400 out of 500. The Beta tells you how much more to trust the second.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Show that $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ is $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$B(1, 1) = \frac{\Gamma(1)\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(2)} = \frac{1 \cdot 1}{1} = 1, \qquad f(x) = \frac{x^0 (1 - x)^0}{1} = 1 \text{ for } 0 \lt x \lt 1$$

That is the $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ density.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Beta}(4, 4)$. Find $E[X]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Compare the
variance with that of $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = \frac{4}{8} = 0.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{4 \cdot 4}{8^2 \cdot 9} = \frac{16}{576} = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.0278$$

The uniform has variance $\frac{1}{12} \approx 0.0833$, three times larger. $\text{Beta}(4, 4)$ is a hill that piles mass near $0.5$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Prove that if $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, then $Y = 1 - X \sim \text{Beta}(\beta, \alpha)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The map $y = 1 - x$ is one-to-one with $\lvert dx/dy \rvert = 1$, so by [change of variables](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.8-Change-of-Variables.md):

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(1 - y) = \frac{(1 - y)^{\alpha - 1} y^{\beta - 1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)} = \frac{y^{\beta - 1}(1 - y)^{\alpha - 1}}{B(\beta, \alpha)}$$

using $B(\alpha, \beta) = B(\beta, \alpha)$. This is the $\text{Beta}(\beta, \alpha)$ density.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** You start with a $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ belief about a coin's heads
probability and see 7 heads in 10 flips. What is your new belief, its mean and
its mode?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Add the counts: $\text{Beta}(1 + 7, 1 + 3) = \text{Beta}(8, 4)$.

$$E[p] = \frac{8}{12} \approx 0.667, \qquad \text{mode} = \frac{8 - 1}{8 + 4 - 2} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7$$

The mode equals the observed fraction $\frac{7}{10}$; the mean is pulled a little toward $\frac{1}{2}$ by the starting belief.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Find $P(X \le \frac{1}{2})$ for $X \sim \text{Beta}(2, 3)$ in two ways: by
integrating the density and by the binomial link.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$B(2, 3) = \frac{1! \cdot 2!}{4!} = \frac{1}{12}$, so $f(x) = 12x(1 - x)^2 = 12(x - 2x^2 + x^3)$. Integrate:

$$P(X \le \tfrac{1}{2}) = 12\left( \frac{0.5^2}{2} - \frac{2 \cdot 0.5^3}{3} + \frac{0.5^4}{4} \right) = 12\left( 0.125 - 0.08333 + 0.015625 \right) = 12 \times 0.0572917 = 0.6875$$

Binomial link with $n = 2 + 3 - 1 = 4$: $P(\text{Bin}(4, 0.5) \ge 2) = \frac{6 + 4 + 1}{16} = \frac{11}{16} = 0.6875$. The two ways agree.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate $\text{Beta}(2, 3)$ as $\frac{G_1}{G_1 + G_2}$ with independent
$G_1 \sim \text{Gamma}(2, 1)$ and $G_2 \sim \text{Gamma}(3, 1)$, and check Problem 5.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
g1 = rng.gamma(shape=2, scale=1.0, size=n_trials)   # rate 1, so scale 1
g2 = rng.gamma(shape=3, scale=1.0, size=n_trials)
x = g1 / (g1 + g2)                                    # Beta(2, 3)
print(f"P(X <= 0.5): simulation {np.mean(x <= 0.5):.4f}, exact {11 / 16:.4f}")
print(f"E[X]:        simulation {x.mean():.4f}, exact {2 / 5:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X <= 0.5): simulation 0.6874, exact 0.6875
E[X]:        simulation 0.3999, exact 0.4000
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ is a distribution on $(0, 1)$: a distribution over a probability.
- PDF $\frac{x^{\alpha - 1}(1 - x)^{\beta - 1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}$ with
  $B(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}$.
- Mean $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$; the variance shrinks as $\alpha + \beta$ grows.
- $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ is uniform; seeing $k$ successes and $n - k$ failures turns
  $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ into $\text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)$.
- In numpy: `rng.beta(a, b, size=n)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}, \qquad E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}}
$$
