# 9.8 Log-Normal

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.8 Log-Normal

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [9.7 Laplace](9.7-Laplace.md)

> **Big idea:** A variable is log-normal when its logarithm is normal, which happens whenever many small independent effects multiply together, and it gives positive, right-skewed values whose mean is pulled far above the median.

---

## Why It Matters

The normal distribution comes from **adding** many small effects. But many
things in life grow by **multiplying**: money earns a percentage, a city
grows by a percentage, a file is compressed by a factor, a delay is made worse
by a factor at each step. Such quantities are always positive and have a long
right tail. The log-normal models stock prices, incomes, file sizes and web
response times, and it teaches a key lesson: for skewed data, **the mean and
the median tell different stories**.

## Intuition

You invest 1000 dollars. Each year your money is multiplied by a random
factor: maybe 1.25 in a good year, 0.85 in a bad year. After 10 years your
money is $1000 \times F_1 \times F_2 \times \dots \times F_{10}$.

Products are hard, but logarithms turn products into sums:
$\ln(\text{money}) = \ln 1000 + \ln F_1 + \dots + \ln F_{10}$. A sum of many
independent pieces is close to normal (the
[Central Limit Theorem](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)).
So the **logarithm** of your money is roughly normal, and the money itself is
**log-normal**.

What does that look like? A normal on the log scale is symmetric, but going
back with $e^{y}$ stretches the right side a lot (from $\ln$-value 9 to 10 the
money grows by about 14,000 dollars) and squeezes the left side (it can never
go below 0). So you get a hump near the left and a long tail to the right. A
few lucky paths end up very rich, and they pull the **mean** above the
**median**.

## Picture

```text
  f(x)

      |            a    a                 b: σ = 0.5
      |            a     a                c: σ = 1

      |          babbb    a
  0.8 +     cc bb a   bbb  a

      |  c   b   a ccccc   bab
      |  c   b  a       cccccaabbbb
      | c  bb  a               aaacbbbbbbbbccccccccc

      +------------------------------------------------------------- x
       0              1              2              3              4
```

All three curves have median $e^0 = 1$; a larger $\sigma$ pushes the peak left
and stretches the right tail.

## Definition

A positive random variable $X$ has the **log-normal distribution** with
parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, written $X \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if:

$$
\ln X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2), \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad X = e^{Y} \text{ with } Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

Here $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are the mean and **variance of $\ln X$**, not of $X$. The
support is $(0, \infty)$.

The PDF and CDF are:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{x \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(\ln x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \qquad F(x) = \Phi\left( \frac{\ln x - \mu}{\sigma} \right), \qquad x \gt 0
$$

**In words:** to get any log-normal probability, take the log and use the
normal table.

The key numbers are:

$$
\text{median} = e^{\mu}, \qquad \text{mode} = e^{\mu - \sigma^2}, \qquad E[X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \left( e^{\sigma^2} - 1 \right) e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}
$$

**In words:** the median is $e^{\mu}$, and the mean is bigger than the median by
the factor $e^{\sigma^2/2}$; the more spread on the log scale, the bigger the gap.

## Why It Is True

**The CDF.** Since $\ln$ is increasing, $X \le x$ exactly when $\ln X \le \ln x$:

$$
F(x) = P(\ln X \le \ln x) = \Phi\left( \frac{\ln x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)
$$

**The PDF.** Differentiate with the chain rule. The inner function $\ln x$ has
derivative $\frac{1}{x}$, which is where the extra $\frac{1}{x}$ comes from (this is the
[change of variables](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.8-Change-of-Variables.md) formula):

$$
f(x) = \varphi\left( \frac{\ln x - \mu}{\sigma} \right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sigma x} = \frac{1}{x \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(\ln x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}
$$

**The median.** $F(m) = \frac{1}{2}$ needs $\frac{\ln m - \mu}{\sigma} = 0$, so $m = e^{\mu}$.

**The moments.** Every moment of $X$ is $E[X^t] = E[e^{tY}]$ for a normal $Y$. Write
the integral and **complete the square** in the exponent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[e^{tY}] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{ty} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(y - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} \thinspace dy && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(y - \mu - \sigma^2 t)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} \right) dy && \text{(complete the square)} \\
&= e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(y - (\mu + \sigma^2 t))^2/(2\sigma^2)} \thinspace dy && \text{(pull out the constant)} \\
&= e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2} && \text{(a normal density has area 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

To check the second line, expand both exponents: $ty - \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ and
$-\frac{(y - \mu - \sigma^2 t)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}$ are equal for every $y$.

Now put $t = 1$ and $t = 2$:

$$
E[X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad E[X^2] = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2} - e^{2\mu + \sigma^2} = \left( e^{\sigma^2} - 1 \right) e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}
$$

The function $E[e^{tY}]$ is the **moment generating function** of the normal;
you will meet it properly in [15.1](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md).

**The mode.** Maximize $\ln f(x) = \text{const} - \ln x - \frac{(\ln x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$. Its
derivative is $-\frac{1}{x} - \frac{\ln x - \mu}{\sigma^2 x}$, which is zero when
$\ln x = \mu - \sigma^2$, so the mode is $e^{\mu - \sigma^2}$.

### Key Properties

- **Order of the centers.** Since $\sigma^2 \gt 0$, the mode, median and mean are always
  in this order: $e^{\mu - \sigma^2} \lt e^{\mu} \lt e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. Right skew always.
- **Products stay log-normal.** If $X_1 \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and
  $X_2 \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are independent, then
  $X_1 X_2 \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$, because the logs add.
- **Powers stay log-normal.** $X^a \sim \text{LogNormal}(a\mu, a^2\sigma^2)$ for $a \ne 0$;
  in particular $\frac{1}{X} \sim \text{LogNormal}(-\mu, \sigma^2)$.
- **A tail between light and heavy.** All moments $E[X^k] = e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}$ are
  finite, but they grow so fast that $E[e^{tX}]$ is infinite for every $t \gt 0$.
  The tail is heavier than any exponential (see [9.13](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Web Response Times

**Problem.** A web service's response time is log-normal with median 200 ms
and $\sigma = 0.5$ on the log scale. (a) Find $\mu$ and the mean response time.
(b) What fraction of requests take more than 500 ms? (c) What is the 99th
percentile?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The median is $e^{\mu} = 200$, so $\mu = \ln 200 \approx 5.298$. The mean is:

$$
E[X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2} = e^{\mu} \cdot e^{0.25/2} = 200 \times e^{0.125} \approx 200 \times 1.1331 = 226.6 \text{ ms}
$$

2. (b) Take logs and standardize:

$$
z = \frac{\ln 500 - \ln 200}{0.5} = \frac{\ln 2.5}{0.5} = \frac{0.9163}{0.5} = 1.8326, \qquad P(X \gt 500) = 1 - \Phi(1.8326) \approx 0.0334
$$

3. (c) The 99th percentile of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ is $2.3263$, so on the log scale the
   99th percentile is $\mu + 2.3263 \times 0.5$. Exponentiate:

$$
x_{0.99} = e^{\mu + 2.3263 \times 0.5} = 200 \times e^{1.16317} \approx 200 \times 3.2001 = 640.0 \text{ ms}
$$

**Answer.** (a) $\mu \approx 5.298$ and the mean is about 226.6 ms. (b) About
3.3% of requests are slower than 500 ms. (c) The 99th percentile is about 640 ms.

**Check.** The mean is above the median, as it must be for a log-normal. The
99th percentile is more than 3 times the median: this long tail is why
engineers watch "p99 latency", not just the average.

### Example 2: Investment Growth

**Problem.** You invest 1000 dollars. Each year the money is multiplied by
$e^{Y_i}$ with $Y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0.05, 0.04)$ independent (so $\sigma = 0.2$ per year). After
10 years, find the median and the mean of your wealth $W$, and the chance you
have lost money.

**Solution.**

1. $W = 1000 \thinspace e^{S}$ with $S = Y_1 + \dots + Y_{10}$. Means and variances of
   independent normals add, so $S \sim \mathcal{N}(0.5, 0.4)$.
2. Median and mean:

$$
\text{median} = 1000 \thinspace e^{0.5} \approx 1648.7, \qquad E[W] = 1000 \thinspace e^{0.5 + 0.4/2} = 1000 \thinspace e^{0.7} \approx 2013.8
$$

3. You lose money when $W \lt 1000$, that is when $S \lt 0$:

$$
P(S \lt 0) = \Phi\left( \frac{0 - 0.5}{\sqrt{0.4}} \right) = \Phi(-0.7906) \approx 0.2146
$$

**Answer.** The typical (median) result is about 1649 dollars, but the mean is
about 2014 dollars. About 21% of the time you end with less than you started.

**Check.** The mean is pulled up by a few very lucky paths. If you advertised
"average result 2014 dollars", most investors (more than half) would get less.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 1. numpy's `rng.lognormal(mean, sigma)` takes the
mean and the **standard deviation of the log**, not of $X$ itself.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
mu, sigma = np.log(200), 0.5

# numpy's mean and sigma describe ln(X): mean mu, standard deviation sigma.
x = rng.lognormal(mean=mu, sigma=sigma, size=n_trials)

print(f"median:      simulation {np.median(x):.2f}, exact {np.exp(mu):.2f}")
print(f"mean:        simulation {x.mean():.2f}, exact {np.exp(mu + sigma**2 / 2):.2f}")
print(f"P(X > 500):  simulation {np.mean(x > 500):.4f}, "
      f"exact {norm.sf(np.log(2.5) / sigma):.4f}")
print(f"99th pct:    simulation {np.quantile(x, 0.99):.1f}, "
      f"exact {np.exp(mu + sigma * norm.ppf(0.99)):.1f}")

# The same thing built by hand: exponentiate normal samples.
y = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=n_trials)
print(f"mean of exp(normal): simulation {np.exp(y).mean():.2f}, exact {np.exp(mu + sigma**2 / 2):.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
median:      simulation 199.92, exact 200.00
mean:        simulation 226.67, exact 226.63
P(X > 500):  simulation 0.0335, exact 0.0334
99th pct:    simulation 640.5, exact 640.0
mean of exp(normal): simulation 226.71, exact 226.63
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ as the mean and variance of $X$.** They belong to $\ln X$.
  $\text{LogNormal}(0, 1)$ has mean $e^{0.5} \approx 1.65$, not 0.
- **Using $e^{E[\ln X]}$ as the mean.** $e^{\mu}$ is the median. The mean is
  $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$, which is always larger. (This is
  [Jensen's inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md) at work.)
- **Forgetting the $\frac{1}{x}$ in the PDF.** The density is not just the normal
  density at $\ln x$; the change of variables adds $\frac{1}{x}$.
- **Passing a variance to numpy.** `rng.lognormal(mean, sigma)` wants the
  standard deviation of $\ln X$.
- **Summarizing skewed data by the mean only.** For incomes, house prices or
  latency, report the median and a high percentile too.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance:** stock prices in the Black-Scholes model follow geometric
  Brownian motion, so the price at a future date is log-normal
  ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Computer science:** file sizes, request latencies, session lengths and
  the time to finish a job are often close to log-normal.
- **AI and machine learning:** priors for positive quantities such as
  learning rates or length scales are often log-normal; random search tries
  learning rates "uniform on the log scale".
- **Economics and biology:** incomes (except the very top), city sizes,
  particle sizes, and the time from infection to symptoms.
- **Everyday life:** "most people earn less than the average" is a log-normal
  fact.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{LogNormal}(1, 0.36)$. Find the median and the mean of $X$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\mu = 1$ and $\sigma^2 = 0.36$:

$$\text{median} = e^{1} \approx 2.718, \qquad E[X] = e^{1 + 0.18} = e^{1.18} \approx 3.254$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For $X \sim \text{LogNormal}(0, 1)$, find $P(X \gt e)$ (where $e \approx 2.718$).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \gt e) = P(\ln X \gt 1) = 1 - \Phi(1) \approx 0.1587$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Show that if $X \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $3X^2$ is also
log-normal, and find its parameters.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take logs: $\ln(3X^2) = \ln 3 + 2 \ln X$. A linear function of a normal is normal:

$$\ln(3X^2) \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln 3 + 2\mu, \ 4\sigma^2)$$

So $3X^2 \sim \text{LogNormal}(\ln 3 + 2\mu, 4\sigma^2)$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Incomes in a town are log-normal with median 30,000 taka per
month and mean 40,000 taka. Find $\sigma^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The ratio of mean to median is $e^{\sigma^2/2}$:

$$e^{\sigma^2/2} = \frac{40000}{30000} = \frac{4}{3} \implies \sigma^2 = 2 \ln \tfrac{4}{3} \approx 2 \times 0.2877 = 0.5754$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that the coefficient of variation of a log-normal,
$\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}}{E[X]}$, equals $\sqrt{e^{\sigma^2} - 1}$, so it does not depend on $\mu$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{(E[X])^2} = \frac{(e^{\sigma^2} - 1) e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}}{e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}} = e^{\sigma^2} - 1$$

Take square roots. Changing $\mu$ only rescales $X$ (it multiplies $X$ by a constant), which does not change the relative spread.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2 by simulating 10 years of random growth

<summary>Answer</summary>


</details>

## Summary

- $X$ is log-normal when $\ln X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$; it comes from multiplying
  many small independent factors.
- PDF $\frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(\ln x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ and CDF
  $\Phi(\frac{\ln x - \mu}{\sigma})$ for $x \gt 0$.
- Median $e^{\mu}$, mean $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$, variance $(e^{\sigma^2} - 1)e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}$;
  mode $\lt$ median $\lt$ mean.
- In numpy: `rng.lognormal(mean=mu, sigma=sigma, size=n)`, where both describe $\ln X$
  and `sigma` is a standard deviation.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\ln X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \implies E[X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \quad \text{median} = e^{\mu}}
$$
