# 9.6 Chi-Square, Student's t and F

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.6 Chi-Square, Student's t and F

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [9.5 Beta](9.5-Beta.md)

> **Big idea:** Square and add standard normals and you get a chi-square; divide a normal by the square root of a scaled chi-square and you get a heavy-tailed Student's t; divide two scaled chi-squares and you get an F.

---

## Why It Matters

These three distributions are all built from normal random variables. They
answer questions such as "how far from the target does a random GPS error
land?" and "how unusual is an average when I had to estimate the noise from
only a few data points?" They are the backbone of classical statistics
(confidence intervals, t-tests, analysis of variance), and the Student's t is
a popular heavy-tailed model in machine learning. In this topic we only study
the distributions themselves; their use in statistics is covered in
[21.2 Statistics and Data Science](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md).

## Intuition

**Chi-square.** A drone tries to land on a target. Its error east-west and its
error north-south are independent standard normals, $Z_1$ and $Z_2$. The squared
distance from the target is $Z_1^2 + Z_2^2$. That squared distance is
always positive and usually small, with a long right tail. Add a third
direction (height) and you get $Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + Z_3^2$. The number of squared
normals you add is called the **degrees of freedom**.

**Student's t.** You measure something whose true spread you do not know, so
you estimate the spread from a few data points. Your estimate is itself
random: sometimes it is too small by luck. When it is too small, dividing by
it makes the ratio too big. So the ratio "normal divided by an estimated
spread" has **heavier tails** than a normal: extreme values happen more often.
With more data, the estimate of spread gets better and the t looks more and
more like a normal.

**F.** If you compare two estimated spreads by taking their ratio, the ratio
is near 1 when the spreads are equal, but it wobbles. That wobble is the F
distribution.

A Student's t has a lower peak and much fatter tails than the standard
normal; with more degrees of freedom it moves toward the normal curve.

## Definition

Throughout, $Z, Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ variables.

### The Chi-Square Distribution

For a whole number $k \ge 1$, the **chi-square distribution with $k$ degrees
of freedom**, written $\chi^2_k$, is the distribution of:

$$
V = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \dots + Z_k^2
$$

It is a special Gamma distribution, $\chi^2_k = \text{Gamma}(\frac{k}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$,
with shape $\frac{k}{2}$ and **rate** $\frac{1}{2}$. So its PDF, mean and variance are:

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{k/2 - 1} e^{-x/2}}{2^{k/2} \thinspace \Gamma(k/2)}, \quad x \gt 0, \qquad E[V] = k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(V) = 2k
$$

**In words:** a sum of $k$ squared standard normals has mean $k$ (each square
has mean 1) and variance $2k$.

With $k = 2$ it is $\text{Gamma}(1, \frac{1}{2}) = \text{Exp}(\frac{1}{2})$, so
$P(\chi^2_2 \gt x) = e^{-x/2}$ in closed form.

### Student's t Distribution

Let $V \sim \chi^2_{\nu}$ be independent of $Z$. The **Student's t distribution
with $\nu$ degrees of freedom** ($\nu$ is the Greek letter "nu"), written $t_{\nu}$, is
the distribution of:

$$
T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V / \nu}}
$$

Its PDF on the whole real line is:

$$
f(t) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu + 1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu \pi} \thinspace \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)} \left( 1 + \frac{t^2}{\nu} \right)^{-(\nu + 1)/2}
$$

and its mean and variance are:

$$
E[T] = 0 \ (\nu \gt 1), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(T) = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2} \ (\nu \gt 2)
$$

**In words:** a standard normal divided by a noisy estimate of its own scale;
it is symmetric about 0 and its variance is a bit more than 1.

For $\nu \le 2$ the variance is infinite, and for $\nu = 1$ even the mean does
not exist (that case is the [Cauchy distribution](9.9-Cauchy.md)). The CDF has
no simple closed form in general; for $\nu = 2$ it is
$F(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{t}{2\sqrt{2 + t^2}}$.

### The F Distribution

Let $U \sim \chi^2_{d_1}$ and $V \sim \chi^2_{d_2}$ be independent. The **F
distribution with $d_1$ and $d_2$ degrees of freedom**, written $F_{d_1, d_2}$,
is the distribution of:

$$
F = \frac{U / d_1}{V / d_2}
$$

Its PDF for $x \gt 0$ and its mean are:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{B\left(\frac{d_1}{2}, \frac{d_2}{2}\right)} \left( \frac{d_1}{d_2} \right)^{d_1/2} x^{d_1/2 - 1} \left( 1 + \frac{d_1 x}{d_2} \right)^{-(d_1 + d_2)/2}, \qquad E[F] = \frac{d_2}{d_2 - 2} \ (d_2 \gt 2)
$$

where $B$ is the [Beta function](9.5-Beta.md#the-beta-function). The variance,
for $d_2 \gt 4$, is $\frac{2 d_2^2 (d_1 + d_2 - 2)}{d_1 (d_2 - 2)^2 (d_2 - 4)}$.

**In words:** the ratio of two "average squared normals"; it is near 1 and
skewed to the right.

## Why It Is True

### One Squared Normal Is Gamma

Let $Y = Z^2$. For $y \gt 0$ use the CDF method
([13.1 CDF Method](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.1-CDF-Method.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_Y(y) &= P(Z^2 \le y) = P(-\sqrt{y} \le Z \le \sqrt{y}) && \text{(take square roots)} \\
&= \Phi(\sqrt{y}) - \Phi(-\sqrt{y}) = 2\Phi(\sqrt{y}) - 1 && \text{(symmetry of } Z \text{)} \\
f_Y(y) &= 2 \varphi(\sqrt{y}) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} y^{-1/2} e^{-y/2} && \text{(chain rule)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Compare with the $\text{Gamma}(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$ density
$\frac{(1/2)^{1/2}}{\Gamma(1/2)} y^{-1/2} e^{-y/2}$. Since $\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}) = \sqrt{\pi}$,
the constant is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\pi}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$. They match, so
$Z^2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$.

### Adding Them Up

Independent Gammas **with the same rate** add their shapes
([9.4 Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md#key-properties)). Here every $Z_i^2$ has rate $\frac{1}{2}$, so:

$$
Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2 \sim \text{Gamma}\left( \tfrac{1}{2} + \dots + \tfrac{1}{2}, \ \tfrac{1}{2} \right) = \text{Gamma}\left( \tfrac{k}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2} \right)
$$

The Gamma formulas then give the mean and variance:

$$
E[V] = \frac{k/2}{1/2} = k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(V) = \frac{k/2}{(1/2)^2} = 2k
$$

You can also see the variance directly: $E[Z^4] = 3$ for a standard normal, so
$\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) = 3 - 1 = 2$, and $k$ independent copies give $2k$.

### Mean and Variance of the t

$T$ is symmetric (replacing $Z$ by $-Z$ does not change its distribution), so
$E[T] = 0$ whenever the mean exists. For the variance, use independence of $Z$
and $V$ and the Gamma result $E[\frac{1}{X}] = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1}$ from
[9.4 Practice 5](9.4-Gamma.md#practice), with $\alpha = \frac{\nu}{2}$, $\beta = \frac{1}{2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[T^2] &= E\left[ \frac{\nu Z^2}{V} \right] = \nu \thinspace E[Z^2] \thinspace E\left[ \frac{1}{V} \right] && \text{(independence)} \\
&= \nu \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1/2}{\nu/2 - 1} = \nu \cdot \frac{1}{\nu - 2} = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2} && \text{(needs } \nu \gt 2 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Mean of the F

Again by independence, with $E[\frac{U}{d_1}] = \frac{d_1}{d_1} = 1$ and
$E[\frac{1}{V}] = \frac{1}{d_2 - 2}$:

$$
E[F] = E\left[ \frac{U}{d_1} \right] \cdot d_2 \thinspace E\left[ \frac{1}{V} \right] = 1 \cdot \frac{d_2}{d_2 - 2} = \frac{d_2}{d_2 - 2}
$$

The PDFs of $t$ and $F$ come from a two-variable change of variables
([13.3 Jacobian](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)),
so we do not derive them here.

### Key Properties

- **The t becomes normal.** As $\nu \to \infty$, $\frac{V}{\nu}$ is an average of
  $\nu$ squared normals, which tends to $1$ by the
  [Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md).
  So $t_{\nu} \to \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The 97.5% point of $t_{\nu}$ is $2.571$ for
  $\nu = 5$, $2.228$ for $\nu = 10$, $2.042$ for $\nu = 30$, and $1.960$ in the limit.
- **Heavy tails.** The t density decays like $\lvert t \rvert^{-(\nu + 1)}$, a power
  of $t$, while the normal decays like $e^{-t^2/2}$ (see
  [9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).
- **Links between them.** $T^2 \sim F_{1, \nu}$ when $T \sim t_{\nu}$, because
  $T^2 = \frac{Z^2 / 1}{V / \nu}$ and $Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$. Also, $\frac{1}{F} \sim F_{d_2, d_1}$.
- **Why statistics uses them.** For $n$ independent normal data points, the
  sample variance is a scaled $\chi^2_{n-1}$, and the standardized sample mean
  with the estimated spread is $t_{n-1}$. The details belong to statistics.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: How Far Does the Drone Land

**Problem.** A drone's landing error is $(X, Y)$ meters from the target, with $X$
and $Y$ independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 9)$, so $\sigma = 3$ m. (a) What is the chance
it lands more than 6 m from the target? (b) Within what radius does it land
95% of the time?

**Solution.**

1. Standardize: $\frac{X}{3}$ and $\frac{Y}{3}$ are standard normals, so the
   squared distance over $9$ is $\chi^2_2$:

$$
\frac{X^2 + Y^2}{9} = \left( \frac{X}{3} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{Y}{3} \right)^2 \sim \chi^2_2 = \text{Exp}\left( \tfrac{1}{2} \right)
$$

2. (a) The distance is more than 6 exactly when the squared distance is more
   than 36, that is when the chi-square is more than $\frac{36}{9} = 4$:

$$
P(\text{distance} \gt 6) = P(\chi^2_2 \gt 4) = e^{-4/2} = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353
$$

3. (b) We want $r$ with $P(\chi^2_2 \le \frac{r^2}{9}) = 0.95$, that is
   $e^{-r^2/18} = 0.05$:

$$
\frac{r^2}{18} = -\ln 0.05 \approx 2.9957 \implies r^2 \approx 53.92 \implies r \approx 7.34 \text{ m}
$$

**Answer.** (a) About 13.5% of landings are more than 6 m away. (b) 95% of
landings are within about 7.34 m.

**Check.** One direction alone has 95% within $1.96 \times 3 = 5.88$ m. In two
directions the errors combine, so the radius must be larger. It is.

### Example 2: How Heavy Are the Tails of t

**Problem.** Compare $P(\lvert T \rvert \gt 3)$ for $T \sim t_2$ with $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 3)$
for a standard normal. Use the closed-form CDF of $t_2$.

**Solution.**

1. The $t_2$ CDF is $F(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{t}{2\sqrt{2 + t^2}}$. At $t = 3$:

$$
F(3) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{2\sqrt{11}} \approx 0.5 + \frac{3}{6.6332} \approx 0.5 + 0.45227 = 0.95227
$$

2. By symmetry, both tails together:

$$
P(\lvert T \rvert \gt 3) = 2(1 - 0.95227) = 2 \times 0.04773 \approx 0.0955
$$

3. For the normal, from [9.2](9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md): $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 3) = 2(1 - 0.99865) \approx 0.0027$.

4. Compare: $\frac{0.0955}{0.0027} \approx 35$.

**Answer.** A $t_2$ value lands beyond 3 about 9.5% of the time, roughly 35
times as often as a normal value.

**Check.** With only 2 degrees of freedom, the estimated spread is very noisy,
so the tails should be much heavier. The variance of $t_2$ is even infinite.

## Simulate It

This code builds each distribution from standard normals, exactly as in the
definitions, and compares with the exact results. numpy also has
`rng.chisquare(df)`, `rng.standard_t(df)` and `rng.f(dfnum, dfden)`.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Chi-square with 2 df: sum of two squared standard normals.
v2 = rng.standard_normal(n) ** 2 + rng.standard_normal(n) ** 2
print(f"P(chi2_2 > 4): simulation {np.mean(v2 > 4):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-2):.4f}")

# Chi-square with 5 df: mean k = 5, variance 2k = 10.
v5 = (rng.standard_normal((n, 5)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
print(f"chi2_5 mean, var: simulation {v5.mean():.3f}, {v5.var():.3f}; exact 5, 10")

# Student t with 5 df: Z / sqrt(V/5); variance 5/3.
t5 = rng.standard_normal(n) / np.sqrt(v5 / 5)
print(f"t_5 variance: simulation {t5.var():.4f}, exact {5 / 3:.4f}")

# F with (4, 10) df: mean 10/8 = 1.25.
u4 = (rng.standard_normal((n, 4)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
v10 = (rng.standard_normal((n, 10)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
f = (u4 / 4) / (v10 / 10)
print(f"F_(4,10) mean: simulation {f.mean():.4f}, exact {10 / 8:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(chi2_2 > 4): simulation 0.1354, exact 0.1353
chi2_5 mean, var: simulation 4.997, 9.979; exact 5, 10
t_5 variance: simulation 1.6674, exact 1.6667
F_(4,10) mean: simulation 1.2488, exact 1.2500
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using a normal when the spread was estimated from few points.** With 5
  data points, the 97.5% point is $2.776$ (that is $t_4$), not $1.96$. Using $1.96$
  makes you far too confident.
- **Forgetting that the chi-square needs standard normals.** $X^2 + Y^2$ with
  $\sigma = 3$ is not $\chi^2_2$; first divide each by $\sigma$.
- **Assuming the t always has a variance.** $\operatorname{Var}(T) = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}$ only
  for $\nu \gt 2$. For $\nu \le 2$ the sample variance never settles down.
- **Mixing up the order of the F degrees of freedom.** $F_{d_1, d_2}$ has the
  numerator's degrees of freedom first. $F_{4, 10}$ and $F_{10, 4}$ are different.
- **Forgetting the independence assumption.** The t and F definitions need the
  top and bottom to be independent. Without it, the formulas fail.

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics:** confidence intervals and t-tests (Student's t), tests of
  variance and goodness of fit (chi-square), analysis of variance and
  regression tests (F). See
  [21.2 Statistics and Data Science](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md).
- **AI and machine learning:** the Student's t is a robust noise model that
  is not fooled by outliers; t-SNE uses a $t_1$ kernel to place points in a
  2-D picture; chi-square scores pick useful features in text classification.
- **Computer science:** checking whether a random number generator or a hash
  function spreads values evenly (a chi-square test).
- **Engineering and navigation:** the chi-square gives error circles and
  spheres for GPS and radar.
- **Everyday life:** "is this die fair?" can be checked with a chi-square
  test on the counts of each face.

<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\operatorname{Var}(t_6) = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(t_{30}) = \frac{30}{28} \approx 1.071$$

**3. (Medium)** Show that $\chi^2_2$ is the exponential distribution with rate
$\frac{1}{2}$, and find its median.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $k = 2$ the PDF is $\frac{x^0 e^{-x/2}}{2^1 \Gamma(1)} = \frac{1}{2} e^{-x/2}$, the $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{2})$ density. Its median is:

$$m = \frac{\ln 2}{1/2} = 2 \ln 2 \approx 1.386$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** If $T \sim t_5$, explain why $T^2 \sim F_{1, 5}$. The 97.5% point of $t_5$
is $2.571$; what is the 95% point of $F_{1, 5}$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$T^2 = \frac{Z^2}{V/5} = \frac{Z^2 / 1}{V / 5}$ with $Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$ independent of $V \sim \chi^2_5$, which is the definition of $F_{1, 5}$. Since $T$ is symmetric:

$$P(T^2 \gt 2.571^2) = P(\lvert T \rvert \gt 2.571) = 0.05$$

So the 95% point of $F_{1, 5}$ is $2.571^2 \approx 6.61$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $E[Z^4] = 3$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ using the Gamma
form of $Z^2$, and conclude $\operatorname{Var}(\chi^2_1) = 2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Z^2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, and for a Gamma $E[X^2] = \frac{\alpha(\alpha + 1)}{\beta^2}$:

$$E[Z^4] = E[(Z^2)^2] = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{3}{2}}{\tfrac{1}{4}} = \frac{3/4}{1/4} = 3$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) = E[Z^4] - (E[Z^2])^2 = 3 - 1 = 2$$

</details>

## Summary

- $\chi^2_k$ is a sum of $k$ squared standard normals; it equals
  $\text{Gamma}(\frac{k}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, with mean $k$ and variance $2k$.
- $t_{\nu} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}$ is a symmetric, heavy-tailed bell with variance
  $\frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}$; it tends to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ as $\nu$ grows, and $t_1$ is the Cauchy.
- $F_{d_1, d_2}$ is a ratio of two independent chi-squares, each divided by its
  degrees of freedom; its mean is $\frac{d_2}{d_2 - 2}$.
- In numpy: `rng.chisquare(k)`, `rng.standard_t(nu)`, `rng.f(d1, d2)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2 \sim \chi^2_k = \text{Gamma}\left( \tfrac{k}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2} \right), \qquad T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}} \sim t_{\nu}}
$$
