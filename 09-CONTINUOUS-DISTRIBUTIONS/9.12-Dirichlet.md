# 9.12 Dirichlet

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.12 Dirichlet

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [9.11 Weibull](9.11-Weibull.md)

> **Big idea:** The Dirichlet distribution is a distribution over probability vectors, the many-category version of the Beta, and you can build it by drawing independent Gammas and dividing each by their total.

---

## Why It Matters

A [Beta](9.5-Beta.md) describes uncertainty about one probability, such as a
coin's chance of heads. Often you have **many** categories: the six faces of a
loaded die, the share of each word in a document, the mix of topics in a news
article, the vote shares of five parties. The unknown is then a whole vector
of probabilities that must add up to 1. The **Dirichlet distribution** is the
standard way to put uncertainty on such a vector. It drives topic models
(LDA), Bayesian smoothing of word counts, and the exploration noise in game
programs such as AlphaZero.

## Intuition

A factory makes dice, and each die is slightly loaded in a different way. Pick
a die at random. Its six face probabilities $(p_1, \dots, p_6)$ are random, but
always non-negative and always add up to 1. The Dirichlet tells you how such a
random vector behaves.

Here is a simple way to make one. Pour some sand into six buckets, a random
amount into each, independently. Then divide each amount by the total. The
results are six non-negative shares that add up to 1. If the random amounts
are Gamma-distributed, the shares are exactly Dirichlet. A bucket that tends
to get more sand (a bigger shape parameter) gets a bigger share on average.

The parameters $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K$ act like pseudo-counts, as in the Beta.
Their **ratios** set the average shares. Their **total** sets how close each
random vector stays to that average: a large total means "all dice are nearly
the same", a small total means "dice are wildly different".

## Definition

The **probability simplex** with $K$ categories is the set of vectors whose
entries are non-negative and add up to 1:

$$
\Delta_K = \left\lbrace (x_1, \dots, x_K) : x_i \ge 0, \ x_1 + \dots + x_K = 1 \right\rbrace
$$

For $K = 3$ it is the triangle in the picture.

A random vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_K)$ has the **Dirichlet distribution** with
parameters $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K \gt 0$, written $\mathbf{X} \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$,
if its density on the simplex is:

$$
f(x_1, \dots, x_K) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})} \prod_{i=1}^{K} x_i^{\alpha_i - 1}, \qquad B(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_1) \cdots \Gamma(\alpha_K)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}, \qquad \alpha_0 = \alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_K
$$

where:

- each $\alpha_i$ is the parameter of category $i$ (think "pseudo-count");
- $\alpha_0$ is the total, called the **concentration**;
- $B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ is the **multivariate Beta function**, the number that makes the total
  probability 1;
- the support is the simplex $\Delta_K$. (Since $x_K = 1 - x_1 - \dots - x_{K-1}$, the density
  is really a density of the first $K - 1$ entries; joint densities are the
  topic of [Module 10](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/README.md).)

**In words:** a product of powers of the shares, one power per category,
scaled so that the total probability is 1.

Each entry, its mean and its variance:

$$
X_i \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \ \alpha_0 - \alpha_i), \qquad E[X_i] = \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_0}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\alpha_i (\alpha_0 - \alpha_i)}{\alpha_0^2 (\alpha_0 + 1)}
$$

**In words:** each share, on its own, is a Beta: "this category" against "all
the others together". Its average is its fraction of the pseudo-counts.

Two different entries move in opposite directions (if one share is big, the
others must be smaller):

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = -\frac{\alpha_i \alpha_j}{\alpha_0^2 (\alpha_0 + 1)} \qquad (i \ne j)
$$

**In words:** the covariance is always negative, because the shares compete
for a fixed total of 1. ([Covariance](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.1-Covariance.md) is studied in Module 11.)

## Why It Is True

### Building a Dirichlet From Gammas

Let $G_1, \dots, G_K$ be independent with $G_i \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_i, 1)$ (shape $\alpha_i$, rate 1),
and let $S = G_1 + \dots + G_K$. Then:

$$
\left( \frac{G_1}{S}, \dots, \frac{G_K}{S} \right) \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)
$$

**In words:** independent Gamma "amounts", divided by their total, give
Dirichlet "shares". This is the bucket-of-sand story, and it is how computers
sample a Dirichlet. The full proof is a $K$-variable change of variables
([13.3 Jacobian](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md));
for $K = 2$ it is exactly the Beta proof in
[9.5](9.5-Beta.md#why-it-is-true), so the Dirichlet with $K = 2$ is the Beta.

### Each Entry Is a Beta

Fix one category $i$ and let $R = S - G_i$ be the sum of the other Gammas. They
are independent with the same rate 1, so their shapes add
([9.4](9.4-Gamma.md#key-properties)): $R \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_0 - \alpha_i, 1)$, and $R$ is
independent of $G_i$. So:

$$
X_i = \frac{G_i}{G_i + R}, \qquad G_i \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_i, 1), \quad R \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_0 - \alpha_i, 1)
$$

By the Gamma-ratio fact from [9.5](9.5-Beta.md#why-it-is-true), $X_i \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \alpha_0 - \alpha_i)$.

### Mean and Variance

Plug $\alpha = \alpha_i$ and $\beta = \alpha_0 - \alpha_i$ into the Beta formulas; the sum
$\alpha + \beta$ is $\alpha_0$:

$$
E[X_i] = \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_i + (\alpha_0 - \alpha_i)} = \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_0}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\alpha_i (\alpha_0 - \alpha_i)}{\alpha_0^2 (\alpha_0 + 1)}
$$

### The Covariance

**Merging categories** gives another Dirichlet: $X_i + X_j = \frac{G_i + G_j}{S}$, and
$G_i + G_j \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_i + \alpha_j, 1)$, so $X_i + X_j \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i + \alpha_j, \alpha_0 - \alpha_i - \alpha_j)$.
Now use $\operatorname{Var}(X_i + X_j) = \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + \operatorname{Var}(X_j) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$
([11.3 Variance of a Sum](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)). Write
$c = \alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)$ and $a = \alpha_i$, $b = \alpha_j$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2c \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) &= (a + b)(\alpha_0 - a - b) - a(\alpha_0 - a) - b(\alpha_0 - b) && \text{(three Beta variances, times } c \text{)} \\
&= (a + b)\alpha_0 - (a + b)^2 - a\alpha_0 + a^2 - b\alpha_0 + b^2 && \text{(expand)} \\
&= -2ab && \text{(everything else cancels)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = -\frac{\alpha_i \alpha_j}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}$.

### Key Properties

- **Uniform on the simplex.** $\text{Dir}(1, \dots, 1)$ has a constant density: every
  probability vector is equally likely (left triangle in the picture).
- **Concentration.** With the ratios fixed, a bigger $\alpha_0$ shrinks every
  variance like $\frac{1}{\alpha_0 + 1}$, so draws crowd around the mean (middle
  triangle). If all $\alpha_i \lt 1$, draws are **sparse**: most of the mass sits on
  a few categories, near the corners and edges (right triangle).
- **Merging.** Adding entries adds their parameters:
  $(X_1 + X_2, X_3, \dots, X_K) \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \dots, \alpha_K)$.
- **Bayesian updating.** If your belief about category probabilities is
  $\text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$ and you observe counts $n_1, \dots, n_K$
  ([7.9 Multinomial](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.9-Multinomial.md)), your new belief is
  $\text{Dir}(\alpha_1 + n_1, \dots, \alpha_K + n_K)$. Why this works is in
  [10.7 Bayes for Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Three Market Shares

**Problem.** The market shares of three phone brands next year are modeled as
$(X_1, X_2, X_3) \sim \text{Dir}(2, 3, 5)$. Find (a) the mean share of each brand,
(b) the standard deviation of $X_1$, (c) $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_3)$, (d) $P(X_3 \gt 0.5)$,
(e) $P(X_1 \gt 0.3)$.

**Solution.**

1. The concentration is $\alpha_0 = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10$.
2. (a) The means are the pseudo-count fractions:

$$
E[X_1] = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2, \qquad E[X_2] = \frac{3}{10} = 0.3, \qquad E[X_3] = \frac{5}{10} = 0.5
$$

3. (b) The variance of $X_1$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_1) = \frac{2 \times 8}{10^2 \times 11} = \frac{16}{1100} \approx 0.014545, \qquad \sigma \approx 0.1206
$$

4. (c) The covariance:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_3) = -\frac{2 \times 5}{10^2 \times 11} = -\frac{10}{1100} \approx -0.00909
$$

5. (d) $X_3 \sim \text{Beta}(5, 10 - 5) = \text{Beta}(5, 5)$, which is symmetric about $0.5$, so
   $P(X_3 \gt 0.5) = 0.5$.
6. (e) $X_1 \sim \text{Beta}(2, 8)$. This is Example 2 of [9.5 Beta](9.5-Beta.md#example-2-how-good-is-the-new-ad),
   which gave $P(X_1 \gt 0.3) \approx 0.1960$.

**Answer.** (a) $0.2$, $0.3$, $0.5$. (b) About $0.121$. (c) About $-0.0091$. (d)
$0.5$. (e) About $0.196$.

**Check.** The three means add up to 1, as shares must. The covariance is
negative: when brand 1 does well, brand 3 tends to do worse.

### Example 2: Is This Die Loaded

**Problem.** You know nothing about a die, so your belief about its face
probabilities is $\text{Dir}(1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1)$. You roll it 30 times and get the
counts $(4, 6, 0, 5, 7, 8)$ for faces 1 to 6. Find your new belief, the
posterior mean for face 3 and face 6, and the standard deviation for face 6.

**Solution.**

1. Add the counts to the parameters:

$$
\text{Dir}(1 + 4, 1 + 6, 1 + 0, 1 + 5, 1 + 7, 1 + 8) = \text{Dir}(5, 7, 1, 6, 8, 9), \qquad \alpha_0 = 36
$$

2. Posterior means:

$$
E[p_3] = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.0278, \qquad E[p_6] = \frac{9}{36} = 0.25
$$

3. For face 6, $p_6 \sim \text{Beta}(9, 27)$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(p_6) = \frac{9 \times 27}{36^2 \times 37} = \frac{243}{47952} \approx 0.005068, \qquad \sigma \approx 0.0712
$$

**Answer.** The new belief is $\text{Dir}(5, 7, 1, 6, 8, 9)$. Face 3 gets about $0.028$
(not 0, even though it never appeared), and face 6 gets $0.25$, give or take
about $0.07$.

**Check.** The raw frequencies would be $\frac{0}{30}$ and $\frac{8}{30} \approx 0.267$. The
posterior means are $\frac{n_i + 1}{n + 6}$, pulled a little toward $\frac{1}{6}$. This is
exactly **add-one (Laplace) smoothing**, used in naive Bayes spam filters so
that a word never seen before does not get probability 0.

## Simulate It

This code samples $\text{Dir}(2, 3, 5)$ in two ways, with numpy's `rng.dirichlet` and by
normalizing Gammas, and checks Example 1.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
alpha = np.array([2.0, 3.0, 5.0])

# Way 1: numpy's Dirichlet sampler.
x = rng.dirichlet(alpha, size=n_trials)

# Way 2: independent Gamma(alpha_i, rate 1) amounts, divided by their total.
# numpy's gamma takes scale = 1/rate, which is 1 here.
g = rng.gamma(shape=alpha, scale=1.0, size=(n_trials, 3))
y = g / g.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

print(f"means (numpy):  {x.mean(axis=0).round(4)}, exact {alpha / alpha.sum()}")
print(f"means (gammas): {y.mean(axis=0).round(4)}")
print(f"SD(X1):         simulation {x[:, 0].std():.4f}, exact {np.sqrt(16 / 1100):.4f}")
print(f"Cov(X1, X3):    simulation {np.cov(x[:, 0], x[:, 2])[0, 1]:.5f}, exact {-10 / 1100:.5f}")
print(f"P(X1 > 0.3):    simulation {np.mean(x[:, 0] > 0.3):.4f}, exact 0.1960")
print(f"rows sum to 1:  {np.allclose(x.sum(axis=1), 1)}")
```

Output:

```text
means (numpy):  [0.2001 0.3001 0.4998], exact [0.2 0.3 0.5]
means (gammas): [0.1999 0.3    0.5001]
SD(X1):         simulation 0.1208, exact 0.1206
Cov(X1, X3):    simulation -0.00910, exact -0.00909
P(X1 > 0.3):    simulation 0.1967, exact 0.1960
rows sum to 1:  True
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking the entries are independent.** They must add up to 1, so they are
  always negatively related. You cannot sample each share separately.
- **Normalizing uniforms instead of Gammas.** Dividing independent
  $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ numbers by their sum does **not** give $\text{Dir}(1, \dots, 1)$. Use
  $\text{Gamma}(1, 1) = \text{Exp}(1)$ amounts instead.
- **Ignoring the concentration.** $\text{Dir}(1, 1, 1)$ and $\text{Dir}(100, 100, 100)$ have the
  same mean $(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3})$ but look completely different.
- **Expecting small $\alpha_i$ to give "even" vectors.** Parameters below 1 push the
  draws to the corners: most vectors put almost all their mass on one or two
  categories.
- **Forgetting the Beta link.** You rarely need the full density. For one share,
  use $X_i \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \alpha_0 - \alpha_i)$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) gives each
  document a Dirichlet mix of topics; Dirichlet priors smooth word counts in
  naive Bayes and n-gram language models
  ([21.1.1 Naive Bayes](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md));
  AlphaZero adds Dirichlet noise to its move probabilities to keep exploring.
- **Reinforcement learning and bandits:** Thompson sampling with many outcomes
  keeps a Dirichlet belief for each action.
- **Statistics:** Bayesian analysis of categorical data and mixture models
  (the mixing weights of [10.8 Mixture Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)).
- **Science:** compositions such as the share of each mineral in a rock, each
  species in an ecosystem, or each nutrient in a diet.
- **Testing software:** random probability vectors to test code that takes a
  probability distribution as input.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $\mathbf{X} \sim \text{Dir}(1, 1, 2)$. Find $E[X_3]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\alpha_0 = 4$:

$$E[X_3] = \frac{2}{4} = 0.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_3) = \frac{2 \times 2}{4^2 \times 5} = \frac{4}{80} = 0.05$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Show that $\text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ with two categories is the same as
$X_1 \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$, with $X_2 = 1 - X_1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $K = 2$, $x_2 = 1 - x_1$, and the density becomes:

$$f = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\Gamma(\alpha_2)} x_1^{\alpha_1 - 1} (1 - x_1)^{\alpha_2 - 1} = \frac{x_1^{\alpha_1 - 1}(1 - x_1)^{\alpha_2 - 1}}{B(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)}$$

which is the $\text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ density.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $\mathbf{X} \sim \text{Dir}(1, 2, 3, 4)$. What is the distribution of $X_1 + X_2$, and
what is $P(X_1 + X_2 \gt \frac{1}{2})$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Merging adds parameters: $X_1 + X_2 \sim \text{Beta}(1 + 2, 3 + 4) = \text{Beta}(3, 7)$. With the binomial link from [9.5](9.5-Beta.md), $n = 3 + 7 - 1 = 9$:

$$P(X_1 + X_2 \le \tfrac{1}{2}) = P(\text{Bin}(9, 0.5) \ge 3) = 1 - \frac{1 + 9 + 36}{512} = \frac{466}{512}$$

So $P(X_1 + X_2 \gt \frac{1}{2}) = \frac{46}{512} \approx 0.0898$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that $\operatorname{Var}(X_1) + \sum_{j \ne 1} \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_j) = 0$.
Why must this be true?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Multiply everything by $\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)$:

$$\alpha_1(\alpha_0 - \alpha_1) - \sum_{j \ne 1} \alpha_1 \alpha_j = \alpha_1(\alpha_0 - \alpha_1) - \alpha_1(\alpha_0 - \alpha_1) = 0$$

It must be true because the expression is $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_1 + \dots + X_K) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, 1) = 0$: the total never changes.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that $\text{Dir}(1, 1, 1)$ is uniform on the triangle, and find
$P(X_1 \gt \frac{1}{2})$ for it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With all $\alpha_i = 1$ every power is $x_i^0 = 1$, so the density is the constant $\frac{1}{B} = \frac{\Gamma(3)}{\Gamma(1)^3} = 2$: uniform. For one entry, $X_1 \sim \text{Beta}(1, 2)$ with density $2(1 - x)$:

$$P(X_1 \gt \tfrac{1}{2}) = \int_{1/2}^{1} 2(1 - x) \thinspace dx = \left[ -(1 - x)^2 \right]_{1/2}^{1} = 0 + \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.25$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Show by simulation that normalizing uniforms is **not** the
same as $\text{Dir}(1, 1, 1)$: compare $P(X_1 \gt \frac{1}{2})$ for both.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
u = rng.random((n, 3))
wrong = u / u.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)        # normalized uniforms
right = rng.dirichlet([1, 1, 1], size=n)        # true Dir(1, 1, 1)
print(f"normalized uniforms: P(X1 > 0.5) = {np.mean(wrong[:, 0] > 0.5):.4f}")
print(f"Dirichlet(1, 1, 1):  P(X1 > 0.5) = {np.mean(right[:, 0] > 0.5):.4f}, exact 0.2500")
```

Output:

```text
normalized uniforms: P(X1 > 0.5) = 0.1673
Dirichlet(1, 1, 1):  P(X1 > 0.5) = 0.2493, exact 0.2500
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$ is a distribution over probability vectors on the simplex.
- Density $\propto \prod x_i^{\alpha_i - 1}$; with $K = 2$ it is the Beta.
- Make it by normalizing independent $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_i, 1)$ amounts.
- Each entry is $\text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \alpha_0 - \alpha_i)$, with mean $\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_0}$; entries have
  negative covariance; $\alpha_0$ controls how concentrated the draws are.
- Observing counts $n_i$ turns $\text{Dir}(\alpha_i)$ into $\text{Dir}(\alpha_i + n_i)$.
- In numpy: `rng.dirichlet(alpha, size=n)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X_i] = \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_0}, \qquad X_i \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \ \alpha_0 - \alpha_i)}
$$
