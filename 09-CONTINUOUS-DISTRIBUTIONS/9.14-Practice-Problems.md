## How to Use This Set

- Try each problem on paper before opening the answer. Write down **which
  distribution** you use and **what each parameter means** (rate or scale?
  variance or standard deviation?) before computing anything.
- Each problem is labelled with the topic it uses, so you can go back and
  reread that file if you get stuck.
- Warm-Up problems check the basic formulas. Core problems need one idea plus
  careful arithmetic. Challenge problems ask for proofs.
- Do at least one Simulation Project. Writing the code is the best way to
  catch parameterization mistakes, especially numpy's use of the **scale**
  $\frac{1}{\text{rate}}$ for the exponential and the Gamma.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy, [9.1 Uniform](9.1-Uniform.md))** $X \sim \text{Unif}(-1, 3)$. Find $P(X \gt 0)$, $E[X]$
and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The interval has length $3 - (-1) = 4$, and the piece $(0, 3]$ has length 3:

$$P(X \gt 0) = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75$$

$$E[X] = \frac{-1 + 3}{2} = 1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{4^2}{12} = \frac{16}{12} \approx 1.333$$

</details>

**W2. (Easy, [9.2 Normal](9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md))** Adult heights are $\mathcal{N}(170, 49)$ in cm. What
fraction of adults are taller than 180 cm?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The variance is 49, so $\sigma = 7$. Standardize:

$$z = \frac{180 - 170}{7} \approx 1.4286, \qquad P(H \gt 180) = 1 - \Phi(1.4286) \approx 0.0766$$

About 7.7% of adults are taller than 180 cm.

</details>

**W3. (Easy, [9.3 Exponential](9.3-Exponential.md))** Buses on a busy route come at random at rate
$0.5$ per minute. Find the mean wait, the median wait, and $P(\text{wait} \gt 3 \text{ minutes})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The wait is $\text{Exp}(0.5)$, with rate $0.5$:

$$E[T] = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2, \qquad \text{median} = \frac{\ln 2}{0.5} \approx 1.386, \qquad P(T \gt 3) = e^{-0.5 \times 3} = e^{-1.5} \approx 0.2231$$

</details>

**W4. (Easy, [9.5 Beta](9.5-Beta.md))** Show that $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ is $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$, then find the
mean and variance of $\text{Beta}(3, 3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$B(1, 1) = \frac{\Gamma(1)\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(2)} = 1$, so the density is $x^0(1 - x)^0 = 1$ on $(0, 1)$: the uniform. For $\text{Beta}(3, 3)$:

$$E[X] = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{3 \times 3}{6^2 \times 7} = \frac{9}{252} = \frac{1}{28} \approx 0.0357$$

</details>

**W5. (Easy, [9.8 Log-Normal](9.8-Log-Normal.md))** $X \sim \text{LogNormal}(2, 0.5)$. Find the median and
the mean of $X$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\mu = 2$ and $\sigma^2 = 0.5$:

$$\text{median} = e^{2} \approx 7.389, \qquad E[X] = e^{2 + 0.5/2} = e^{2.25} \approx 9.488$$

The mean is above the median, as always for a log-normal.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium, [9.3 Exponential](9.3-Exponential.md))** Show that the exponential distribution is
memoryless. Then use it: a light bulb's life is $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{1000})$ in hours.
It has already worked for 1500 hours. What is the chance it works 500 more?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $s, t \ge 0$, the event $X \gt s + t$ is inside $X \gt s$, so:

$$P(X \gt s + t \mid X \gt s) = \frac{P(X \gt s + t)}{P(X \gt s)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s + t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(X \gt t)$$

For the bulb, the 1500 hours do not matter:

$$P(X \gt 2000 \mid X \gt 1500) = P(X \gt 500) = e^{-500/1000} = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065$$

</details>

**C2. (Medium, [9.4 Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md))** Customers arrive at a shop at rate 3 per hour. What
is the distribution of the arrival time of the 4th customer? Find its mean and
the probability that the 4th customer arrives within the first hour.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The 4th arrival time is a sum of 4 independent $\text{Exp}(3)$ waits, so $T \sim \text{Gamma}(4, 3)$ with mean $\frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333$ hours. $T \le 1$ means at least 4 arrivals in one hour, where the count is $\text{Pois}(3)$:

$$P(T \le 1) = 1 - e^{-3}\left(1 + 3 + \frac{9}{2} + \frac{27}{6}\right) = 1 - 13e^{-3} \approx 1 - 0.6472 = 0.3528$$

</details>

**C3. (Medium, [9.2 Normal](9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md))** A machine fills bottles with
$\mathcal{N}(500, 16)$ ml of juice, independently. A pack holds 4 bottles. What is the
chance that a pack holds less than 1990 ml in total?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Means and variances of independent normals add: the total is $\mathcal{N}(2000, 64)$, so $\sigma = 8$:

$$P(\text{total} \lt 1990) = \Phi\left( \frac{1990 - 2000}{8} \right) = \Phi(-1.25) \approx 0.1056$$

Note that the standard deviation of the total is $8$, not $4 \times 4 = 16$.

</details>

**C4. (Medium, [9.11 Weibull](9.11-Weibull.md))** A pump's lifetime is $\text{Weibull}(2, 1000)$ in hours
(shape 2, scale 1000). Find $P(\text{life} \gt 500)$ and the mean life. Does the pump
age?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \gt 500) = e^{-(500/1000)^2} = e^{-0.25} \approx 0.7788, \qquad E[X] = 1000 \thinspace \Gamma(1.5) = 1000 \times 0.8862 \approx 886.2$$

The shape is $k = 2 \gt 1$, so the hazard $h(x) = \frac{2x}{1000^2}$ grows with age: yes, the pump wears out.

</details>

**C5. (Medium, [9.9 Cauchy](9.9-Cauchy.md))** For $X \sim \text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$, find $P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 10)$ and
compare it with $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 10)$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Then explain in one or two
sentences why averaging 100 Cauchy measurements does not help.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 10) = 1 - \frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctan}(10) \approx 0.0635, \qquad P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 10) \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-23}$$

The Cauchy has no mean, so the Law of Large Numbers does not apply. In fact the average of 100 iid $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$ values is again $\text{Cauchy}(0, 1)$, exactly as spread out as a single value.

</details>

**C6. (Medium, [9.5 Beta](9.5-Beta.md))** Your belief about a new drug's success rate is
$\text{Beta}(2, 2)$. In a trial, 8 of 10 patients improve. What is your new belief, its
mean and its standard deviation?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Add successes to $\alpha$ and failures to $\beta$: $\text{Beta}(2 + 8, 2 + 2) = \text{Beta}(10, 4)$.

$$E[p] = \frac{10}{14} \approx 0.714, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(p) = \frac{10 \times 4}{14^2 \times 15} = \frac{40}{2940} \approx 0.01361, \qquad \sigma \approx 0.1167$$

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, proof, [9.4 Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md))** Prove that the sum $S_n$ of $n$ independent
$\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ variables is $\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$, this time using the Poisson
process: the number of events by time $t$ is $\text{Pois}(\lambda t)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$S_n$ is the time of the $n$-th event. It is later than $t$ exactly when fewer than $n$ events happen in $[0, t]$:

$$P(S_n \gt t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}$$

Differentiate in $t$ and change the sign to get the density. The derivative of $e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^k/k!$ is $\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} - \lambda e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}$ (the first part is 0 when $k = 0$). Summing over $k = 0, \dots, n-1$, the terms cancel in pairs (telescope) and only the last negative term survives:

$$f_{S_n}(t) = -\frac{d}{dt} P(S_n \gt t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} = \frac{\lambda^n}{\Gamma(n)} t^{n-1} e^{-\lambda t}$$

This is the $\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$ density, since $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, proof, [9.1 Uniform](9.1-Uniform.md) and [9.5 Beta](9.5-Beta.md))** Let $M$ be the maximum of $n$
independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ values. Prove that $M \sim \text{Beta}(n, 1)$ and find $E[M]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$M \le x$ exactly when all $n$ values are at most $x$. By independence, for $0 \le x \le 1$:

$$F_M(x) = x^n, \qquad f_M(x) = n x^{n-1}$$

$\text{Beta}(n, 1)$ has density $\frac{x^{n-1}(1 - x)^0}{B(n, 1)}$ with $B(n, 1) = \frac{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(n + 1)} = \frac{1}{n}$, so it is $n x^{n-1}$: the same. Hence:

$$E[M] = \frac{n}{n + 1}$$

With 9 values, the maximum is $0.9$ on average.

</details>

**H3. (Hard, proof, [9.10 Logistic and Gumbel](9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md))** Use the fact that
$G_1 - G_2 \sim \text{Logistic}(0, 1)$ for independent standard Gumbels to prove the
Gumbel-max trick for two categories: $P(\ln p_1 + G_1 \gt \ln p_2 + G_2) = p_1$ when
$p_1 + p_2 = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The event is $G_1 - G_2 \gt \ln p_2 - \ln p_1$. With $D = G_1 - G_2$ logistic, $P(D \gt d) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-d}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{d}}$. Put $d = \ln \frac{p_2}{p_1}$:

$$P(D \gt d) = \frac{1}{1 + p_2/p_1} = \frac{p_1}{p_1 + p_2} = p_1$$

</details>

**H4. (Hard, proof, [9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md))** Prove that if
$X \sim \text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)$, then $\ln(X/x_m) \sim \text{Exp}(\alpha)$. Use this to give a way to
sample a Pareto from one uniform random number.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \ge 0$:

$$P\left(\ln \frac{X}{x_m} \gt y\right) = P(X \gt x_m e^{y}) = \left(\frac{x_m}{x_m e^{y}}\right)^{\alpha} = e^{-\alpha y}$$

This is the survival function of $\text{Exp}(\alpha)$ (rate $\alpha$). So a power-law tail on the original scale is an exponential tail on the log scale. To sample: take $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, set $E = -\frac{\ln U}{\alpha} \sim \text{Exp}(\alpha)$, and return:

$$X = x_m e^{E} = x_m U^{-1/\alpha}$$

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**P1. ([9.9 Cauchy](9.9-Cauchy.md))** Simulate one million standard Cauchy values and
watch the running mean and the running median. Compare with standard normal
values. Which estimate of the center settles down?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
cauchy = rng.standard_cauchy(1_000_000)
normal = rng.standard_normal(1_000_000)

for n in [100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    c, z = cauchy[:n], normal[:n]
    print(f"n = {n:>9,}: Cauchy mean {c.mean():7.3f}, median {np.median(c):7.4f}")
    print(f"{'':13s}normal mean {z.mean():7.3f}, median {np.median(z):7.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =       100: Cauchy mean  -0.313, median  0.2389
             normal mean  -0.121, median  0.0065
n =    10,000: Cauchy mean  -0.301, median  0.0349
             normal mean  -0.008, median -0.0182
n = 1,000,000: Cauchy mean  -0.387, median  0.0018
             normal mean   0.001, median  0.0007
```

The Cauchy mean keeps jumping, but the Cauchy median settles near the center 0, just like both normal estimates. For heavy-tailed data, use the median.

</details>

**P2. ([9.3 Exponential](9.3-Exponential.md) and [9.4 Gamma](9.4-Gamma.md))** Build a Poisson process with
rate $\lambda = 2$ by adding exponential gaps. Check that (a) the number of events
in $[0, 1]$ has mean and variance 2, and $P(\text{0 events}) = e^{-2}$; (b) the time of the
3rd event has mean $1.5$ and $P(T_3 \le 1) = 1 - 5e^{-2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_runs, rate = 200_000, 2.0

# 30 gaps per run is plenty: more than 30 events in [0, 1] is essentially impossible.
gaps = rng.exponential(scale=1 / rate, size=(n_runs, 30))   # numpy: scale = 1/rate
times = np.cumsum(gaps, axis=1)                             # event times

count = (times <= 1.0).sum(axis=1)                          # events in [0, 1]
print(f"count mean, var: {count.mean():.4f}, {count.var():.4f} (exact 2, 2)")
print(f"P(0 events):     {np.mean(count == 0):.4f} (exact {np.exp(-2):.4f})")

t3 = times[:, 2]                                            # 3rd event time
print(f"E[T3]:           {t3.mean():.4f} (exact 1.5000)")
print(f"P(T3 <= 1):      {np.mean(t3 <= 1):.4f} (exact {1 - 5 * np.exp(-2):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
count mean, var: 2.0005, 1.9949 (exact 2, 2)
P(0 events):     0.1350 (exact 0.1353)
E[T3]:           1.4992 (exact 1.5000)
P(T3 <= 1):      0.3237 (exact 0.3233)
```

</details>
