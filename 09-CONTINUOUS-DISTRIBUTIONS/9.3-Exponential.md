# 9.3 Exponential

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.3 Exponential

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [9.2 Normal (Gaussian)](9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)

> **Big idea:** The exponential distribution is the waiting time until the next random event when events happen at a steady rate, and it is the only continuous distribution with no memory of how long you have already waited.

---

## Why It Matters

How long until the next request hits your server? Until the next customer
walks in, the next earthquake, the next decay of a radioactive atom? When
events happen at random at a constant average rate, the waiting time is
exponential. It is the building block of queueing theory, reliability,
survival analysis and the
[Poisson process](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.5-Poisson-Process.md), and
it leads straight to the [Gamma distribution](9.4-Gamma.md).

## Intuition

A web server receives on average 6 requests per minute, that is $0.1$ per
second, and the requests arrive independently of each other. You start a
stopwatch. How long until the next request?

In any short second, there is about a 10% chance that a request comes. To
wait more than 20 seconds, you need "no request" in each of those seconds.
Nothing that happened before matters, because the requests are independent.
Long waits are possible but rare, and each extra second of waiting cuts the
chance by the same factor. A quantity that shrinks by the same factor at each
step is an **exponential decay**, and that gives the distribution its name.

There is a surprising consequence. Suppose you have already waited 30 seconds
with no request. Is the next request now "due"? No. The server does not know
you have been waiting. The extra wait has exactly the same distribution as a
fresh wait. This is called being **memoryless**.


The density starts at height $\lambda$ and decays toward zero; the long right
tail pulls the mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ above the median $\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$.

## Definition

A random variable $X$ has the **exponential distribution** with **rate**
$\lambda \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, if its PDF is:

$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\lambda e^{-\lambda x} & \text{if } x \ge 0 \\
0 & \text{if } x \lt 0
\end{cases}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the **rate**: the average number of events per unit of
time. The support is $[0, \infty)$. Here $\lambda$ is the rate, so the mean is
$\frac{1}{\lambda}$: 6 requests per minute means an average wait of
$\frac{1}{6}$ minute.

**In words:** the density starts at $\lambda$ and shrinks by the factor
$e^{-\lambda}$ for every unit of time.

The CDF and the **survival function** (the chance of lasting longer than $x$)
have simple closed forms:

$$
F(x) = P(X \le x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad P(X \gt x) = e^{-\lambda x} \qquad (x \ge 0)
$$

**In words:** the chance that you are still waiting at time $x$ decays
exponentially.

The mean, variance and median are:

$$
E[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}, \qquad \text{median} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}
$$

**In words:** the mean wait is one over the rate, and the standard deviation
equals the mean.

## Why It Is True

**Where the formula comes from.** Events arrive at rate $\lambda$, so the
number of events in a window of length $t$ is $\text{Pois}(\lambda t)$
([7.5 Poisson](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.5-Poisson.md)). The wait $T$ for
the first event is longer than $t$ exactly when the window has zero events:

$$
P(T \gt t) = P(\text{0 events in } [0, t]) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^0}{0!} = e^{-\lambda t}
$$

So $F(t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$, and the PDF is its derivative,
$f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$. The total area is $F(\infty) = 1$.

**The mean.** Integrate by parts with $u = x$ and $dv = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} dx$,
so $v = -e^{-\lambda x}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \int_0^{\infty} x \thinspace \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \Big[ -x e^{-\lambda x} \Big]_0^{\infty} + \int_0^{\infty} e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx && \text{(integration by parts)} \\
&= 0 + \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\lambda} && \text{(}e^{-\lambda x}\text{ beats } x \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The second moment.** Same trick with $u = x^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^2] &= \int_0^{\infty} x^2 \thinspace \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= \Big[ -x^2 e^{-\lambda x} \Big]_0^{\infty} + \int_0^{\infty} 2x \thinspace e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx && \text{(integration by parts)} \\
&= 0 + \frac{2}{\lambda} \int_0^{\infty} x \thinspace \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx = \frac{2}{\lambda} E[X] = \frac{2}{\lambda^2} && \text{(spot } E[X] \text{ inside)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The variance.**

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \frac{2}{\lambda^2} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}
$$

**The median.** Solve $F(m) = \frac{1}{2}$: $1 - e^{-\lambda m} = \frac{1}{2}$, so
$e^{-\lambda m} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $m = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$.

### Memorylessness

A waiting time $X$ is **memoryless** if, for all $s, t \ge 0$:

$$
P(X \gt s + t \mid X \gt s) = P(X \gt t)
$$

**In words:** if you have already waited $s$, the chance of waiting $t$ more is
the same as the chance of waiting $t$ from the start.

**Proof for the exponential.** The event $X \gt s + t$ is inside the event
$X \gt s$, so their intersection is just $X \gt s + t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X \gt s + t \mid X \gt s) &= \frac{P(X \gt s + t)}{P(X \gt s)} && \text{(definition of conditional probability)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-\lambda (s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} && \text{(survival function)} \\
&= e^{-\lambda t} = P(X \gt t) && \text{(cancel } e^{-\lambda s} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The exponential is the **only** continuous memoryless distribution. Its
discrete cousin is the [geometric distribution](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md).

<details>
<summary>Why it is the only one (optional)</summary>

Let $G(t) = P(X \gt t)$. Memorylessness says $G(s + t) = G(s) \thinspace G(t)$ for all $s, t \ge 0$. Then $G(2) = G(1)^2$, $G(n) = G(1)^n$, $G(1/n)^n = G(1)$, and so $G(q) = G(1)^q$ for every positive fraction $q$. Since $G$ is a survival function, it is decreasing and continuous from the right, so $G(t) = G(1)^t$ for every $t \ge 0$. Writing $G(1) = e^{-\lambda}$ gives $G(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, the exponential.

</details>

### More Key Properties

- **Constant hazard.** The **hazard rate** $h(x) = \frac{f(x)}{P(X \gt x)}$ is the
  "chance per unit time of failing right now, given you are still alive". For
  the exponential, $h(x) = \frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda x}}{e^{-\lambda x}} = \lambda$. It
  never ages. ([9.11 Weibull](9.11-Weibull.md) allows aging.)
- **The minimum of exponentials is exponential.** If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are
  independent with $X_i \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$, then
  $\min(X_1, \dots, X_n) \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n)$, because
  $P(\min \gt t) = \prod_i e^{-\lambda_i t} = e^{-(\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n) t}$.
  Rates of competing events add.
- **Scaling.** If $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and $c \gt 0$, then $cX \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda / c)$.
  Changing seconds to minutes changes the rate, not the shape.
- **Easy to sample.** If $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, then $X = -\frac{\ln U}{\lambda} \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$.
  Here is why: $P(X \gt x) = P(\ln U \lt -\lambda x) = P(U \lt e^{-\lambda x}) = e^{-\lambda x}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Requests to a Server

**Problem.** A server receives requests at rate $\lambda = 0.1$ per second. Let $T$
be the time to the next request. Find (a) the mean and standard deviation of
$T$, (b) $P(T \gt 20)$, (c) $P(T \le 5)$, (d) the median of $T$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) Mean and standard deviation:

$$
E[T] = \frac{1}{0.1} = 10 \text{ seconds}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{0.1^2}} = 10 \text{ seconds}
$$

2. (b) Survival function at 20:

$$
P(T \gt 20) = e^{-0.1 \times 20} = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353
$$

3. (c) CDF at 5:

$$
P(T \le 5) = 1 - e^{-0.1 \times 5} = 1 - e^{-0.5} \approx 1 - 0.6065 = 0.3935
$$

4. (d) Median:

$$
m = \frac{\ln 2}{0.1} \approx \frac{0.6931}{0.1} = 6.931 \text{ seconds}
$$

**Answer.** The average wait is 10 seconds, but half of all waits are shorter
than about 6.9 seconds. A wait over 20 seconds happens about 13.5% of the time.

**Check.** The median is below the mean, as expected for a distribution with a
long right tail. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Radioactive Decay

**Problem.** A carbon-14 atom decays after an exponential time with a **half-life**
(median) of 5730 years. (a) Find the rate $\lambda$. (b) What is the chance that
an atom survives 10,000 years? (c) An atom has already survived 5000 years.
What is the chance it survives 5000 more?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The median is $\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = 5730$, so:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx \frac{0.6931}{5730} \approx 0.000121 \text{ per year}
$$

2. (b) Use the survival function. Since $e^{-\lambda t} = e^{-(\ln 2) t / 5730} = 2^{-t/5730}$:

$$
P(T \gt 10000) = 2^{-10000/5730} = 2^{-1.745} \approx 0.2983
$$

3. (c) By memorylessness, the past 5000 years do not matter:

$$
P(T \gt 10000 \mid T \gt 5000) = P(T \gt 5000) = 2^{-5000/5730} = 2^{-0.8726} \approx 0.5462
$$

**Answer.** (a) About $0.000121$ per year. (b) About 29.8%. (c) About 54.6%,
exactly the same as for a brand-new atom.

**Check.** 10,000 years is a bit less than two half-lives, so the survival
chance should be a bit more than $\frac{1}{4}$. It is $0.298$. Atoms do not age,
which is why carbon dating works.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 1 and the memoryless property. Note that numpy's
`rng.exponential` takes the **scale** $= \frac{1}{\lambda}$ (the mean), not the rate.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
rate = 0.1   # requests per second

# numpy uses scale = 1/rate (the mean wait), NOT the rate itself.
t = rng.exponential(scale=1 / rate, size=n_trials)

print(f"E[T]:      simulation {t.mean():.3f}, exact {1 / rate:.3f}")
print(f"P(T > 20): simulation {np.mean(t > 20):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-2):.4f}")
print(f"median:    simulation {np.median(t):.3f}, exact {np.log(2) / rate:.3f}")

# Memoryless: having waited 30 s, P(wait 20 s more) equals P(T > 20).
waited = t[t > 30]
print(f"P(T > 50 | T > 30): simulation {np.mean(waited > 50):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-2):.4f}")

# Inverse transform: -ln(U)/rate is also Exp(rate).
u = rng.random(n_trials)
print(f"mean of -ln(U)/rate: simulation {np.mean(-np.log(u) / rate):.3f}, exact {1 / rate:.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[T]:      simulation 9.998, exact 10.000
P(T > 20): simulation 0.1354, exact 0.1353
median:    simulation 6.927, exact 6.931
P(T > 50 | T > 30): simulation 0.1351, exact 0.1353
mean of -ln(U)/rate: simulation 9.999, exact 10.000
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Passing the rate to numpy.** `rng.exponential(0.1)` has mean $0.1$, so it is
  $\text{Exp}(10)$, not $\text{Exp}(0.1)$. Pass `scale=1/rate`.
- **Mixing up rate and mean.** In this book $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ uses the rate:
  $\text{Exp}(2)$ has mean $\frac{1}{2}$. Some books write the mean instead.
  Always check.
- **Thinking an event is "due".** After a long wait, the next event is not
  closer. The gambler's fallacy for waiting times is still a fallacy (see
  [22.5 Gambler's Fallacy](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md)).
- **Using the exponential for things that age.** Car tires and human bodies
  wear out, so their hazard rises with age. Memorylessness is wrong for them;
  use a [Weibull](9.11-Weibull.md).
- **Confusing the mean with the typical wait.** The median $\frac{0.693}{\lambda}$
  is smaller than the mean. Most waits are shorter than average.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** time between packets or requests, service times in
  queueing models ([21.3.4 Networks and Queues](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.4-Networks-and-Queues.md)),
  randomized exponential backoff, and time to failure of hardware with no
  wear-out.
- **AI and machine learning:** continuous-time Markov models, survival
  analysis, and the "exponential race" behind the Gumbel-max trick
  ([9.10 Logistic and Gumbel](9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md)).
- **Physics:** radioactive decay and the free path of particles.
- **Reliability and insurance:** the time between accidents or claims when the
  risk is constant.
- **Everyday life:** waiting for a taxi on a busy street, or for the next
  shooting star.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Calls arrive at a help desk at rate 3 per hour. What is the
probability that no call arrives in the next 30 minutes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The wait is $\text{Exp}(3)$ in hours, and 30 minutes is $0.5$ hours:

$$P(T \gt 0.5) = e^{-3 \times 0.5} = e^{-1.5} \approx 0.2231$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Exp}(4)$. Find $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and the median.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{16} = 0.0625, \qquad \text{median} = \frac{\ln 2}{4} \approx 0.1733$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A bank has two tellers, both busy. Each finishes their customer
after an independent $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{4})$ time in minutes (mean 4 minutes). You
are next in line. How long do you wait on average for a teller to become free?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

You wait for the minimum of the two times, which is exponential with the sum of the rates:

$$\min(X_1, X_2) \sim \text{Exp}\left(\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = \text{Exp}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right), \qquad E[\min] = 2 \text{ minutes}$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Prove that if $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and $c \gt 0$, then
$cX \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda / c)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \ge 0$, compute the survival function of $Y = cX$:

$$P(Y \gt y) = P(cX \gt y) = P(X \gt y/c) = e^{-\lambda y / c} = e^{-(\lambda/c) y}$$

This is the survival function of $\text{Exp}(\lambda/c)$, and a survival function decides the distribution.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $X_1 \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_2)$ are
independent. Show that $P(X_1 \lt X_2) = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Condition on the value $x$ of $X_1$ and use $P(X_2 \gt x) = e^{-\lambda_2 x}$:

$$P(X_1 \lt X_2) = \int_0^{\infty} \lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1 x} e^{-\lambda_2 x} \thinspace dx = \lambda_1 \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) x} \thinspace dx = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}$$

In words: in a race of exponential clocks, each clock wins with probability proportional to its rate.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Problem 5 with $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 3$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
x1 = rng.exponential(scale=1 / 1, size=n_trials)   # rate 1, so scale 1
x2 = rng.exponential(scale=1 / 3, size=n_trials)   # rate 3, so scale 1/3
print(f"Simulation: {np.mean(x1 < x2):.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {1 / (1 + 3):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.2497
Exact:      0.2500
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ is the waiting time for the next event when events come
  at rate $\lambda$. Here $\lambda$ is the rate and the mean is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$.
- PDF $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, CDF $1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, survival $e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$.
- Mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$, variance $\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$, median $\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$.
- It is memoryless and has a constant hazard rate; the minimum of independent
  exponentials is exponential with the rates added.
- In numpy: `rng.exponential(scale=1/rate, size=n)`. The scale is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X \gt x) = e^{-\lambda x}}
$$
