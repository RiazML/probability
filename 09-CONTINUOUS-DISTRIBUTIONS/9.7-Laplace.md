# 9.7 Laplace

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.7 Laplace

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [9.6 Chi-Square, Student's t and F](9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)

> **Big idea:** The Laplace distribution is two exponential distributions glued back to back, giving a sharp peak and tails that are heavier than the normal's, and it is the noise behind L1 penalties and differential privacy.

---

## Why It Matters

The normal distribution says big errors are extremely rare. Real data often
has a sharper peak (most errors are tiny) and more big errors than the normal
allows. The **Laplace distribution**, also called the **double exponential**,
models exactly this. It explains why minimizing absolute errors gives the
median, why the L1 penalty (LASSO) makes many weights exactly zero, and how
companies like Apple and Google add noise to protect privacy.

## Intuition

Two runners each finish a lap in a random time. The leader's advantage is the
**difference** of their times. If each lap time beyond the minimum is
exponential, then the difference is just as likely to be positive as negative,
it is most likely to be near zero, and it decays exponentially in both
directions.

Another way to see it: flip a fair coin to choose a direction, left or right,
then walk an exponential distance in that direction. The landing point has a
Laplace distribution. Because the exponential has its highest density at 0,
the Laplace has a sharp point (a "tent") at its center, not a smooth round top
like the normal.

With the same variance, the Laplace has a taller, sharper peak and fatter
tails than the normal; the normal has more mass at medium distances.

## Definition

A random variable $X$ has the **Laplace distribution** with **location** $\mu$
and **scale** $b \gt 0$, written $X \sim \text{Laplace}(\mu, b)$, if its PDF is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\left( -\frac{\lvert x - \mu \rvert}{b} \right), \qquad -\infty \lt x \lt \infty
$$

where:

- $\mu$ is the center (the mean, median and mode);
- $b$ is a **scale**: the typical distance from the center. It is not a rate
  and not a variance;
- the support is the whole real line.

**In words:** an exponential decay on each side of $\mu$, with half of the
mass on each side.

The CDF has a closed form in two pieces:

$$
F(x) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{2} \exp\left( \dfrac{x - \mu}{b} \right) & \text{if } x \lt \mu \\
1 - \dfrac{1}{2} \exp\left( -\dfrac{x - \mu}{b} \right) & \text{if } x \ge \mu
\end{cases}
$$

so the two-sided tail is $P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \gt d) = e^{-d/b}$ for $d \ge 0$.

The mean, variance and mean absolute deviation are:

$$
E[X] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = 2b^2, \qquad E\big[\lvert X - \mu \rvert\big] = b
$$

**In words:** the average distance from the center is $b$, and the standard
deviation is $\sqrt{2} \thinspace b$.

## Why It Is True

**Reduce to the exponential.** Let $Y = \lvert X - \mu \rvert$, the distance from the
center. For $y \ge 0$, the event $Y \gt y$ is $X \gt \mu + y$ or $X \lt \mu - y$. The
two tails are equal, and each one is a simple integral:

$$
P(Y \gt y) = 2 \int_{\mu + y}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2b} e^{-(x - \mu)/b} \thinspace dx = e^{-y/b}
$$

So the distance $Y$ is $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{b})$: exponential with **rate** $\frac{1}{b}$
and mean $b$ ([9.3 Exponential](9.3-Exponential.md)). The sign of $X - \mu$ is $+$
or $-$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. This proves the "coin flip plus
exponential walk" story and the tail formula.

**The mean.** The density is symmetric about $\mu$, and the tails are thin
enough for the mean to exist ($E[Y] = b$ is finite), so $E[X] = \mu$.

**The variance.** Since $(X - \mu)^2 = Y^2$, and for $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ the second
moment is $\frac{2}{\lambda^2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[Y^2] && \text{(definition, since } E[X] = \mu \text{)} \\
&= \frac{2}{(1/b)^2} = 2b^2 && \text{(second moment of Exp}(1/b)\text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The CDF.** For $x \lt \mu$, integrate the left half:
$\int_{-\infty}^{x} \frac{1}{2b} e^{(t - \mu)/b} dt = \frac{1}{2} e^{(x - \mu)/b}$.
For $x \ge \mu$, use symmetry: $F(x) = 1 - F(2\mu - x) = 1 - \frac{1}{2} e^{-(x - \mu)/b}$.

### Difference of Two Exponentials

**Claim.** If $X_1, X_2 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, then
$D = X_1 - X_2 \sim \text{Laplace}(0, \frac{1}{\lambda})$.

**Proof.** For $d \ge 0$, condition on $X_2$ and use the exponential survival
function $P(X_1 \gt s) = e^{-\lambda s}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(D \gt d) &= P(X_1 \gt X_2 + d) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-\lambda (x + d)} \thinspace \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \thinspace dx && \text{(condition on } X_2 = x \text{)} \\
&= e^{-\lambda d} \int_0^{\infty} \lambda e^{-2\lambda x} \thinspace dx = e^{-\lambda d} \cdot \frac{1}{2} && \text{(integrate)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is $\frac{1}{2} e^{-d/b}$ with $b = \frac{1}{\lambda}$, the Laplace right tail. By
symmetry ($X_1$ and $X_2$ play the same role), the left tail matches too.

### Key Properties

- **Sharp peak, exponential tails.** The density has a corner at $\mu$. Its tails
  decay like $e^{-\lvert x \rvert / b}$: slower than the normal's $e^{-x^2/2}$, but still
  "light" in the sense of [9.13](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md), so all moments exist.
- **Linear changes.** If $X \sim \text{Laplace}(\mu, b)$, then
  $cX + d \sim \text{Laplace}(c\mu + d, \lvert c \rvert b)$ for $c \ne 0$.
- **Absolute error and the median.** Minimizing the sum of absolute errors
  $\sum \lvert x_i - m \rvert$ gives the median. This is why Laplace noise leads to
  median-based, outlier-resistant estimates, while normal noise leads to
  averages (see [6.10 Expectation as Best Guess](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Differential Privacy

**Problem.** A hospital publishes how many patients have a disease. The true
count is 500. To protect privacy, it adds noise $N \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b)$ with
$b = \frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}$, where the **sensitivity** $\Delta = 1$ (one patient
changes the count by at most 1) and the **privacy level** $\varepsilon = 0.5$. (a) What
is $b$ and the standard deviation of the noise? (b) What is the chance the
published number is off by more than 5? (c) What is the chance it is within 2
of the truth?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The scale and standard deviation:

$$
b = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{2b^2} = \sqrt{2 \times 4} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83
$$

2. (b) Use the two-sided tail $P(\lvert N \rvert \gt d) = e^{-d/b}$:

$$
P(\lvert N \rvert \gt 5) = e^{-5/2} = e^{-2.5} \approx 0.0821
$$

3. (c) The complement of the tail at $d = 2$:

$$
P(\lvert N \rvert \le 2) = 1 - e^{-2/2} = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 1 - 0.3679 = 0.6321
$$

**Answer.** (a) $b = 2$ and the noise has standard deviation about 2.83. (b)
About 8.2% of the time the published count is off by more than 5. (c) About
63% of the time it is within 2.

**Check.** A smaller $\varepsilon$ means stronger privacy: $\varepsilon = 0.1$ would
give $b = 10$ and much larger noise. Privacy and accuracy trade off.

### Example 2: Laplace Against Normal

**Problem.** Take $X \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b)$ with variance 1, and $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
Compare $P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 3)$ with $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 3)$, and $P(\lvert X \rvert \lt 0.1)$
with $P(\lvert Z \rvert \lt 0.1)$.

**Solution.**

1. Variance 1 means $2b^2 = 1$, so $b = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$ and $\frac{1}{b} = \sqrt{2}$.
2. The far tail:

$$
P(\lvert X \rvert \gt 3) = e^{-3\sqrt{2}} = e^{-4.2426} \approx 0.01437, \qquad P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 3) \approx 0.0027
$$

3. Near the center:

$$
P(\lvert X \rvert \lt 0.1) = 1 - e^{-0.1\sqrt{2}} = 1 - e^{-0.14142} \approx 0.1319, \qquad P(\lvert Z \rvert \lt 0.1) = 2\Phi(0.1) - 1 \approx 0.0797
$$

**Answer.** With the same variance, the Laplace is about $\frac{0.01437}{0.0027} \approx 5.3$
times as likely to land beyond 3, and about $1.65$ times as likely to land
within $0.1$ of the center.

**Check.** This is the picture above: more mass in the peak and in the far
tails, less in between. The variance is the same because these effects
balance out.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 1 and the "difference of exponentials" story. numpy's
`rng.laplace(loc, scale)` uses the same scale $b$ as this book.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
b = 2.0

# Laplace noise for differential privacy (numpy scale = b).
noise = rng.laplace(loc=0.0, scale=b, size=n_trials)
print(f"P(|N| > 5):  simulation {np.mean(np.abs(noise) > 5):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-2.5):.4f}")
print(f"P(|N| <= 2): simulation {np.mean(np.abs(noise) <= 2):.4f}, exact {1 - np.exp(-1):.4f}")
print(f"Var(N):      simulation {noise.var():.4f}, exact {2 * b**2:.4f}")

# Difference of two Exp(rate 1/b) times is Laplace(0, b).
# numpy's exponential wants scale = 1/rate = b.
d = rng.exponential(scale=b, size=n_trials) - rng.exponential(scale=b, size=n_trials)
print(f"Difference:  P(|D| > 5) {np.mean(np.abs(d) > 5):.4f}, Var {d.var():.4f} "
      f"(exact {np.exp(-2.5):.4f}, {2 * b**2:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
P(|N| > 5):  simulation 0.0820, exact 0.0821
P(|N| <= 2): simulation 0.6321, exact 0.6321
Var(N):      simulation 8.0025, exact 8.0000
Difference:  P(|D| > 5) 0.0816, Var 7.9745 (exact 0.0821, 8.0000)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing the scale with the standard deviation.** The standard deviation of
  $\text{Laplace}(\mu, b)$ is $\sqrt{2} \thinspace b$, not $b$. To match a variance of 1,
  use $b \approx 0.707$.
- **Confusing the scale with a rate.** The tails decay like $e^{-\lvert x \rvert / b}$, so a
  bigger $b$ means **more** spread, unlike the rate $\lambda$ in $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$.
- **Forgetting the factor $\frac{1}{2}$.** One tail is $P(X - \mu \gt d) = \frac{1}{2} e^{-d/b}$.
  Both tails together give $e^{-d/b}$.
- **Calling the Laplace "heavy-tailed".** Its tails are heavier than the normal's
  but still exponential; all its moments are finite. Truly heavy tails decay
  like a power (see [9.13](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a Laplace prior on weights gives the L1
  penalty (LASSO), which sets many weights to exactly zero; the L1 loss (mean
  absolute error) is the Laplace noise model.
- **Privacy:** the **Laplace mechanism** of differential privacy adds
  $\text{Laplace}(0, \frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon})$ noise to counts and sums, as in national
  census data.
- **Signal and image processing:** differences between neighboring pixels and
  audio samples are close to Laplace, which helps compression.
- **Finance:** daily returns often have a sharper peak and fatter tails than the
  normal; the Laplace is a simple first fix.
- **Everyday life:** the gap between two people's arrival times when both are
  "a bit late" in an exponential way.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \text{Laplace}(10, 3)$. Find $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $P(X \gt 13)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 10, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = 2 \times 3^2 = 18, \qquad P(X \gt 13) = \frac{1}{2} e^{-(13 - 10)/3} = \frac{1}{2} e^{-1} \approx 0.1839$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For $X \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b)$, find the value $q$ with $P(\lvert X \rvert \le q) = 0.95$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$1 - e^{-q/b} = 0.95 \implies e^{-q/b} = 0.05 \implies q = b \ln 20 \approx 2.996 \thinspace b$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A count is released with $\text{Laplace}(0, \frac{1}{\varepsilon})$ noise. How
small can $\varepsilon$ be if you want the noise to be within $\pm 10$ with probability
at least $0.99$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need $e^{-10\varepsilon} \le 0.01$:

$$10 \varepsilon \ge \ln 100 \approx 4.605 \implies \varepsilon \ge 0.4605$$

So $\varepsilon$ can be as small as about $0.46$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that $E[\lvert X - \mu \rvert] = b$ for $X \sim \text{Laplace}(\mu, b)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The distance $Y = \lvert X - \mu \rvert$ has $P(Y \gt y) = e^{-y/b}$, so $Y \sim \text{Exp}(\frac{1}{b})$ with rate $\frac{1}{b}$:

$$E\big[\lvert X - \mu \rvert\big] = E[Y] = \frac{1}{1/b} = b$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $S \in \lbrace -1, +1 \rbrace$ be a fair random sign and $Y \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$
be independent of $S$. Find the PDF of $X = SY$ and show it is $\text{Laplace}(0, \frac{1}{\lambda})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $x \gt 0$, $X \gt x$ needs $S = +1$ and $Y \gt x$. For $x \lt 0$, $X \lt x$ needs $S = -1$ and $Y \gt -x = \lvert x \rvert$. So for any $x \ne 0$:

$$P(X \gt x) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-\lambda x} \ (x \gt 0), \qquad P(X \lt x) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-\lambda \lvert x \rvert} \ (x \lt 0)$$

Differentiating either tail gives $f(x) = \frac{\lambda}{2} e^{-\lambda \lvert x \rvert} = \frac{1}{2b} e^{-\lvert x \rvert / b}$ with $b = \frac{1}{\lambda}$.

</details>

## Summary

- $\text{Laplace}(\mu, b)$ has PDF $\frac{1}{2b} e^{-\lvert x - \mu \rvert / b}$: two exponentials back to back.
- Here $b$ is a scale: the mean distance from $\mu$. The variance is $2b^2$.
- The distance from the center is $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{b})$, and the difference of two
  independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ times is $\text{Laplace}(0, \frac{1}{\lambda})$.
- Sharper peak and fatter tails than a normal with the same variance.
- In numpy: `rng.laplace(loc=mu, scale=b, size=n)`.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \gt d) = e^{-d/b}}
$$
