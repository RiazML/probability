# 9.2 Normal (Gaussian)

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 09: Continuous Distributions](README.md) / 9.2 Normal (Gaussian)

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [9.1 Uniform](9.1-Uniform.md)

> **Big idea:** The normal distribution is the symmetric bell curve that appears whenever many small independent effects add up, and every normal is just a shifted and stretched copy of one standard bell.

---

## Why It Matters

Heights, exam scores, measurement errors and the noise in a sensor all look
like a bell. The reason is the
[Central Limit Theorem](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md):
a sum of many small independent effects is close to normal, whatever the
effects are. The normal is also the workhorse of machine learning: weight
initialization, Gaussian noise, diffusion models, variational autoencoders and
the [multivariate Gaussian](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/README.md) all start here.

## Intuition

Think about the heights of adult men in a city. Most are near the average,
say 170 cm. Fewer are 160 cm or 180 cm, and very few are 150 cm or 190 cm. If
you draw a histogram of a million heights, you get a smooth, symmetric hill.
That hill is the **bell curve**.

Why a bell? A height is the result of many small causes: hundreds of genes,
food, sleep, illness. Each pushes the height a little up or a little down.
Most people get a mix of ups and downs that roughly cancel, so they land near
the middle. Getting almost all "ups" is rare, so the tails are thin.

Two numbers describe the whole hill: the center $\mu$ (where the peak is) and
the spread $\sigma$ (how wide it is). Once you know them, you can answer
questions like "what fraction of men are taller than 185 cm?" with one rule:
**count how many standard deviations away you are**.

## Picture

```text
                            |
                         .--+--.
                       /    |    \
                     /      |      \
                   /|       |       |\
                 /  |       |       |  \
              _/    |       |       |    \_
          ___/      |       |       |      \___
     ----+-----+----+-------+-------+----+-----+----
       μ-3σ  μ-2σ  μ-σ      μ      μ+σ  μ+2σ  μ+3σ
                    |<----68%------>|
               |<---------95%----------->|
          |<-------------99.7%--------------->|
```

The normal curve is a symmetric bell centered at $\mu$; about 68%, 95% and
99.7% of the area lies within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the center.

## Definition

A random variable $X$ has the **normal distribution** (also called the
**Gaussian distribution**) with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, written
$X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if its PDF is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \qquad -\infty \lt x \lt \infty
$$

where:

- $\mu$ is the **mean**, the center of the bell (any real number);
- $\sigma^2 \gt 0$ is the **variance**. The second parameter is the variance,
  not the standard deviation, so $\mathcal{N}(0, 4)$ has $\sigma = 2$;
- the support is the whole real line $\mathbb{R}$.

**In words:** the density is highest at $\mu$ and falls off like
$e^{-(\text{distance})^2}$, where the distance is measured in units of $\sigma$.

The **standard normal** is $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Its PDF and CDF have
their own names, $\varphi$ ("phi") and $\Phi$ ("capital phi"):

$$
\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}, \qquad \Phi(z) = P(Z \le z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} \varphi(t) \thinspace dt
$$

The integral for $\Phi$ has **no closed form**: it cannot be written with
ordinary functions. We read it from a table, from `scipy.stats.norm.cdf`, or
from the error function: $\Phi(z) = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \operatorname{erf}(z / \sqrt{2})\right)$.

The **standardization** rule turns any normal into the standard one. The
number $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ is called the **z-score** of $x$: how many
standard deviations $x$ is above the mean.

$$
X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \implies Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \qquad P(X \le x) = \Phi\left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)
$$

**In words:** to find any normal probability, convert to a z-score, then
look up the standard normal.

Facts worth knowing by heart:

| Event | Probability |
|---|---|
| $\lvert Z \rvert \le 1$ | $0.6827$ (about 68%) |
| $\lvert Z \rvert \le 2$ | $0.9545$ (about 95%) |
| $\lvert Z \rvert \le 3$ | $0.9973$ (about 99.7%) |
| $\lvert Z \rvert \le 1.96$ | $0.95$ exactly (to 4 digits) |
| $Z \le 1.645$ | $0.95$ |
| $Z \le 1.2816$ | $0.90$ |

## Why It Is True

**Step 1: the PDF integrates to 1.** It is enough to show
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-z^2/2} \thinspace dz = \sqrt{2\pi}$. This is the
famous **Gaussian integral**.

<details>
<summary>Full proof (optional)</summary>

Call the integral $I$. Multiply two copies, one in $x$ and one in $y$, so that $I^2$ is an integral over the whole plane:

$$I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2} \thinspace dx \thinspace dy$$

Switch to polar coordinates: $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and $dx \thinspace dy = r \thinspace dr \thinspace d\theta$:

$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2/2} \thinspace r \thinspace dr \thinspace d\theta = 2\pi \left[ -e^{-r^2/2} \right]_0^{\infty} = 2\pi (0 - (-1)) = 2\pi$$

So $I = \sqrt{2\pi}$, since $I \gt 0$. The extra factor $r$ is what makes the inner integral easy; without the trick of squaring, no one can integrate $e^{-z^2/2}$ by hand.

</details>

**Step 2: the standard normal has mean 0.** The function $z \thinspace \varphi(z)$
is odd: its value at $-z$ is minus its value at $z$. Its integral over the whole
line is finite (the tails die very fast), so the two halves cancel and
$E[Z] = 0$.

**Step 3: the standard normal has variance 1.** Notice that
$\varphi'(z) = -z \thinspace \varphi(z)$. Integrate by parts with $u = z$ and
$dv = z \thinspace \varphi(z) \thinspace dz$, so $v = -\varphi(z)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[Z^2] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z \cdot z \thinspace \varphi(z) \thinspace dz && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= \Big[ -z \thinspace \varphi(z) \Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \varphi(z) \thinspace dz && \text{(integration by parts)} \\
&= 0 + 1 = 1 && \text{(}\varphi \text{ dies fast; total area is 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\operatorname{Var}(Z) = E[Z^2] - (E[Z])^2 = 1 - 0 = 1$.

**Step 4: every normal is a stretched standard normal.** Let $X = \mu + \sigma Z$
with $\sigma \gt 0$. Its CDF is:

$$
F_X(x) = P(\mu + \sigma Z \le x) = P\left( Z \le \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right) = \Phi\left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)
$$

Differentiate with the chain rule ($\Phi' = \varphi$):

$$
f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma} \varphi\left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}
$$

This is exactly the $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ density. So
$E[X] = \mu + \sigma E[Z] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 \operatorname{Var}(Z) = \sigma^2$.
Reading the same steps backwards proves the standardization rule.

### Key Properties

- **Shape.** Symmetric about $\mu$, so mean = median = mode = $\mu$. The peak
  height is $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx \frac{0.399}{\sigma}$. The curve
  bends (changes from "frown" to "smile") at $\mu \pm \sigma$.
- **Linear changes stay normal.** If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and
  $a \ne 0$, then $aX + b \sim \mathcal{N}(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)$. Step 4 is the case
  $a = \sigma$, $b = \mu$ applied to $Z$.
- **Sums stay normal.** If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and
  $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are independent, then
  $X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. The proof is in
  [13.5 Sums of Known Distributions](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md).
  **Variances add, standard deviations do not.**
- **Very light tails.** $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 3) \approx 0.0027$ and
  $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 5) \approx 5.7 \times 10^{-7}$. Far values almost never happen
  (compare [9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Exam Scores

**Problem.** Scores on a national exam are $X \sim \mathcal{N}(70, 100)$, so
$\mu = 70$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{100} = 10$. (a) What fraction of students score
above 85? (b) What fraction score between 60 and 80? (c) What score puts a
student in the top 10%?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Standardize 85:

$$
z = \frac{85 - 70}{10} = 1.5, \qquad P(X \gt 85) = 1 - \Phi(1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668
$$

2. (b) Standardize both ends: $\frac{60 - 70}{10} = -1$ and $\frac{80 - 70}{10} = 1$.

$$
P(60 \lt X \lt 80) = \Phi(1) - \Phi(-1) = 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6827
$$

3. (c) We need the score $x$ with $P(X \le x) = 0.90$. The standard normal has
   $\Phi(1.2816) = 0.90$, so go $1.2816$ standard deviations above the mean:

$$
x = \mu + 1.2816 \thinspace \sigma = 70 + 1.2816 \times 10 = 82.816
$$

**Answer.** (a) About 6.7% score above 85. (b) About 68% score between 60 and
80. (c) A score of about 82.8 or more is in the top 10%.

**Check.** Part (b) is the "68" in the 68-95-99.7 rule, as it should be: 60 and
80 are exactly one standard deviation from the mean.

### Example 2: Quality Control for Bolts

**Problem.** A machine makes bolts with diameter $D \sim \mathcal{N}(10, 0.0004)$
millimeters, so $\sigma = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$ mm. A bolt is accepted if its
diameter is between 9.95 and 10.05 mm. What fraction is rejected?

**Solution.**

1. Standardize the limits:

$$
\frac{9.95 - 10}{0.02} = -2.5, \qquad \frac{10.05 - 10}{0.02} = 2.5
$$

2. The accepted fraction:

$$
P(-2.5 \le Z \le 2.5) = \Phi(2.5) - \Phi(-2.5) = 0.99379 - 0.00621 = 0.98758
$$

3. The rejected fraction:

$$
1 - 0.98758 = 0.01242
$$

**Answer.** About 1.24% of the bolts are rejected, roughly 12 in every 1,000.

**Check.** The limits are 2.5 standard deviations out, a bit beyond the "95%
within 2" mark, so the reject rate should be a bit below 5%. It is.

## Simulate It

This code checks Example 1. Note that `rng.normal(loc, scale)` takes the
**standard deviation** as `scale`, not the variance.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
mu, var = 70, 100

# numpy wants the standard deviation, so pass sqrt(variance) = 10.
scores = rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=np.sqrt(var), size=n_trials)

print(f"P(X > 85):      simulation {np.mean(scores > 85):.4f}, exact {norm.sf(1.5):.4f}")
print(f"P(60 < X < 80): simulation {np.mean((scores > 60) & (scores < 80)):.4f}, "
      f"exact {norm.cdf(1) - norm.cdf(-1):.4f}")
print(f"90th percentile: simulation {np.quantile(scores, 0.9):.3f}, "
      f"exact {mu + 10 * norm.ppf(0.9):.3f}")
print(f"Mean, variance:  simulation {scores.mean():.3f}, {scores.var():.3f}; exact 70, 100")
```

Output:

```text
P(X > 85):      simulation 0.0668, exact 0.0668
P(60 < X < 80): simulation 0.6829, exact 0.6827
90th percentile: simulation 82.815, exact 82.816
Mean, variance:  simulation 70.001, 100.097; exact 70, 100
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up variance and standard deviation.** In this book
  $\mathcal{N}(70, 100)$ has variance 100 and $\sigma = 10$. Many papers and
  numpy use the standard deviation instead. Always check which one is meant.
- **Forgetting to standardize.** $P(X \gt 85)$ is not $1 - \Phi(85)$. First
  compute $z = \frac{85 - 70}{10}$.
- **Adding standard deviations.** For independent normals the **variances**
  add. Two independent errors with $\sigma = 3$ and $\sigma = 4$ give
  $\sigma = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, not $7$.
- **Assuming everything is normal.** Incomes, waiting times and stock returns
  are skewed or heavy-tailed. Using a normal there badly underestimates
  extreme events (see [9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).
- **Reading a density as a probability.** $f(70) \approx 0.0399$ for exam
  scores is not "the chance of scoring 70". For a continuous variable that
  chance is 0.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** He and Glorot normal weight initialization;
  Gaussian noise in diffusion models; the reparameterization trick
  $x = \mu + \sigma z$ in variational autoencoders; least squares regression,
  which is the best fit when the noise is normal.
- **Computer science:** modeling sensor noise and network jitter; Gaussian
  blur in image processing; random projections for fast nearest-neighbor search.
- **Science and engineering:** measurement errors, quality control
  ("six sigma"), the Kalman filter in navigation and robotics.
- **Statistics and medicine:** z-scores for growth charts and test results; the
  normal approximation from the
  [Central Limit Theorem](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md).
- **Everyday life:** heights, birth weights, and "how unusual is this score?"

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(50, 25)$. Find $P(X \gt 60)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\sigma = \sqrt{25} = 5$, so $z = \frac{60 - 50}{5} = 2$:

$$P(X \gt 60) = 1 - \Phi(2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A student scores 82 on a test with mean 74 and standard deviation
4, and 88 on a test with mean 80 and standard deviation 8. On which test did
the student do better compared with the class?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Compare z-scores:

$$z_1 = \frac{82 - 74}{4} = 2, \qquad z_2 = \frac{88 - 80}{8} = 1$$

The first test is better: 2 standard deviations above the mean, against only 1.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Two independent tasks take $A \sim \mathcal{N}(30, 16)$ and
$B \sim \mathcal{N}(20, 9)$ minutes. What is the probability that both together
take more than 55 minutes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The sum is normal with mean $30 + 20 = 50$ and variance $16 + 9 = 25$, so $\sigma = 5$:

$$P(A + B \gt 55) = 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{55 - 50}{5} \right) = 1 - \Phi(1) = 0.1587$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that if $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then
$P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \le k\sigma)$ does not depend on $\mu$ or $\sigma$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Standardize. The event $\lvert X - \mu \rvert \le k\sigma$ is the same as $\lvert Z \rvert \le k$ with $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$:

$$P(\lvert X - \mu \rvert \le k\sigma) = P(\lvert Z \rvert \le k) = 2\Phi(k) - 1$$

This uses only $k$. That is why the 68-95-99.7 rule works for every normal.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that the peak of the $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ density is at
$x = \mu$ and that the curve changes from bending down to bending up at
$x = \mu \pm \sigma$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $f(x) = c \thinspace e^{-u^2/2}$ with $u = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ and $c = 1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$. Differentiate with the chain rule ($du/dx = 1/\sigma$):

$$f'(x) = -\frac{u}{\sigma} f(x), \qquad f''(x) = \frac{u^2 - 1}{\sigma^2} f(x)$$

$f'(x) = 0$ only at $u = 0$, that is $x = \mu$, and $f'$ changes from positive to negative there, so it is the peak. $f''(x) = 0$ when $u^2 = 1$, that is $x = \mu \pm \sigma$. Between them $f'' \lt 0$ (bending down), outside them $f'' \gt 0$ (bending up).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the 68-95-99.7 rule by simulating a million standard
normal values.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
z = rng.standard_normal(1_000_000)
for k in [1, 2, 3]:
    sim = np.mean(np.abs(z) <= k)
    print(f"P(|Z| <= {k}): simulation {sim:.4f}, exact {2 * norm.cdf(k) - 1:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(|Z| <= 1): simulation 0.6829, exact 0.6827
P(|Z| <= 2): simulation 0.9543, exact 0.9545
P(|Z| <= 3): simulation 0.9972, exact 0.9973
```

</details>

## Summary

- $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is a symmetric bell with mean $\mu$ and variance
  $\sigma^2$. In this book, the second parameter is the variance.
- Every normal is $\mu + \sigma Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, so
  standardize with $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ and use $\Phi$.
- The CDF $\Phi$ has no closed form; use a table or `scipy.stats.norm`.
- About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of the mass is within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations.
- Independent normals add to a normal: means add and variances add.
- In numpy: `rng.normal(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=n)`, where `scale` is the
  **standard deviation**.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(X \le x) = \Phi\left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)}
$$
