# 4.2 Multiplication Rule and Chain Rule

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.2 Multiplication Rule and Chain Rule

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [4.1 Conditional Probability](4.1-Conditional-Probability.md)

> **Big idea:** The chance that several things all happen is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second given the first, times the chance of the third given the first two, and so on.

---

## Why It Matters

Many questions ask for the chance that **several** things all happen: two aces in
a row, three servers all failing, a whole sentence being produced word by word.
The **multiplication rule** turns one hard "and" question into a product of easy
steps. Its long form, the **chain rule**, is exactly how language models give a
probability to a sentence, and it is the backbone of tree diagrams and Bayes'
theorem later in this module.

## Intuition

You draw two cards from a shuffled deck, without putting the first one back. What
is the chance that both are aces?

Think of it in two steps. The first card is an ace with chance $\frac{4}{52}$. Now
**suppose** it was an ace. The deck has 51 cards left, and 3 of them are aces. So
the second card is an ace with chance $\frac{3}{51}$. To get "both", you need the
first step to succeed **and then** the second step to succeed. A fraction of a
fraction is a product:

$$
\frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} = \frac{12}{2652} = \frac{1}{221} \approx 0.0045
$$

It helps to imagine the experiment done 2,652 times. In about
$2652 \times \frac{4}{52} = 204$ of them, the first card is an ace. Of those 204, a
fraction $\frac{3}{51}$ also has an ace second: $204 \times \frac{3}{51} = 12$ runs.
So about 12 runs out of 2,652 give two aces, which is 1 in 221.

## Picture

```text
   2,652 deals of two cards
          |
          |   keep the fraction P(A1) = 4/52
          v
     204 deals where card 1 is an ace
          |
          |   keep the fraction P(A2 | A1) = 3/51
          v
      12 deals where card 1 AND card 2 are aces

   P(A1 ∩ A2) = 12 / 2,652 = (4/52) x (3/51) = 1/221
```

Each step keeps a fraction of what survived the step before, so the fractions multiply.

## Definition

The **multiplication rule** says that for any events $A$ and $B$ with
$P(B) \gt 0$ (or $P(A) \gt 0$ for the second form):

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(B) \thinspace P(A \mid B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B \mid A)
$$

where $P(A \cap B)$ is the chance that both happen, and $P(A \mid B)$ is the chance
of $A$ once we know $B$ happened.

**In words:** the chance of "both" is the chance of one of them, times the chance of
the other given the first.

The **chain rule** is the same idea for $n$ events $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$, as long
as $P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}) \gt 0$:

$$
P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \thinspace P(A_2 \mid A_1) \thinspace P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1})
$$

**In words:** to get the chance that everything happens, go through the events one
at a time, and at each step use the chance of the next event given **all** the
events before it.

> **Note:** You may put the events in any order you like. For three events,
> $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(C) \thinspace P(B \mid C) \thinspace P(A \mid B \cap C)$ is just
> as true. Choose the order in which the conditional probabilities are easiest to
> find, usually the order in which things happen in time.

## Why It Is True

**Multiplication rule.** Start from the definition of conditional probability in
[4.1](4.1-Conditional-Probability.md) and multiply both sides by $P(B)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \mid B) &= \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} && \text{(definition, } P(B) \gt 0 \text{)} \\
P(B) \thinspace P(A \mid B) &= P(A \cap B) && \text{(multiply both sides by } P(B) \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Swap the roles of $A$ and $B$ and use $B \cap A = A \cap B$ to get the second form.

**Chain rule.** Write each conditional probability with its definition. Every
numerator cancels the next denominator, like a telescope folding up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& P(A_1) \cdot \frac{P(A_1 \cap A_2)}{P(A_1)} \cdot \frac{P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3)}{P(A_1 \cap A_2)} \cdots \frac{P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n)}{P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1})} && \text{(definition of each factor)} \\
&= P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n) && \text{(everything else cancels)}
\end{aligned}
$$

All denominators are positive. Here is why: each one is the probability of a
bigger event than $A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}$, so each is at least
$P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}) \gt 0$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Three Hearts in a Row

**Problem.** Deal 3 cards from a shuffled deck, without replacement. What is the
probability that all three are hearts?

**Solution.**

1. Let $H_i$ = "card $i$ is a heart". We want $P(H_1 \cap H_2 \cap H_3)$.
2. First card: 13 hearts among 52 cards, so $P(H_1) = \frac{13}{52}$.
3. Given the first was a heart: 12 hearts among 51 cards, so
   $P(H_2 \mid H_1) = \frac{12}{51}$.
4. Given the first two were hearts: 11 hearts among 50 cards, so
   $P(H_3 \mid H_1 \cap H_2) = \frac{11}{50}$.
5. Multiply with the chain rule:

$$
P(H_1 \cap H_2 \cap H_3) = \frac{13}{52} \times \frac{12}{51} \times \frac{11}{50} = \frac{1716}{132600} = \frac{11}{850} \approx 0.0129
$$

**Answer.** About 1.3%, or roughly 1 deal in 77.

**Check.** Count instead: there are $\binom{13}{3} = 286$ sets of three hearts out of
$\binom{52}{3} = 22100$ sets of three cards. And $\frac{286}{22100} = \frac{11}{850}$.
The two methods agree, and so does the simulation below.

### Example 2: How a Language Model Scores a Sentence

**Problem.** A language model writes text one word at a time. For the sentence
"I love probability" it reports these numbers:

- $P(\text{first word is "I"}) = 0.02$,
- $P(\text{"love"} \mid \text{"I"}) = 0.01$,
- $P(\text{"probability"} \mid \text{"I love"}) = 0.001$.

What probability does the model give to the whole sentence?

**Solution.**

1. Let $W_1, W_2, W_3$ be the events "word 1 is I", "word 2 is love", "word 3 is
   probability".
2. Apply the chain rule in time order:

$$
P(W_1 \cap W_2 \cap W_3) = P(W_1) \thinspace P(W_2 \mid W_1) \thinspace P(W_3 \mid W_1 \cap W_2) = 0.02 \times 0.01 \times 0.001
$$

3. Multiply: $0.02 \times 0.01 = 0.0002$, and $0.0002 \times 0.001 = 0.0000002$.

**Answer.** $2 \times 10^{-7}$, so about 2 in 10 million.

**Check.** The number is tiny, but that is normal: there are millions of possible
three-word sentences. Because products of many small numbers get too small for a
computer, real models add **logarithms** instead:
$\ln(0.02) + \ln(0.01) + \ln(0.001) \approx -3.91 - 4.61 - 6.91 = -15.42$, and
$e^{-15.42} \approx 2 \times 10^{-7}$. More in
[21.1.3 Language Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.3-Language-Models.md).

### Example 3: The Birthday Problem as a Chain

**Problem.** 23 people are in a room. Birthdays are equally likely to be any of 365
days (ignore 29 February), and independent. What is the chance that **no two**
share a birthday?

**Solution.**

1. Let $D_i$ be the event that person $i$ has a birthday different from persons
   $1, \dots, i-1$.
2. Person 1 can have any day: $P(D_1) = \frac{365}{365}$.
3. Given no match so far, person 2 must avoid 1 used day:
   $P(D_2 \mid D_1) = \frac{364}{365}$.
4. Given no match among the first $i - 1$ people, $i - 1$ days are used, so person
   $i$ must pick one of the $365 - (i - 1)$ free days:
   $P(D_i \mid D_1 \cap \dots \cap D_{i-1}) = \frac{366 - i}{365}$.
5. Chain rule over 23 people:

$$
P(\text{no match}) = \frac{365}{365} \times \frac{364}{365} \times \frac{363}{365} \times \cdots \times \frac{343}{365} \approx 0.4927
$$

**Answer.** About $0.493$, so the chance of **at least one** shared birthday is
about $1 - 0.4927 = 0.5073$, just over one half.

**Check.** This matches the counting answer in
[2.11.1 Birthday Problem](../02-COUNTING/2.11-Classic-Counting-Problems/2.11.1-Birthday-Problem.md).
The chain rule gives the same result with no big factorials.

## Simulate It

This code shuffles a real deck many times and checks the three-hearts answer from
Example 1. It does **not** use the chain rule, so it is an honest test of it.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# Each row is a full shuffle of the cards 0..51. Cards 0..12 are the hearts.
decks = rng.permuted(np.tile(np.arange(52), (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)
first_three = decks[:, :3]
all_hearts = np.all(first_three < 13, axis=1)

estimate = all_hearts.mean()
exact = (13 / 52) * (12 / 51) * (11 / 50)

print(f"Simulation: {estimate:.5f}")
print(f"Exact:      {exact:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.01282
Exact:      0.01294
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to update after each step.** Without replacement, the deck changes.
  Writing $\frac{13}{52} \times \frac{13}{52} \times \frac{13}{52}$ is wrong; that is the
  answer **with** replacement.
- **Multiplying plain probabilities when events depend on each other.**
  $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$ is true only for independent events
  ([4.7 Independence](4.7-Independence.md)). In general you need $P(B \mid A)$.
- **Conditioning on only the last event.** In the chain rule, step $k$ is
  conditioned on **all** earlier events, not just the one before it. (Assuming
  only the last one matters is the Markov property, a special model from
  [18.3.1 Markov Property](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.1-Markov-Property.md).)
- **Underflow in code.** A product of hundreds of small probabilities becomes
  $0.0$ in floating point. Add log-probabilities instead.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** language models, and all **autoregressive** models,
  write $P(\text{sequence})$ as a chain-rule product of next-token probabilities.
- **Bayesian networks:** a big joint probability is written as a product of small
  conditional pieces, one per node.
- **Computer science:** the chance that a request passes every stage of a pipeline
  (load balancer, server, database) is a product of stage-by-stage chances.
- **Card games and lotteries:** the chance of a hand or a ticket is a chain of
  draws without replacement.
- **Everyday life:** "I need to catch the bus, **and** then the train must be on
  time, **and** then the meeting must not run late." Long chains of "and" get
  unlikely fast.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A bag has 5 red and 3 blue balls. You draw 2 balls without
replacement. What is the probability that both are red?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(R_1 \cap R_2) = P(R_1) \thinspace P(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14} \approx 0.357$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $P(A) = 0.6$ and $P(B \mid A) = 0.5$. Find $P(A \cap B)$ and
$P(A \cap B^c)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(A \cap B) = 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.3$$

Given $A$, the complement rule gives $P(B^c \mid A) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$, so:

$$P(A \cap B^c) = 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.3$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A job applicant must pass three interview rounds. She passes round
1 with probability $0.6$. If she passes round 1, she passes round 2 with
probability $0.5$. If she passes rounds 1 and 2, she passes round 3 with
probability $0.4$. What is the chance she gets the job?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(R_1 \cap R_2 \cap R_3) = 0.6 \times 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.12$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A phone uses a 4-digit PIN (10,000 choices). A thief tries 3
**different** PINs at random, then the phone locks. Use the chain rule to find the
chance that all 3 guesses are wrong.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Given that the first $k$ guesses were wrong, the next guess is a random choice from
the $10000 - k$ untried PINs, and $10000 - k - 1$ of them are wrong:

$$P(\text{all wrong}) = \frac{9999}{10000} \times \frac{9998}{9999} \times \frac{9997}{9998} = \frac{9997}{10000} = 0.9997$$

The fractions telescope. So the chance of success is $\frac{3}{10000}$, the same as
in [1.1 What is Probability](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.1-What-is-Probability.md).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Deal 4 cards from a shuffled deck. What is the probability that all
four have different suits?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The first card can be anything. The second must avoid the first card's suit: 39 of
the 51 remaining cards. The third must avoid two suits: 26 of 50. The fourth must
be the last suit: 13 of 49.

$$P = \frac{52}{52} \times \frac{39}{51} \times \frac{26}{50} \times \frac{13}{49} = \frac{13182}{124950} \approx 0.1055$$

</details>

**6. (Hard)** Prove the chain rule for three events by using the multiplication
rule twice. Assume $P(A \cap B) \gt 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Treat $A \cap B$ as one event and apply the multiplication rule to it and $C$.
Then apply the multiplication rule again to $A \cap B$:

$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A \cap B) \thinspace P(C \mid A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B \mid A) \thinspace P(C \mid A \cap B)$$

The condition $P(A \cap B) \gt 0$ makes $P(C \mid A \cap B)$ defined, and it also gives
$P(A) \ge P(A \cap B) \gt 0$, so $P(B \mid A)$ is defined. The same idea, repeated,
proves the chain rule for any $n$ by induction.

</details>

## Summary

- The multiplication rule turns "and" into a product:
  $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B \mid A)$.
- The chain rule repeats this for many events. Each factor is conditioned on
  **everything before it**.
- You can choose the order of the events. Pick the order where each conditional
  probability is easy, usually the time order.
- Without replacement, update the counts after every step.
- Language models score sentences with the chain rule, and use sums of logs to
  avoid tiny numbers.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \thinspace P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1})}
$$
