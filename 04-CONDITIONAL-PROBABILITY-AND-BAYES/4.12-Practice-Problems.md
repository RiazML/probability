## How to Use This Set

- Try each problem on paper before you open the answer. Write the conditional
  probability you want **in words** first, for example "the chance of disease given a
  positive test".
- Each problem is labeled with the topic it uses, so you can go back and reread that
  file if you get stuck.
- Use the checks of this module: posteriors add up to 1, a total probability lies
  between its case rates, and tree leaves add up to 1.
- The Warm-Up problems take a few minutes each, the Core problems 10 to 15 minutes, and
  the Challenge problems longer. Do the Simulation Projects on a computer.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy, 4.1 and 4.7)** $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.5$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.8$. Find
$P(A \cap B)$ and $P(A \mid B)$. Are $A$ and $B$ independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From the addition rule:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.8 = 0.3$$

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 0.6$$

Since $P(A \mid B) = 0.6 = P(A)$ (equivalently $0.3 = 0.6 \times 0.5$), the events are
independent.

</details>

**W2. (Easy, 4.2)** A box has 4 red and 6 blue balls. You draw 3 without replacement.
What is the chance that all 3 are blue?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the chain rule:

$$P(\text{all blue}) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9} \times \frac{4}{8} = \frac{120}{720} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$$

</details>

**W3. (Easy, 4.3)** A shop buys 70% of its chargers from supplier 1, which has a defect
rate of 2%, and 30% from supplier 2, which has a defect rate of 5%. What fraction of
the chargers are defective?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(D) = 0.02 \times 0.7 + 0.05 \times 0.3 = 0.014 + 0.015 = 0.029$$

The answer lies between $0.02$ and $0.05$, as it must.

</details>

**W4. (Easy, 4.5)** Your prior probability for a hypothesis is $0.2$. You see evidence
with likelihood ratio 6. What is your posterior probability?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds: $\frac{0.2}{0.8} = 0.25$. Posterior odds: $0.25 \times 6 = 1.5$.

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{1.5}{1 + 1.5} = 0.6$$

</details>

**W5. (Easy, 4.7)** Roll two fair dice. Let $A$ = "the first die shows 6" and $B$ = "the
sum is at least 11". Are $A$ and $B$ independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(A) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$. $B = \lbrace (5,6), (6,5), (6,6) \rbrace$, so
$P(B) = \frac{3}{36} = \frac{1}{12}$. $A \cap B = \lbrace (6,5), (6,6) \rbrace$, so:

$$P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{36} = \frac{1}{18} \ne \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{72}$$

They are dependent: a 6 on the first die makes a big sum much more likely.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium, 4.11.2)** Rework the medical test of
[4.11.2](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.2-Medical-Testing.md) (sensitivity $0.99$, false
positive rate $0.05$) with prevalence $0.1$ instead of $0.01$. Find $P(D \mid +)$. What
changes, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.1}{0.99 \times 0.1 + 0.05 \times 0.9} = \frac{0.099}{0.099 + 0.045} = \frac{0.099}{0.144} = 0.6875$$

The answer rises from about $0.167$ to about $0.69$. The test is the same (likelihood
ratio $19.8$); only the prior changed, from odds 1 to 99 to odds 1 to 9. With more sick
people in the group, true positives make up a much bigger share of all positives.

</details>

**C2. (Medium, 4.11.1)** In the Monty Hall game you pick door 1 and the host, who knows
where the car is, opens door 2 to show a goat. Use Bayes' theorem to find the chance
that the car is behind door 3.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Likelihoods of "host opens door 2": $\frac{1}{2}$ if the car is behind door 1 (he picks at
random), $0$ if behind door 2, $1$ if behind door 3 (he is forced). The priors are all
$\frac{1}{3}$ and cancel:

$$P(C_3 \mid O_2) = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{2} + 0 + 1} = \frac{2}{3}$$

Switching to door 3 wins with chance $\frac{2}{3}$.

</details>

**C3. (Medium, 4.8)** A fair four-sided die has faces 1, 2, 3, 4. Let $A = \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$,
$B = \lbrace 1, 3 \rbrace$ and $C = \lbrace 1, 4 \rbrace$. Show that these three events are
pairwise independent but not mutually independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each event has probability $\frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$. Every pair meets only in face 1:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \cap C) = P(B \cap C) = P(\lbrace 1 \rbrace) = \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$$

So they are pairwise independent. But the triple also meets only in face 1:

$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = \frac{1}{4} \ne \frac{1}{8} = P(A) P(B) P(C)$$

So they are not mutually independent. Given $A$ and $B$, the face must be 1, so $C$ is
certain.

</details>

**C4. (Medium, 4.4 and 4.10)** Urn 1 has 2 red and 3 blue balls; urn 2 has 4 red and 1
blue ball. You choose urn 1 with probability $\frac{1}{3}$ and urn 2 with probability
$\frac{2}{3}$, then draw one ball. It is red. What is the chance you chose urn 2?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Tree leaves for "red": urn 1 gives $\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{2}{15}$; urn 2 gives
$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{8}{15}$.

$$P(\text{urn 2} \mid \text{red}) = \frac{8/15}{2/15 + 8/15} = \frac{8}{10} = 0.8$$

</details>

**C5. (Medium, 4.6)** A machine is either working normally (each item defective with
probability $0.01$) or broken (each item defective with probability $0.1$). You think
it is broken with probability $0.05$. You inspect 2 items, and both are defective.
Assume the items are independent given the machine's state. What is the chance the
machine is broken?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each defective item has likelihood ratio $\frac{0.1}{0.01} = 10$ for "broken". Prior odds:
$\frac{0.05}{0.95} = \frac{1}{19}$.

$$\text{posterior odds} = \frac{1}{19} \times 10 \times 10 = \frac{100}{19} \approx 5.26, \qquad P(\text{broken}) = \frac{100}{119} \approx 0.840$$

</details>

**C6. (Medium, 4.9)** Half of the students in a class prepared well. A well-prepared
student passes each of two exams with probability $0.9$; others pass each with
probability $0.4$. Given a student's preparation, the two results are independent.
Find $P(\text{pass exam 1})$, $P(\text{pass both})$ and
$P(\text{pass exam 2} \mid \text{pass exam 1})$. Are the two results independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{pass 1}) = 0.9 \times 0.5 + 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.65$$

$$P(\text{pass both}) = 0.9^2 \times 0.5 + 0.4^2 \times 0.5 = 0.405 + 0.08 = 0.485$$

$$P(\text{pass 2} \mid \text{pass 1}) = \frac{0.485}{0.65} \approx 0.746$$

Since $0.746 \ne 0.65$ (and $0.485 \ne 0.65^2 = 0.4225$), the results are dependent. They are
independent only given the preparation, a common cause.

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, 4.11.5, proof)** Gambler's ruin: you start with $k$ taka and bet 1 taka on
each flip of a fair coin, stopping when you reach 0 or $N$ taka. Let $w_k$ be the chance
of reaching $N$. Prove that $w_k = \frac{k}{N}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Condition on the first flip (law of total probability). After it, the game starts fresh
from $k + 1$ or $k - 1$, because the flips are independent:

$$w_k = \tfrac{1}{2} w_{k+1} + \tfrac{1}{2} w_{k-1} \quad (0 \lt k \lt N), \qquad w_0 = 0, \quad w_N = 1$$

Multiply by 2 and rearrange: $w_{k+1} - w_k = w_k - w_{k-1}$. So all the steps
$d = w_{k+1} - w_k$ are equal, and $w_k = w_0 + k d = k d$. From $w_N = 1$ we get
$N d = 1$, so $d = \frac{1}{N}$ and:

$$w_k = \frac{k}{N}$$

</details>

**H2. (Hard, 4.1 and 4.7, proof)** Suppose $P(A) \gt 0$ and $P(B) \gt 0$. Prove that if
$P(A \mid B) \gt P(A)$, then $P(B \mid A) \gt P(B)$. (If $B$ makes $A$ more likely, then $A$
makes $B$ more likely.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Multiply $P(A \mid B) \gt P(A)$ by $P(B) \gt 0$:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B) \gt P(A) P(B)$$

Now divide by $P(A) \gt 0$:

$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \gt P(B)$$

So "positive association" is symmetric, just like independence. (A good clue for a
disease raises the chance of disease, and the disease raises the chance of the clue.)

</details>

**H3. (Hard, 4.4)** Three cards are in a hat: one is red on both sides, one is blue on
both sides, and one is red on one side and blue on the other. You pick a card at random
and look at one random side. It is red. What is the chance that the other side is also
red? (Many people say $\frac{1}{2}$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The other side is red exactly when you picked the red-red card. Likelihoods of "a red
side is showing": red-red card $1$, red-blue card $\frac{1}{2}$, blue-blue card $0$. Equal
priors cancel:

$$P(\text{red-red} \mid \text{red showing}) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1}{2} + 0} = \frac{2}{3}$$

Counting sides makes it clear: there are 3 red sides, equally likely to be the one you
see, and 2 of them belong to the red-red card. The answer $\frac{1}{2}$ forgets that the
red-red card is twice as likely to show red.

</details>

**H4. (Hard, 4.3)** You roll two fair dice again and again. What is the probability
that a sum of 7 appears before a sum of 6? Use first-step analysis.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

On each roll, a 7 has chance $\frac{6}{36}$, a 6 has chance $\frac{5}{36}$, and anything else
($\frac{25}{36}$) means "roll again", after which the situation is the same as at the start.
Let $w$ be the answer. Condition on the first roll:

$$w = \frac{6}{36} \cdot 1 + \frac{5}{36} \cdot 0 + \frac{25}{36} \cdot w$$

So $\frac{11}{36} w = \frac{6}{36}$ and:

$$w = \frac{6}{11} \approx 0.545$$

In words: only the rolls that end the game matter, and among them, 7 has 6 chances
against 5 for the 6.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Monty Hall, 4.11.1)** Simulate 100,000 Monty Hall games with a host who knows
where the car is. Estimate the winning chance of "always stick" and "always switch",
and compare with $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{3}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_games = 100_000
car = rng.integers(0, 3, size=n_games)
pick = rng.integers(0, 3, size=n_games)
# The host opens a goat door that is not your pick. Switching wins exactly when
# your first pick was wrong, because the host must leave the car door closed.
stick_wins = np.mean(car == pick)
switch_wins = np.mean(car != pick)
print(f"stick:  {stick_wins:.4f}  (exact {1 / 3:.4f})")
print(f"switch: {switch_wins:.4f}  (exact {2 / 3:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
stick:  0.3312  (exact 0.3333)
switch: 0.6688  (exact 0.6667)
```

The code uses the key fact from [4.11.1](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.1-Monty-Hall.md):
with a knowing host, switching wins whenever the first pick was wrong. That topic's
"Simulate It" section also simulates the host's choice door by door.

</details>

**S2. (Medical testing, 4.11.2)** For prevalences $0.001$, $0.01$, $0.1$ and $0.5$,
simulate 1,000,000 people tested with sensitivity $0.99$ and false positive rate $0.05$.
Print the simulated and exact $P(D \mid +)$ for each prevalence.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people, sens, fpr = 1_000_000, 0.99, 0.05
for prev in [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5]:
    sick = rng.random(n_people) < prev
    positive = rng.random(n_people) < np.where(sick, sens, fpr)
    exact = sens * prev / (sens * prev + fpr * (1 - prev))
    print(f"prevalence {prev:<6}: P(D | +) simulation {sick[positive].mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
prevalence 0.001 : P(D | +) simulation 0.0196, exact 0.0194
prevalence 0.01  : P(D | +) simulation 0.1667, exact 0.1667
prevalence 0.1   : P(D | +) simulation 0.6876, exact 0.6875
prevalence 0.5   : P(D | +) simulation 0.9519, exact 0.9519
```

The same test goes from almost useless (about 2%) to very convincing (about 95%) as
the prior grows.

</details>
