# 4.3 Law of Total Probability

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.3 Law of Total Probability

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [4.2 Multiplication Rule and Chain Rule](4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)

> **Big idea:** To find the chance of $A$, split the world into cases, find the chance of $A$ in each case, and take the average weighted by how likely each case is.

---

## Why It Matters

Often you cannot find $P(A)$ directly, but you can find it easily **if you knew
which case you are in**. The **law of total probability** lets you "pretend to
know" the case, solve each case, then combine the answers. It is the "divide and
conquer" of probability. It also gives the denominator of Bayes' theorem in
[4.4](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md), and it is the main tool for puzzles like
[gambler's ruin](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md).

## Intuition

A factory has three machines that make phones. Machine 1 makes half of all phones,
machine 2 makes 30%, and machine 3 makes 20%. Machine 1 is the newest: only 1% of
its phones are broken. Machine 2 breaks 2%, and the old machine 3 breaks 3%. What
fraction of **all** phones are broken?

Imagine 1,000 phones:

- Machine 1 makes 500 phones, and 1% of 500 is 5 broken.
- Machine 2 makes 300 phones, and 2% of 300 is 6 broken.
- Machine 3 makes 200 phones, and 3% of 200 is 6 broken.

So $5 + 6 + 6 = 17$ broken phones out of 1,000, which is $0.017$. You never needed
to know the machine of any one phone. You split into cases, solved each case, and
added the pieces.

Notice that $0.017$ is a **weighted average** of the three rates $0.01$, $0.02$,
$0.03$. It is closer to $0.01$ because machine 1, with the lowest rate, has the
biggest weight.

## Picture

```text
   Ω  cut into three cases B1, B2, B3 (a partition)

  +----------------------+---------------+------------+
  |          B1          |      B2       |     B3     |
  |         0.5          |      0.3      |    0.2     |
  |                      |               |            |
  |   +------------------+---------------+------+     |
  |   |      A ∩ B1      |    A ∩ B2     |A ∩ B3|     |
  |   +------------------+---------------+------+     |
  |                      |               |            |
  +----------------------+---------------+------------+

   A is cut into three pieces that do not overlap:
   P(A) = P(A ∩ B1) + P(A ∩ B2) + P(A ∩ B3)
```

The cases cut $A$ into pieces. Add the pieces to get all of $A$.

## Definition

A **partition** of $\Omega$ is a list of events $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_n$ that do not
overlap (they are pairwise disjoint) and together cover all of $\Omega$. So exactly
one of them happens. (See [1.7 Partitions](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.7-Partitions.md).)

**Law of total probability.** If $B_1, \dots, B_n$ is a partition and every
$P(B_i) \gt 0$, then for any event $A$:

$$
P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \thinspace P(B_i)
$$

where:

- $P(B_i)$ is the chance of case $i$ (the **weight** of the case),
- $P(A \mid B_i)$ is the chance of $A$ inside case $i$.

**In words:** the overall chance of $A$ is the average of its chance in each case,
weighted by how likely each case is.

The most used version has just two cases, $B$ and $B^c$:

$$
P(A) = P(A \mid B) \thinspace P(B) + P(A \mid B^c) \thinspace P(B^c)
$$

**In words:** either $B$ happens or it does not; add the chance of $A$ along each
route.

The same law works for a countably infinite partition $B_1, B_2, \dots$, with an
infinite sum.

## Why It Is True

Since exactly one $B_i$ happens, every outcome in $A$ lies in exactly one piece
$A \cap B_i$. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= (A \cap B_1) \cup (A \cap B_2) \cup \dots \cup (A \cap B_n) && \text{(the } B_i \text{ cover } \Omega \text{)} \\
P(A) &= P(A \cap B_1) + P(A \cap B_2) + \dots + P(A \cap B_n) && \text{(pieces are disjoint; axiom 3)} \\
&= P(A \mid B_1) P(B_1) + \dots + P(A \mid B_n) P(B_n) && \text{(multiplication rule on each piece)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**A useful consequence.** The weights $P(B_i)$ are non-negative and add up to 1.
So $P(A)$ is a weighted average of the numbers $P(A \mid B_i)$, and a weighted
average always lies between the smallest and the largest number:

$$
\min_i P(A \mid B_i) \le P(A) \le \max_i P(A \mid B_i)
$$

**In words:** the overall rate is never below the lowest case rate or above the
highest case rate. This is a quick check for every answer.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Factory

**Problem.** Use the factory from the Intuition section. Machines 1, 2, 3 make 50%,
30%, 20% of the phones, with broken rates 1%, 2%, 3%. Find the probability that a
random phone is broken.

**Solution.**

1. Cases: $B_1, B_2, B_3$ = "made by machine 1, 2, 3". They form a partition.
2. Weights: $P(B_1) = 0.5$, $P(B_2) = 0.3$, $P(B_3) = 0.2$. They add to 1.
3. Case rates: $P(D \mid B_1) = 0.01$, $P(D \mid B_2) = 0.02$, $P(D \mid B_3) = 0.03$,
   where $D$ = "broken".
4. Apply the law:

$$
P(D) = 0.01 \times 0.5 + 0.02 \times 0.3 + 0.03 \times 0.2 = 0.005 + 0.006 + 0.006 = 0.017
$$

**Answer.** $P(D) = 0.017$, so 1.7% of all phones are broken.

**Check.** $0.017$ lies between the lowest rate $0.01$ and the highest $0.03$, as it
must. In [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md) we turn this around and ask:
given a broken phone, which machine made it?

### Example 2: Is the Second Card an Ace

**Problem.** Deal two cards from a shuffled deck, without replacement. You do
**not** look at the first card. What is the chance that the second card is an ace?

**Solution.**

1. The second card depends on the first, so split into two cases:
   $A_1$ = "first card is an ace" and $A_1^c$ = "first card is not an ace".
2. Weights: $P(A_1) = \frac{4}{52}$ and $P(A_1^c) = \frac{48}{52}$.
3. If the first was an ace, 3 aces remain among 51 cards: $P(A_2 \mid A_1) = \frac{3}{51}$.
4. If the first was not an ace, 4 aces remain among 51 cards: $P(A_2 \mid A_1^c) = \frac{4}{51}$.
5. Apply the two-case law:

$$
P(A_2) = \frac{3}{51} \times \frac{4}{52} + \frac{4}{51} \times \frac{48}{52} = \frac{12}{2652} + \frac{192}{2652} = \frac{204}{2652} = \frac{1}{13}
$$

**Answer.** $P(A_2) = \frac{1}{13}$, exactly the same as for the first card.

**Check.** This makes sense by symmetry. Before you look at anything, the second
card of a shuffled deck is just as likely to be any of the 52 cards as the first
card is. Information you never see cannot change your probability.

### Example 3: Packet Loss on Two Routes

**Problem.** A router sends 60% of packets along route A and 40% along route B.
Route A loses 2% of its packets, and route B loses 5%. What fraction of all
packets is lost? If the company moves all traffic to route A, how much does loss
drop?

**Solution.**

1. Let $L$ = "packet is lost". Cases: route A or route B, with weights $0.6$ and $0.4$.
2. Apply the law:

$$
P(L) = 0.02 \times 0.6 + 0.05 \times 0.4 = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032
$$

3. With all traffic on route A, $P(L) = 0.02$.

**Answer.** Today 3.2% of packets are lost. Moving everything to route A lowers
this to 2%, a drop of $1.2$ percentage points.

**Check.** The weights now are $1$ and $0$, so the weighted average equals route A's
rate. The law handles "what if" questions by changing only the weights.

## Simulate It

The code deals two cards many times and looks at the second card, to check the
surprising answer $\frac{1}{13}$ from Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 2_000_000

# Cards are numbered 0..51; cards 0, 1, 2, 3 are the four aces.
first = rng.integers(0, 52, size=n_trials)
# The second card is uniform over the 51 cards that are left.
second = rng.integers(0, 51, size=n_trials)
second = second + (second >= first)   # skip over the card already dealt
first_ace = first < 4
second_ace = second < 4

print(f"P(2nd ace):                  simulation {second_ace.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 13:.4f}")
print(f"P(2nd ace | 1st ace):        simulation {second_ace[first_ace].mean():.4f}, exact {3 / 51:.4f}")
print(f"P(2nd ace | 1st not ace):    simulation {second_ace[~first_ace].mean():.4f}, exact {4 / 51:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(2nd ace):                  simulation 0.0767, exact 0.0769
P(2nd ace | 1st ace):        simulation 0.0586, exact 0.0588
P(2nd ace | 1st not ace):    simulation 0.0782, exact 0.0784
```

The two case rates differ, but their weighted average is exactly $\frac{1}{13}$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using cases that overlap or miss something.** The $B_i$ must be a partition.
  If cases overlap, some outcomes are counted twice; if they miss part of $\Omega$,
  some are not counted.
- **Forgetting the weights.** $P(A)$ is **not** the plain average of the case rates.
  In the factory, the plain average of $0.01, 0.02, 0.03$ is $0.02$, but the true
  answer is $0.017$.
- **Using $P(B_i \mid A)$ instead of $P(A \mid B_i)$.** The law needs the chance of
  $A$ **inside each case**. Turning that around is Bayes' theorem, which comes next.
- **Answer outside the range.** If your $P(A)$ is smaller than every $P(A \mid B_i)$
  or bigger than every one, there is a mistake.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a **mixture model** says each data point comes from
  one of several hidden groups, and its probability is a weighted sum over the
  groups ([10.8 Mixture Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)).
  In Bayes' theorem, the law gives the "evidence" in the denominator.
- **Computer science:** the average-case cost of an algorithm splits on the cases of
  the input, such as "the pivot is good" or "the pivot is bad"
  ([21.3.3 Average-Case Analysis](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.3-Average-Case-Analysis.md)).
- **Random processes:** **first-step analysis** conditions on what happens in the
  first step, as in [4.11.5 Gambler's Ruin](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.5-Gamblers-Ruin.md).
- **Business:** total failure rate of products from several suppliers; total
  click rate from several kinds of users.
- **Everyday life:** "Will I be late? If it rains, likely; if not, unlikely; and rain
  is 30% likely." You already do this in your head.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** The chance of rain tomorrow is $0.3$. If it rains, you are late with
probability $0.4$. If it does not rain, you are late with probability $0.1$. What
is the chance you are late?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{late}) = 0.4 \times 0.3 + 0.1 \times 0.7 = 0.12 + 0.07 = 0.19$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** In a class, 40% of students studied a lot and 60% did not. Those who
studied a lot pass with probability $0.9$; the others pass with probability $0.5$.
What fraction of the class passes? Check that your answer is between the two case
rates.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{pass}) = 0.9 \times 0.4 + 0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.36 + 0.30 = 0.66$$

And $0.5 \le 0.66 \le 0.9$, as it must be.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Urn 1 has 3 red and 2 blue balls. Urn 2 has 2 red and 4 blue balls.
You move one random ball from urn 1 into urn 2, then draw one random ball from
urn 2. What is the chance it is red?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Split on the color of the moved ball. If it is red (chance $\frac{3}{5}$), urn 2 has
3 red out of 7. If it is blue (chance $\frac{2}{5}$), urn 2 has 2 red out of 7.

$$P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{7} \times \frac{3}{5} + \frac{2}{7} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{9}{35} + \frac{4}{35} = \frac{13}{35} \approx 0.371$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Prove that if $B_1, \dots, B_n$ is a partition with every
$P(B_i) \gt 0$, then $P(A) \le \max_i P(A \mid B_i)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $M = \max_i P(A \mid B_i)$. Every term satisfies $P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i) \le M P(B_i)$, so:

$$P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i) \le \sum_{i} M P(B_i) = M \sum_{i} P(B_i) = M$$

The last step uses that the $P(B_i)$ add up to 1, because the $B_i$ form a partition.
The proof for the minimum is the same with $\ge$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Roll a fair die and call the result $N$. Then flip $N$ fair coins.
What is the probability that all $N$ coins show heads?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Split on the value of the die. Given $N = n$, all heads has chance $(\frac{1}{2})^n$,
and each $n$ from 1 to 6 has weight $\frac{1}{6}$:

$$P(\text{all heads}) = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{n=1}^{6} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = \frac{1}{6} \left(1 - \frac{1}{64}\right) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{63}{64} = \frac{63}{384} = \frac{21}{128} \approx 0.164$$

The sum is a geometric series: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{64} = 1 - \frac{1}{64}$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the answer to Problem 5 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
n_coins = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
# All N coins are heads with chance (1/2)^N, so draw one uniform number per trial.
all_heads = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.5 ** n_coins
print(f"Simulation: {all_heads.mean():.4f}, exact {21 / 128:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.1641, exact 0.1641
```

</details>

## Summary

- Split the world into cases that do not overlap and cover everything (a
  partition).
- Find the chance of $A$ in each case, then take the weighted average, with weights
  equal to the case probabilities.
- The answer always lies between the smallest and the largest case rate.
- The two-case version, with $B$ and $B^c$, is the one you will use most.
- This law gives the denominator of Bayes' theorem, which comes next.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i) \thinspace P(B_i)}
$$
