# 4.6 Sequential Bayesian Updating

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.6 Sequential Bayesian Updating

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.5 Odds Form of Bayes](4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)

> **Big idea:** Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior: you can learn from evidence one piece at a time, and if the pieces are independent given each hypothesis, the order does not matter and the result is the same as using everything at once.

---

## Why It Matters

Evidence rarely arrives all at once. A doctor orders a second test, a spam filter
reads one word after another, a robot gets a new sensor reading every tenth of a
second. **Sequential updating** says how to fold in each new piece without starting
over. It is how learning works over time, in people and in machines.

## Intuition

A friend has two coins in a pocket. One is fair. The other is bent and lands heads
80% of the time. The friend takes one at random, so you start at 50% for "bent".
Then the friend flips it, and you watch the flips one by one: H, H, T, H, H.

Use the odds form from [4.5](4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md). A head is
$\frac{0.8}{0.5} = 1.6$ times as likely with the bent coin. A tail is
$\frac{0.2}{0.5} = 0.4$ times as likely. Start with odds 1 (bent against fair) and
multiply after each flip:

| After | Multiply by | Odds (bent : fair) | P(bent) |
|---|---|---|---|
| start | | 1 | 0.500 |
| H | 1.6 | 1.6 | 0.615 |
| H H | 1.6 | 2.56 | 0.719 |
| H H T | 0.4 | 1.024 | 0.506 |
| H H T H | 1.6 | 1.6384 | 0.621 |
| H H T H H | 1.6 | 2.62144 | 0.724 |

After each flip, your new belief becomes the starting belief for the next flip.
Heads push you toward "bent", the tail pushes you back. If you had waited and used
all five flips at once, you would get the same final number, because
$1.6 \times 1.6 \times 0.4 \times 1.6 \times 1.6 = 2.62144$ in any order.

## Picture

```text
  after          P(bent)   0         0.25      0.5       0.75      1
                           |---------|---------|---------|---------|
  start           0.500    ####################
  H               0.615    #########################
  H H             0.719    #############################
  H H T           0.506    ####################
  H H T H         0.621    #########################
  H H T H H       0.724    #############################

  each row:  prior (row above)  x  likelihood of new flip  -->  posterior
```

Belief moves up with each head and down with the tail; every posterior is the prior of the next row.

## Definition

Suppose evidence $E_1, E_2, \dots, E_n$ arrives one piece at a time. **Sequential
updating** applies Bayes' theorem once per piece, using the last posterior as the
new prior:

$$
P(H \mid E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_k) \propto P(E_k \mid H \cap E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_{k-1}) \thinspace P(H \mid E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_{k-1})
$$

**In words:** new belief is proportional to the old belief times how likely the
newest evidence is, given the hypothesis and everything seen before.

Very often the pieces of evidence are **conditionally independent given the
hypothesis**: once you know which hypothesis is true, one piece tells you nothing
about the next. (Coin flips are like this: once you know which coin it is, the flips
are independent.) This is defined in
[4.9 Conditional Independence](4.9-Conditional-Independence.md). Then
$P(E_k \mid H \cap E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_{k-1}) = P(E_k \mid H)$, and all the updates
together give the **batch** formula:

$$
P(H \mid E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n) \propto P(H) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(E_i \mid H)
$$

**In words:** the final posterior is the prior times the likelihood of each piece
of evidence, then normalized.

In odds form, with $\text{LR}_i = \frac{P(E_i \mid H)}{P(E_i \mid H^c)}$:

$$
\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times \text{LR}_1 \times \text{LR}_2 \times \dots \times \text{LR}_n
$$

**In words:** every independent piece of evidence multiplies the odds by its own
likelihood ratio. The order of the factors does not matter.

## Why It Is True

**One step.** Conditional probabilities are real probabilities
([4.1](4.1-Conditional-Probability.md)). So Bayes' theorem also holds when
everything is conditioned on the old evidence $F = E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_{k-1}$:

$$
P(H \mid E_k \cap F) = \frac{P(E_k \mid H \cap F) \thinspace P(H \mid F)}{P(E_k \mid F)}
$$

Here $P(H \mid F)$ is the old posterior, now acting as the prior. This is exactly the
sequential rule.

**Sequential equals batch.** Apply the [chain rule](4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)
to the evidence, inside the world where $H$ is true:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n \mid H) &= P(E_1 \mid H) \thinspace P(E_2 \mid H \cap E_1) \cdots P(E_n \mid H \cap E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_{n-1}) && \text{(chain rule given } H \text{)} \\
&= P(E_1 \mid H) \thinspace P(E_2 \mid H) \cdots P(E_n \mid H) && \text{(conditional independence given } H \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Put this into one big Bayes step with all the evidence:

$$
P(H \mid E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n) = \frac{P(H) \prod_{i} P(E_i \mid H)}{P(E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n)}
$$

The numerator is the same product that the step-by-step updates build up, one
factor at a time. Both results are probabilities over the hypotheses that add up to
1, so their denominators must match too. A product does not depend on the order of
its factors, so **the order of the evidence does not matter**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Positive Medical Tests

**Problem.** A disease has prevalence 1%. A test detects 99% of sick people and gives
a false positive for 5% of healthy people. A patient tests positive, and then tests
positive again on a second, independent test of the same kind. What is the chance
the patient is sick after each test?

**Solution.**

1. Prior: $P(D) = 0.01$. Each positive has likelihood ratio $\frac{0.99}{0.05} = 19.8$.
2. First update, as in [4.5](4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md): odds
   $\frac{1}{99} \times 19.8 = 0.2$, so $P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.2}{1.2} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$.
3. This posterior is the new prior. Second update:

$$
\text{odds} = 0.2 \times 19.8 = 3.96, \qquad P(D \mid +, +) = \frac{3.96}{1 + 3.96} = \frac{3.96}{4.96} \approx 0.798
$$

4. Natural-frequency check. Of 10,000 people, 594 test positive: 99 sick and 495
   healthy. Test those 594 again: $99 \times 0.99 = 98.01$ sick and
   $495 \times 0.05 = 24.75$ healthy test positive again.
   $\frac{98.01}{98.01 + 24.75} = \frac{98.01}{122.76} \approx 0.798$.

**Answer.** About 17% after one positive test, about 80% after two.

**Check.** This needs the two test results to be independent **given** the disease
status. If the false positive comes from something in the patient's blood that
fools the test every time, the second test adds little. Doctors often use a
**different kind** of test for confirmation for this reason.

### Example 2: Searching for Lost Keys

**Problem.** You lost your keys. You think they are in your bag with probability
$0.6$ and in your office with probability $0.4$. A quick search of the bag finds the
keys with probability $0.7$ if they are there. You search the bag and do not find
them. Then you search the bag again and still do not find them. Where should you
look next?

**Solution.**

1. Prior: $P(\text{bag}) = 0.6$, $P(\text{office}) = 0.4$.
2. Likelihood of "not found in bag": $0.3$ if the keys are in the bag; $1$ if they
   are in the office.
3. First search fails:

$$
P(\text{bag} \mid \text{fail}) = \frac{0.6 \times 0.3}{0.6 \times 0.3 + 0.4 \times 1} = \frac{0.18}{0.58} \approx 0.310
$$

4. The posterior $0.310$ is the new prior (office: $0.690$). Second search fails:

$$
P(\text{bag} \mid \text{fail, fail}) = \frac{0.310 \times 0.3}{0.310 \times 0.3 + 0.690 \times 1} \approx \frac{0.0931}{0.7828} \approx 0.119
$$

5. Batch check: $\frac{0.6 \times 0.3^2}{0.6 \times 0.3^2 + 0.4} = \frac{0.054}{0.454} \approx 0.119$.

**Answer.** After two failed searches, the bag has only about 12%, and the office
about 88%. Go to the office.

**Check.** Not finding something is evidence too. Each failure multiplies the odds
for "bag" by $0.3$. Real search-and-rescue teams update a map of the sea this way
after every search.

## Simulate It

The code picks a coin, flips it 5 times, keeps only the runs that gave exactly
H, H, T, H, H, and checks the final belief $0.724$ from the Intuition section.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Prior: the bent coin (P(heads) = 0.8) is chosen with probability 0.5.
bent = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.5
p_heads = np.where(bent, 0.8, 0.5)

# Five flips per trial; given the coin, the flips are independent.
flips = rng.random((n_trials, 5)) < p_heads[:, None]     # True = heads
pattern = np.array([True, True, False, True, True])      # H H T H H
match = np.all(flips == pattern, axis=1)

estimate = bent[match].mean()
odds = 1.6 * 1.6 * 0.4 * 1.6 * 1.6                        # product of LRs
print(f"Runs with H H T H H: {match.sum():,}")
print(f"P(bent | H H T H H): simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {odds / (1 + odds):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Runs with H H T H H: 56,720
P(bent | H H T H H): simulation 0.7245, exact 0.7239
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the same evidence twice.** If you update on a test result and later
  "update" again on the same result, you count it twice and become overconfident.
  Each piece of evidence enters exactly once.
- **Assuming independence that is not there.** Multiplying likelihood ratios is
  correct only if the pieces are independent **given** each hypothesis. Two news
  stories copied from the same source are one piece of evidence, not two.
- **Starting from 0 or 1.** If your prior is exactly 0, every posterior is 0 no
  matter what you see. Keep a small probability for anything possible. (This is
  sometimes called **Cromwell's rule**.)
- **Ignoring "nothing happened".** A search that finds nothing, or a test that
  stays quiet, is evidence. Its likelihood ratio is usually below 1.
- **Thinking order matters.** With conditionally independent evidence, H then T
  gives the same belief as T then H. If your answer depends on the order, check
  your arithmetic.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** online learning updates a model with each new data
  point; Naive Bayes multiplies one likelihood per word; Thompson sampling in
  recommendation systems keeps a posterior per option and updates it after each
  click.
- **Robotics:** robot localization and the Kalman filter repeat "predict, then
  update with the new sensor reading" many times a second.
- **Computer science:** a spam filter keeps updating its word probabilities as users
  mark emails; an anomaly detector updates its belief that a server is failing with
  each new log line.
- **Medicine:** a positive screening test followed by a confirmation test.
- **Everyday life:** changing your opinion step by step as news arrives, without
  overreacting to any single report
  ([22.2 Bayesian Updating in Daily Life](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Your prior odds for a hypothesis are 1 to 3. You see two pieces of
evidence, independent given the hypothesis, with likelihood ratios 2 and 3. What is
your final probability?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{posterior odds} = \frac{1}{3} \times 2 \times 3 = 2$$

So the probability is $\frac{2}{1 + 2} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You see evidence with likelihood ratio 4, and later evidence with
likelihood ratio $\frac{1}{4}$ (both independent given the hypothesis). Where does
your belief end up? Does the order matter?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The odds are multiplied by $4 \times \frac{1}{4} = 1$, so your belief ends exactly
where it started. The order does not matter, because $4 \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4} \times 4$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A bag holds a fair coin and a two-headed coin. You pick one at random
and flip it $n$ times. All $n$ flips are heads. Find $P(\text{two-headed} \mid n \text{ heads})$.
What is it for $n = 5$? What happens if the next flip is tails?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds are 1. Each head has likelihood ratio $\frac{1}{1/2} = 2$, so the odds are
$2^n$:

$$P(\text{two-headed} \mid n \text{ heads}) = \frac{2^n}{2^n + 1}$$

For $n = 5$: $\frac{32}{33} \approx 0.970$. A tail has probability 0 with the
two-headed coin, so its likelihood ratio is 0 and the posterior drops to exactly 0.
One tail proves the coin is fair.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In Example 2 (lost keys), find a formula for $P(\text{bag})$ after $k$
failed searches of the bag. After how many failed searches does the bag drop below
5%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each failure multiplies the bag's prior-times-likelihood by $0.3$:

$$P(\text{bag} \mid k \text{ fails}) = \frac{0.6 \times 0.3^k}{0.6 \times 0.3^k + 0.4}$$

For $k = 2$: $\frac{0.054}{0.454} \approx 0.119$. For $k = 3$: $\frac{0.0162}{0.4162} \approx 0.039$.
So after 3 failed searches the bag is below 5%.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose the second test in Example 1 is useless: it is just a copy of the
first result, so $E_2$ happens exactly when $E_1$ happens. Prove that then
$P(D \mid E_1 \cap E_2) = P(D \mid E_1)$. What is the wrong answer you would get by
multiplying the two likelihood ratios?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If $E_2$ happens exactly when $E_1$ happens, then $E_1 \cap E_2 = E_1$ as events. So:

$$P(D \mid E_1 \cap E_2) = \frac{P(D \cap E_1 \cap E_2)}{P(E_1 \cap E_2)} = \frac{P(D \cap E_1)}{P(E_1)} = P(D \mid E_1) \approx 0.167$$

Multiplying the two likelihood ratios would give odds $\frac{1}{99} \times 19.8^2 = 3.96$
and a probability of about $0.798$, far too confident. The mistake is that $E_2$ is
not independent of $E_1$ given $D$: once you know $E_1$, $E_2$ is certain, so its
real likelihood ratio is $\frac{1}{1} = 1$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Example 1: give people a disease with probability $0.01$,
apply two independent tests, and estimate $P(D \mid +)$ and $P(D \mid +, +)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 2_000_000
sick = rng.random(n_people) < 0.01
p_pos = np.where(sick, 0.99, 0.05)
test1 = rng.random(n_people) < p_pos
test2 = rng.random(n_people) < p_pos      # independent given sick or healthy
print(f"P(D | +):    simulation {sick[test1].mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 6:.4f}")
print(f"P(D | +, +): simulation {sick[test1 & test2].mean():.4f}, exact {3.96 / 4.96:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(D | +):    simulation 0.1655, exact 0.1667
P(D | +, +): simulation 0.7974, exact 0.7984
```

</details>

## Summary

- Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. Update one piece of evidence at a time.
- If the evidence is conditionally independent given the hypothesis, the final
  posterior is the prior times all the likelihoods, normalized.
- In odds form, each piece multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio. The order
  does not matter.
- Count every piece of evidence once, and check that the pieces really are
  independent given the hypothesis.
- "Nothing found" is evidence too.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(H \mid E_1 \cap \dots \cap E_n) \propto P(H) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(E_i \mid H) \quad \text{(evidence conditionally independent given } H \text{)}}
$$
