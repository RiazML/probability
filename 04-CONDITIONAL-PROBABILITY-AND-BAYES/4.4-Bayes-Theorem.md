# 4.4 Bayes' Theorem

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.4 Bayes' Theorem

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [4.3 Law of Total Probability](4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)

> **Big idea:** To find how likely a cause is after you see its effect, take how likely the cause was before (the prior), multiply by how well it explains the effect (the likelihood), and rescale so all causes add up to 1.

---

## Why It Matters

We usually know probabilities in the "wrong" direction. A test maker knows
$P(\text{positive} \mid \text{sick})$, but the patient wants
$P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive})$. A spam filter's training data gives
$P(\text{word} \mid \text{spam})$, but the filter must decide
$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{word})$. **Bayes' theorem** turns one direction into the
other. It is the rule for learning from evidence, so it sits at the heart of
medicine, law, science, spam filters and much of machine learning.

## Intuition

A city has two taxi companies. 85% of its taxis are green and 15% are blue. One
night a taxi hits a car and drives away. A witness says: "The taxi was blue." In
tests under the same night conditions, the witness names the correct color 80% of
the time, for both colors. How likely is it that the taxi really was blue?

Most people say "80%". Let us count instead. Imagine this accident happened 100
times, each time with a random taxi from the city:

- About 15 times the taxi is blue. The witness says "blue" in 80% of these: **12** times.
- About 85 times the taxi is green. The witness wrongly says "blue" in 20% of
  these: **17** times.

So the witness says "blue" $12 + 17 = 29$ times, and the taxi really is blue in only
12 of them. The answer is $\frac{12}{29} \approx 0.41$, not $0.8$.

Here is the key. The witness is good, but green taxis are so common that the
witness's **few mistakes on many green taxis** (17) outnumber the **many correct
answers on few blue taxis** (12). Bayes' theorem is just this counting, written as
a formula. It always mixes two things:

1. **How common was the cause before the evidence?** (15 blue in 100: the prior.)
2. **How well does each cause explain the evidence?** (A blue taxi gives "blue" 80% of
   the time, a green taxi 20%: the likelihood.)

## Picture

```text
                            +-- 0.8 --> says blue    15 x 0.8 = 12  <- keep
           +-- 0.15 --> Blue
           |                +-- 0.2 --> says green   15 x 0.2 =  3
 100 taxis +
           |                +-- 0.2 --> says blue    85 x 0.2 = 17  <- keep
           +-- 0.85 --> Green
                            +-- 0.8 --> says green   85 x 0.8 = 68

   The witness said "blue", so keep only the paths that end in "says blue":

   prior x likelihood    blue:  15 x 0.8 = 12   ############
                         green: 85 x 0.2 = 17   #################

   divide by the total 12 + 17 = 29

   posterior             blue:  12 / 29 = 0.41
                         green: 17 / 29 = 0.59
```

Bayes' theorem keeps the paths that agree with the evidence, then rescales them to add up to 1.

## Definition

Let $H$ be a **hypothesis** (a possible cause, such as "the taxi is blue") and $E$
be the **evidence** we observed (such as "the witness says blue"), with
$P(E) \gt 0$. **Bayes' theorem** says:

$$
P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H)}{P(E)}
$$

Each part has a name:

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $P(H)$ | **prior** | how likely $H$ was **before** seeing the evidence |
| $P(E \mid H)$ | **likelihood** | how likely the evidence is **if** $H$ is true |
| $P(E)$ | **evidence** (or marginal likelihood) | how likely the evidence is overall |
| $P(H \mid E)$ | **posterior** | how likely $H$ is **after** seeing the evidence |

**In words:** the posterior is the prior times the likelihood, divided by the total
chance of the evidence.

Usually we find $P(E)$ with the [law of total probability](4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md).
With two hypotheses, $H$ and $H^c$:

$$
P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H)}{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H) + P(E \mid H^c) \thinspace P(H^c)}
$$

With many hypotheses $H_1, \dots, H_n$ that form a partition (exactly one is true),
for each $j$:

$$
P(H_j \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_j) \thinspace P(H_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(E \mid H_i) \thinspace P(H_i)}
$$

**In words:** the posterior of each hypothesis is its share of the total
"prior times likelihood".

The denominator is the same for every hypothesis. It only rescales. So the short
form is:

$$
\underbrace{P(H_j \mid E)}_{\text{posterior}} \propto \underbrace{P(E \mid H_j)}_{\text{likelihood}} \times \underbrace{P(H_j)}_{\text{prior}}
$$

**In words:** posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Compute
"likelihood times prior" for every hypothesis, then divide each by their sum.

> **Tip:** Bayes' theorem in three steps: **(1)** write the prior of each hypothesis,
> **(2)** multiply by the likelihood of the evidence, **(3)** divide by the total so
> the results add up to 1. The table method in Example 2 does exactly this.

## Why It Is True

Write $P(H \cap E)$ in two ways with the
[multiplication rule](4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md), then solve:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H \mid E) \thinspace P(E) &= P(H \cap E) && \text{(multiplication rule, one way)} \\
&= P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H) && \text{(multiplication rule, the other way)} \\
P(H \mid E) &= \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H)}{P(E)} && \text{(divide by } P(E) \gt 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For the expanded form, replace $P(E)$ with the law of total probability over the
partition $H_1, \dots, H_n$:

$$
P(E) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(E \mid H_i) \thinspace P(H_i)
$$

That is the whole proof. The theorem is short; its power is in how often it
applies.

**Check that the posteriors add up to 1.** Every posterior has the same
denominator, and the numerators add up to exactly that denominator:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(H_j \mid E) = \frac{\sum_{j} P(E \mid H_j) P(H_j)}{\sum_{i} P(E \mid H_i) P(H_i)} = 1
$$

**Natural frequencies give the same answer.** Imagine $N$ cases. About $N P(H_i)$ of
them have hypothesis $H_i$, and of those about $N P(H_i) P(E \mid H_i)$ show the
evidence. The fraction of evidence-showing cases that have $H_j$ is the Bayes
formula with $N$ cancelled. This is exactly what we did with the 100 taxis.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Taxi, With the Formula

**Problem.** 15% of taxis are blue ($B$) and 85% are green. A witness says "blue"
($W$). The witness is right 80% of the time for each color. Find $P(B \mid W)$.

**Solution.**

1. Prior: $P(B) = 0.15$, so $P(B^c) = 0.85$.
2. Likelihoods: $P(W \mid B) = 0.8$ (correct answer) and $P(W \mid B^c) = 0.2$
   (mistake on a green taxi).
3. Evidence, by total probability:

$$
P(W) = 0.8 \times 0.15 + 0.2 \times 0.85 = 0.12 + 0.17 = 0.29
$$

4. Posterior:

$$
P(B \mid W) = \frac{0.8 \times 0.15}{0.29} = \frac{0.12}{0.29} \approx 0.414
$$

**Answer.** About 41%. The witness's report raised the chance of "blue" from 15% to
41%, but "green" is still more likely.

**Check.** It matches the 12 out of 29 from the Intuition section. The simulation
below agrees too. The posterior is between the prior ($0.15$) and the witness's
accuracy ($0.8$): good evidence moved us, but a strong prior held us back.

### Example 2: Which Machine Made the Broken Phone

**Problem.** In the factory of [4.3](4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md), machines 1, 2, 3
make 50%, 30%, 20% of the phones, with broken rates 1%, 2%, 3%. A customer returns a
broken phone. For each machine, what is the probability that it made the phone?

**Solution.** Use the **Bayes table**. One row per hypothesis:

| Machine | Prior $P(M_i)$ | Likelihood $P(D \mid M_i)$ | Prior x likelihood | Posterior $P(M_i \mid D)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5 | 0.01 | 0.005 | 0.005 / 0.017 = 0.294 |
| 2 | 0.3 | 0.02 | 0.006 | 0.006 / 0.017 = 0.353 |
| 3 | 0.2 | 0.03 | 0.006 | 0.006 / 0.017 = 0.353 |
| **Sum** | 1 | (no rule) | **0.017** | 1.000 |

1. Fill in the priors and the likelihoods from the problem.
2. Multiply across each row: $0.5 \times 0.01 = 0.005$, $0.3 \times 0.02 = 0.006$,
   $0.2 \times 0.03 = 0.006$.
3. Add that column: $0.005 + 0.006 + 0.006 = 0.017$. This is $P(D)$, the evidence.
4. Divide each product by $0.017$ to get the posterior.

**Answer.** Machine 1: about 29%; machines 2 and 3: about 35% each.

**Check.** Machine 3 makes only 20% of the phones but about 35% of the broken ones,
because its broken rate is high. Machine 1 makes half of all phones but only about
29% of the broken ones. The posterior column adds to $0.294 + 0.353 + 0.353 = 1.000$.
Notice that the likelihood column does **not** need to add up to 1; it is not a
distribution over machines.

### Example 3: A Spam Filter Sees "Lottery"

**Problem.** 40% of the email a company receives is spam. The word "lottery"
appears in 10% of spam emails and in 0.5% of normal emails. An email contains
"lottery". What is the chance it is spam?

**Solution.**

1. Prior: $P(S) = 0.4$, $P(S^c) = 0.6$.
2. Likelihoods: $P(L \mid S) = 0.1$, $P(L \mid S^c) = 0.005$.
3. Prior times likelihood: spam $0.4 \times 0.1 = 0.04$; normal
   $0.6 \times 0.005 = 0.003$.
4. Evidence: $P(L) = 0.04 + 0.003 = 0.043$.
5. Posterior:

$$
P(S \mid L) = \frac{0.04}{0.043} \approx 0.930
$$

**Answer.** About 93%. One word moved the filter from 40% to 93%.

**Check.** The word is 20 times more common in spam ($\frac{0.1}{0.005} = 20$), so
it is strong evidence. That number, the **likelihood ratio**, is the star of the
next topic, [4.5 Odds Form of Bayes](4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md). A real filter
combines many words this way; see
[21.1.1 Probabilistic Classifiers and Naive Bayes](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md).

### Example 4: The Medical Test in One Line

**Problem.** A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 99% of sick people, and
gives a false positive for 5% of healthy people. You test positive. What is
$P(D \mid +)$?

**Solution.**

$$
P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0099 + 0.0495} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167
$$

**Answer.** About 17%, even though the test is "99% accurate" for sick people.

**Check.** Out of 10,000 people, 100 are sick and 99 of them test positive; 9,900
are healthy and 495 of them test positive. Only 99 of the 594 positives are sick.
The full story, with what to do next, is in
[4.11.2 Medical Testing](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.2-Medical-Testing.md).

## Simulate It

The code builds many random accidents, keeps only those where the witness said
"blue", and asks how often the taxi was really blue.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Prior: each taxi is blue with probability 0.15.
is_blue = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.15

# Likelihood: the witness says "blue" with probability 0.8 if blue, 0.2 if green.
p_says_blue = np.where(is_blue, 0.8, 0.2)
says_blue = rng.random(n_trials) < p_says_blue

# Posterior: among the "blue" reports, the fraction that were really blue.
estimate = is_blue[says_blue].mean()
exact = 0.12 / 0.29

print(f"Reports of blue:      {says_blue.mean():.4f}  (exact 0.2900)")
print(f"P(blue | says blue):  {estimate:.4f}  (exact {exact:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Reports of blue:      0.2899  (exact 0.2900)
P(blue | says blue):  0.4133  (exact 0.4138)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing $P(E \mid H)$ with $P(H \mid E)$.** "The witness is 80% accurate"
  means $P(\text{says blue} \mid \text{blue}) = 0.8$. It does **not** mean
  $P(\text{blue} \mid \text{says blue}) = 0.8$. This swap is called the **confusion
  of the inverse**; in court it is the
  [prosecutor's fallacy](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.3-Prosecutors-Fallacy.md).
- **Ignoring the prior (base rate neglect).** If a cause is rare, even strong
  evidence may leave it unlikely. Always ask: "How common was this before the
  evidence?"
- **Forgetting the other hypotheses in the denominator.** $P(E)$ must include
  **every** way the evidence can happen, such as the false positives from healthy
  people.
- **Expecting the likelihoods to add up to 1.** $P(E \mid H_1), P(E \mid H_2), \dots$ are
  chances of the **same** evidence under different hypotheses. They can add up to
  anything. Only the priors and the posteriors must add up to 1.
- **Using a prior of exactly 0 or 1.** Then no evidence can ever change your mind,
  because $0 \times \text{anything} = 0$. Keep a small chance for things that are
  possible.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Naive Bayes classifiers, Bayesian neural networks,
  and learning a parameter from data
  ([10.7 Bayes for Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)).
  A classifier that predicts $P(\text{class} \mid \text{input})$ is estimating a posterior.
- **Computer science:** spam and fraud detection, robot localization (where am I,
  given what my sensors see?), and probabilistic programming.
- **Medicine:** turning a test result into a chance of disease
  ([21.6 Medicine and Diagnostic Testing](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)).
- **Search and rescue:** searchers update a map of where a lost ship might be after
  each area is searched without success.
- **Everyday life:** changing your mind by the right amount when you hear news
  ([22.2 Bayesian Updating in Daily Life](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.2-Bayesian-Updating-in-Daily-Life.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $P(H) = 0.2$, $P(E \mid H) = 0.9$ and $P(E \mid H^c) = 0.3$. Find
$P(E)$ and $P(H \mid E)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(E) = 0.9 \times 0.2 + 0.3 \times 0.8 = 0.18 + 0.24 = 0.42$$

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{0.18}{0.42} = \frac{3}{7} \approx 0.429$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Box 1 has 3 red balls and 1 blue ball. Box 2 has 1 red ball and 3 blue
balls. You pick a box at random (each with chance $\frac{1}{2}$) and draw one ball.
It is red. What is the chance that you picked box 1?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{box 1} \mid \text{red}) = \frac{\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{2}}{\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{2}} = \frac{3/8}{4/8} = \frac{3}{4}$$

With equal priors, the posterior is just the share of the likelihoods:
$\frac{3/4}{3/4 + 1/4}$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A bank sees fraud in 0.1% of card payments. Its detector flags 95%
of fraudulent payments and 1% of normal payments. A payment is flagged. What is
the chance it is fraud? What does this mean for the bank's staff?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(F \mid \text{flag}) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.001}{0.95 \times 0.001 + 0.01 \times 0.999} = \frac{0.00095}{0.00095 + 0.00999} = \frac{0.00095}{0.01094} \approx 0.0868$$

Only about 9% of flags are real fraud. Staff who check flagged payments will see
about 10 false alarms for every real case, because normal payments are about 1,000
times more common.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A bag has three coins: a fair coin, a two-headed coin, and a coin
that lands heads with probability $0.25$. You pick one coin at random and flip it.
It lands heads. What is the probability it is the two-headed coin?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each prior is $\frac{1}{3}$, so it cancels. Use the likelihoods $0.5$, $1$, $0.25$:

$$P(\text{two-headed} \mid H) = \frac{1}{0.5 + 1 + 0.25} = \frac{1}{1.75} = \frac{4}{7} \approx 0.571$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $0 \lt P(H) \lt 1$ and $P(E) \gt 0$. Prove that the evidence
raises the chance of $H$, that is $P(H \mid E) \gt P(H)$, exactly when
$P(E \mid H) \gt P(E \mid H^c)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Bayes, $P(H \mid E) \gt P(H)$ means $\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E)} \gt 1$, that is
$P(E \mid H) \gt P(E)$. Expand $P(E)$ with total probability:

$$P(E \mid H) \gt P(E \mid H) P(H) + P(E \mid H^c) (1 - P(H))$$

Move the first term on the right to the left side:

$$P(E \mid H) (1 - P(H)) \gt P(E \mid H^c) (1 - P(H))$$

Since $1 - P(H) \gt 0$, divide by it to get $P(E \mid H) \gt P(E \mid H^c)$. Every
step can be reversed, so the two statements are equivalent. In words: evidence
supports $H$ exactly when it is more likely under $H$ than under its complement $H^c$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate 10 million card payments to check the answer to
Problem 3.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_payments = 10_000_000
is_fraud = rng.random(n_payments) < 0.001

flagged = rng.random(n_payments) < p_flag

```

Output:

```text
P(fraud | flag): simulation 0.0865, exact 0.0868
```

</details>

## Summary

- Bayes' theorem turns $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{cause})$ into
  $P(\text{cause} \mid \text{evidence})$.
- Posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Divide by the total to make
  the posteriors add up to 1.
- The denominator $P(E)$ comes from the law of total probability and includes
  **every** way the evidence can happen.
- Think in natural frequencies: "out of 100 cases, how many show the evidence, and
  how many of those have the cause?"
- A rare cause can stay unlikely even after strong evidence. Never forget the
  prior.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H)}{P(E)}}
$$
