# 4.9 Conditional Independence

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.9 Conditional Independence

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [4.8 Pairwise vs Mutual Independence](4.8-Pairwise-vs-Mutual-Independence.md)

> **Big idea:** Two events can be independent once you know a third thing, even though they are linked when you do not know it, and the reverse can happen too.

---

## Why It Matters

Real data is full of hidden causes. Two flips of a mystery coin, two symptoms of
one disease, two words in one email: they are related to each other **through**
something else. **Conditional independence** describes this exactly. It is the
assumption behind Naive Bayes, Bayesian networks, hidden Markov models, and the
sequential updating of [4.6](4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md). It also
explains a strange effect called "explaining away".

## Intuition

**A mystery coin.** A bag has two coins: a fair one, and a bent one that lands heads
90% of the time. A friend picks one at random and flips it twice. If you **know**
which coin it is, the two flips are independent: the coin has no memory. But if you
**do not know**, the first flip tells you something. A head makes the bent coin more
likely, and so it makes a second head more likely. Before any flips, the second flip
is heads with chance $0.7$; after a first head, the chance goes up to about $0.757$.
The flips are **dependent**, but **independent given the coin**. The coin is a
**common cause**.

**A burglar alarm.** Burglaries and earthquakes have nothing to do with each other:
they are independent. Your alarm rings if either one happens. One day your phone
says "your alarm is ringing". Now you are worried about a burglary. Then the radio
reports a small earthquake near your home. Suddenly you relax: the earthquake
**explains** the alarm, so a burglary is now much less likely. Burglary and
earthquake were independent, but **given the alarm**, they became dependent. The
alarm is a **common effect**.

## Picture

```text
   COMMON CAUSE                            COMMON EFFECT

          [ which coin ]                [ burglary ]     [ earthquake ]
           /         \                         \             /
          v           v                         v           v
    [ flip 1 ]    [ flip 2 ]                   [ alarm rings ]

   flips: DEPENDENT                        burglary, earthquake: INDEPENDENT
   given the coin: INDEPENDENT             given the alarm: DEPENDENT
```

Knowing a common cause can remove a dependence; knowing a common effect can create one.

## Definition

Let $C$ be an event with $P(C) \gt 0$. Events $A$ and $B$ are **conditionally
independent given $C$** if:

$$
P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) \thinspace P(B \mid C)
$$

where every probability is computed inside the world where $C$ happened.

**In words:** once you know $C$, the chance of both $A$ and $B$ is the product of
their chances.

When $P(B \cap C) \gt 0$, this is the same as:

$$
P(A \mid B \cap C) = P(A \mid C)
$$

**In words:** if you already know $C$, also learning $B$ does not change the chance
of $A$.

Two facts to remember:

- Conditional independence given $C$ does **not** imply independence (common cause).
- Independence does **not** imply conditional independence given $C$ (common effect).

## Why It Is True

**The two forms agree.** This is the proof from [4.7](4.7-Independence.md), done inside
the world of $C$. Assume $P(B \cap C) \gt 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \mid B \cap C) &= \frac{P(A \cap B \cap C)}{P(B \cap C)} && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \frac{P(A \cap B \mid C) \thinspace P(C)}{P(B \mid C) \thinspace P(C)} && \text{(multiplication rule, top and bottom)} \\
&= \frac{P(A \cap B \mid C)}{P(B \mid C)} && \text{(cancel } P(C) \gt 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This equals $P(A \mid C)$ exactly when $P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) P(B \mid C)$.

**Conditional independence does not give independence.** In the mystery-coin story,
let $F$ = "fair coin", $F^c$ = "bent coin", and $H_1, H_2$ = "flip 1 (2) is heads".
Given either coin, the flips are independent. By total probability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H_1) = P(H_2) &= 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.9 \times 0.5 = 0.7 && \text{(average over the two coins)} \\
P(H_1 \cap H_2) &= 0.5^2 \times 0.5 + 0.9^2 \times 0.5 = 0.125 + 0.405 = 0.53 && \text{(independent given each coin)}
\end{aligned}
$$

But $P(H_1) P(H_2) = 0.49 \ne 0.53$. So $H_1$ and $H_2$ are dependent, and
$P(H_2 \mid H_1) = \frac{0.53}{0.7} \approx 0.757$.

**Independence does not give conditional independence.** Example 2 below shows
two independent events that become dependent once we condition on a common effect.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Mystery Coin, Step by Step

**Problem.** A coin is fair or bent ($P(\text{heads}) = 0.9$), each with chance
$\frac{1}{2}$. It is flipped twice. Find $P(H_2 \mid H_1)$ and
$P(H_2 \mid H_1 \cap \text{bent})$.

**Solution.**

1. From "Why It Is True": $P(H_1) = 0.7$ and $P(H_1 \cap H_2) = 0.53$.
2. So $P(H_2 \mid H_1) = \frac{0.53}{0.7} \approx 0.757$.
3. Given the bent coin, the flips are independent, so
   $P(H_2 \mid H_1 \cap \text{bent}) = P(H_2 \mid \text{bent}) = 0.9$.
4. Another view: after one head, Bayes gives
   $P(\text{bent} \mid H_1) = \frac{0.9 \times 0.5}{0.7} = \frac{0.45}{0.7} \approx 0.643$.
   Then by total probability, given $H_1$:
   $P(H_2 \mid H_1) = 0.9 \times 0.643 + 0.5 \times 0.357 \approx 0.757$.

**Answer.** $P(H_2 \mid H_1) \approx 0.757$, higher than $P(H_2) = 0.7$. But given the
coin, the first flip adds nothing: $P(H_2 \mid H_1 \cap \text{bent}) = 0.9$.

**Check.** The first flip matters only because it is information about the coin.
Once you know the coin, that path is blocked. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Explaining Away

**Problem.** A burglary ($R$) happens with probability $0.01$, and an earthquake ($Q$)
with probability $0.02$, independently. The alarm ($A$) rings exactly when at least
one of them happens. Find $P(R \mid A)$ and $P(R \mid A \cap Q)$.

**Solution.**

1. The alarm rings when $R \cup Q$ happens. With the addition rule and independence:

$$
P(A) = P(R) + P(Q) - P(R) P(Q) = 0.01 + 0.02 - 0.0002 = 0.0298
$$

2. A burglary always rings the alarm, so $R \cap A = R$, and:

$$
P(R \mid A) = \frac{P(R)}{P(A)} = \frac{0.01}{0.0298} \approx 0.336
$$

3. If we also know an earthquake happened, the alarm is fully explained: $A \cap Q = Q$.
   So, using independence of $R$ and $Q$:

$$
P(R \mid A \cap Q) = P(R \mid Q) = P(R) = 0.01
$$

**Answer.** The alarm raises burglary from 1% to about 34%. Hearing about the
earthquake drops it back to 1%.

**Check.** Given the alarm, $R$ and $Q$ are dependent: learning $Q$ changed the chance
of $R$ from $0.336$ to $0.01$. Yet without the alarm, they are independent. A common
effect links its causes.

### Example 3: Naive Bayes With Two Words

**Problem.** 40% of email is spam ($S$). The word "lottery" is in 10% of spam and 0.5%
of normal email. The word "winner" is in 8% of spam and 0.2% of normal email. Assume
the two words appear **independently given the class** (spam or normal). An email
contains both words. What is $P(S \mid \text{both})$?

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds: $\frac{0.4}{0.6} = \frac{2}{3}$.
2. Likelihood ratios: "lottery" $\frac{0.1}{0.005} = 20$; "winner" $\frac{0.08}{0.002} = 40$.
3. Conditional independence lets us multiply the likelihoods within each class, so
   the likelihood ratios multiply ([4.6](4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md)):

$$
\text{posterior odds} = \frac{2}{3} \times 20 \times 40 = \frac{1600}{3} \approx 533.3
$$

4. Back to probability:

$$
P(S \mid \text{both}) = \frac{533.3}{1 + 533.3} \approx 0.998
$$

**Answer.** About 99.8%.

**Check.** The two words are surely **not** independent overall: they tend to appear
together, because both are common in spam. The Naive Bayes assumption is only that
they are independent **within** each class. Even when that assumption is not quite
true, the classifier often works well. See
[21.1.1 Probabilistic Classifiers and Naive Bayes](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md).

## Simulate It

The code simulates the mystery coin and checks that the flips are dependent
overall but independent given the coin.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

bent = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.5                # which coin (the common cause)
p_heads = np.where(bent, 0.9, 0.5)
h1 = rng.random(n_trials) < p_heads              # flips are independent GIVEN the coin
h2 = rng.random(n_trials) < p_heads

print(f"P(H2)              = {h2.mean():.4f}   exact 0.7000")
print(f"P(H2 | H1)         = {h2[h1].mean():.4f}   exact {0.53 / 0.7:.4f}")
print(f"P(H2 | bent)       = {h2[bent].mean():.4f}   exact 0.9000")
print(f"P(H2 | H1, bent)   = {h2[h1 & bent].mean():.4f}   exact 0.9000")
```

Output:

```text
P(H2)              = 0.7002   exact 0.7000
P(H2 | H1)         = 0.7573   exact 0.7571
P(H2 | bent)       = 0.9003   exact 0.9000
P(H2 | H1, bent)   = 0.9002   exact 0.9000
```

Overall, a first head raises the chance of a second head. Given the coin, it does
not.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating conditional independence given $C$ as plain independence.** Two symptoms of the same
  disease are linked in the whole population, even if they are independent among
  sick people.
- **Treating "independent" as "independent given anything".** Conditioning on a
  common effect creates dependence. In data, selecting only some cases (only
  hospital patients, only accepted students, only famous people) is conditioning on
  an effect, and it can create fake links between causes.
- **Forgetting to check every value of the condition.** For Naive Bayes, the words
  must be independent given spam **and** given normal. One class is not enough.
- **Mixing up the two diagram shapes.** Arrows out of a shared node (common cause):
  knowing the node blocks the link. Arrows into a shared node (common effect):
  knowing the node opens the link.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Naive Bayes assumes features are conditionally
  independent given the class. **Bayesian networks** encode many conditional
  independences in one graph, so a huge joint distribution becomes a product of
  small pieces. Hidden Markov models assume each observation depends only on the
  current hidden state.
- **Statistics:** "i.i.d. given the parameter" is the model behind Bayesian learning
  ([10.7 Bayes for Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)).
- **Medicine:** two tests are often assumed independent given the disease status,
  which justifies multiplying likelihood ratios.
- **Data science:** understanding **selection bias** and confounding: why ice cream
  sales and drownings are linked (a common cause, hot weather), and why a link can
  appear in a filtered dataset
  ([22.8 Correlation vs Causation](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$, with
$P(A \mid C) = 0.3$ and $P(B \mid C) = 0.6$. Find $P(A \cap B \mid C)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(A \cap B \mid C) = 0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.18$$

</details>

**2. (Medium)** A bag has a fair coin and a two-headed coin. You pick one at random
and flip it twice. Find $P(H_1)$, $P(H_1 \cap H_2)$ and $P(H_2 \mid H_1)$. Are the
flips independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(H_1) = 0.5 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 0.5 = 0.75$$

$$P(H_1 \cap H_2) = 0.25 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 0.5 = 0.625$$

$$P(H_2 \mid H_1) = \frac{0.625}{0.75} \approx 0.833$$

Since $0.833 \ne 0.75$ (equivalently $0.625 \ne 0.75^2 = 0.5625$), the flips are
dependent. They are independent only given the coin.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Flip two independent fair coins. Let $A$ = "first is heads", $B$ =
"second is heads", $C$ = "at least one head". Are $A$ and $B$ conditionally
independent given $C$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$C = \lbrace HH, HT, TH \rbrace$ with probability $\frac{3}{4}$. Given $C$:

$$P(A \mid C) = \frac{2}{3}, \quad P(B \mid C) = \frac{2}{3}, \quad P(A \cap B \mid C) = \frac{1}{3}$$

But $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{9} \ne \frac{1}{3}$. Not conditionally
independent: given at least one head, learning the first is heads makes the second
less likely ($\frac{1}{2}$ instead of $\frac{2}{3}$). This is explaining away again.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In Example 3, the email also contains the word "meeting", which is in
1% of spam and 10% of normal email. Assume all three words are independent given
the class. What is $P(S \mid \text{all three words})$ now?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The likelihood ratio of "meeting" is $\frac{0.01}{0.1} = 0.1$, so it points toward normal
email:

$$\text{posterior odds} = \frac{2}{3} \times 20 \times 40 \times 0.1 = \frac{160}{3} \approx 53.3$$

$$P(S \mid \text{all three}) = \frac{53.3}{54.3} \approx 0.982$$

Still very likely spam, but less sure than before.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$ and also
given $C^c$, with $0 \lt P(C) \lt 1$. Suppose also that $P(A \mid C) = P(A \mid C^c)$.
Prove that $A$ and $B$ are independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $a = P(A \mid C) = P(A \mid C^c)$. By total probability,
$P(A) = a P(C) + a P(C^c) = a$. Now split $P(A \cap B)$ on $C$ and use conditional
independence in each case:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid C) P(B \mid C) P(C) + P(A \mid C^c) P(B \mid C^c) P(C^c) = a \big(P(B \mid C) P(C) + P(B \mid C^c) P(C^c)\big) = a P(B)$$

So $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$. In words: if the common cause does not affect $A$ at
all, it cannot link $A$ to $B$. In the mystery-coin story the dependence came from
$P(H_1 \mid \text{bent}) \ne P(H_1 \mid \text{fair})$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the burglar alarm of Example 2 and estimate $P(R \mid A)$
and $P(R \mid A \cap Q)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_days = 5_000_000
burglary = rng.random(n_days) < 0.01
quake = rng.random(n_days) < 0.02
alarm = burglary | quake
print(f"P(R | A):      simulation {burglary[alarm].mean():.4f}, exact {0.01 / 0.0298:.4f}")
print(f"P(R | A, Q):   simulation {burglary[alarm & quake].mean():.4f}, exact 0.0100")
```

Output:

```text
P(R | A):      simulation 0.3352, exact 0.3356
P(R | A, Q):   simulation 0.0101, exact 0.0100
```

</details>

## Summary

- $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$ when
  $P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) P(B \mid C)$: once you know $C$, one tells you
  nothing about the other.
- A **common cause** makes events dependent; knowing the cause can make them
  independent.
- A **common effect** can make independent events dependent once you know the effect
  (explaining away).
- Naive Bayes, Bayesian networks and sequential updating all rely on conditional
  independence.
- Always say **what** you condition on. Independence and independence given $C$ are
  different claims.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) \thinspace P(B \mid C)}
$$
