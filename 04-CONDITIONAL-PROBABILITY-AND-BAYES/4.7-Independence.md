# 4.7 Independence

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.7 Independence

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.6 Sequential Bayesian Updating](4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md)

> **Big idea:** Two events are independent when learning that one happened does not change the chance of the other, and then the chance of both is just the product of their chances.

---

## Why It Matters

Independence is what makes most probability calculations possible. Ten coin flips,
a thousand users, a million packets: if they are independent, the chance of a
combined result is a simple product. But assuming independence when it is false is
one of the most dangerous mistakes in practice. It has caused wrong court
verdicts and badly underestimated financial risks. You need to know both how to
use it and how to check it.

## Intuition

Flip a coin and roll a die. If someone tells you the coin landed heads, does that
change your chance of rolling a 6? No. The coin and the die have nothing to do with
each other. The chance of a 6 stays $\frac{1}{6}$.

Now a surprising one. Draw one card from a deck. Let $A$ = "it is an ace" and $B$ =
"it is a heart". Before you learn anything, $P(A) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}$. Now
someone says "it is a heart". Among the 13 hearts, exactly 1 is an ace, so
$P(A \mid B) = \frac{1}{13}$. The news did not change the chance. These two events are
independent, even though they are about the **same** card. Independence is not
about "physically separate". It is about **information**: knowing one tells you
nothing about the other.

Compare with $C$ = "the card is red". Given $C$, the card is a heart with chance
$\frac{13}{26} = \frac{1}{2}$, but before you knew it was $\frac{1}{4}$. So "heart" and
"red" are **dependent**: one tells you a lot about the other.

## Picture

```text
                 P(A) = 0.5
          <------------------->
        +-------------------+-------------------+  ---
        |###################|                   |   ^
        |####  A ∩ B  ######|        B          |   |  P(B) = 0.3
        |###################|                   |   v
        +-------------------+-------------------+  ---
        |                   |                   |
        |                   |                   |
        |        A          |                   |
        |                   |                   |
        |                   |                   |
        |                   |                   |
        |                   |                   |
        +-------------------+-------------------+

   Area of A ∩ B = 0.5 x 0.3 = 0.15 = P(A) x P(B)
```

In a unit square, $A$ is the left half and $B$ is the top 30%; they cross like a grid, so the overlap is the product of the two sizes.

## Definition

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B)
$$

where $P(A \cap B)$ is the chance that both happen. We sometimes write $A \perp B$.

**In words:** the chance that both happen equals the product of their separate
chances.

When $P(B) \gt 0$, this is the same as:

$$
P(A \mid B) = P(A)
$$

**In words:** learning that $B$ happened does not change the chance of $A$.

If the events are not independent, they are **dependent**.

> **Warning:** Independent is **not** the same as disjoint (mutually exclusive). If
> $A$ and $B$ are disjoint and both have positive probability, then learning $B$
> tells you that $A$ did **not** happen. That is the strongest possible dependence.

## Why It Is True

**The two forms agree.** Suppose $P(B) \gt 0$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \mid B) = P(A) &\iff \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = P(A) && \text{(definition of } P(A \mid B) \text{)} \\
&\iff P(A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B) && \text{(multiply both sides by } P(B) \gt 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The product form is used as the definition because it is symmetric in $A$ and $B$
and it also works when $P(B) = 0$. It also shows that independence goes both ways:
if $B$ tells you nothing about $A$, then $A$ tells you nothing about $B$.

**Complements of independent events are independent.** If $A$ and $B$ are
independent, then so are $A$ and $B^c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \cap B^c) &= P(A) - P(A \cap B) && \text{(} A \text{ splits into } A \cap B \text{ and } A \cap B^c \text{)} \\
&= P(A) - P(A) \thinspace P(B) && \text{(independence)} \\
&= P(A) \thinspace (1 - P(B)) = P(A) \thinspace P(B^c) && \text{(complement rule)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the same argument again gives that $A^c$ and $B^c$ are independent. So if
"rain" and "traffic jam" are independent, so are "no rain" and "no traffic jam".

**Disjoint events are dependent.** If $A \cap B = \varnothing$ with $P(A) \gt 0$ and
$P(B) \gt 0$, then $P(A \cap B) = 0$ but $P(A) P(B) \gt 0$. The two sides differ, so
the events are not independent.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Ace and Heart

**Problem.** Draw one card from a standard deck. Are $A$ = "ace" and $B$ = "heart"
independent? Are $B$ = "heart" and $C$ = "red card" independent?

**Solution.**

1. $P(A) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}$ and $P(B) = \frac{13}{52} = \frac{1}{4}$.
2. $A \cap B$ is the ace of hearts, so $P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{52}$.
3. Compare: $P(A) P(B) = \frac{1}{13} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{52}$. Equal, so $A$
   and $B$ are independent.
4. For $B$ and $C$: every heart is red, so $B \cap C = B$ and
   $P(B \cap C) = \frac{1}{4}$.
5. Compare: $P(B) P(C) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8}$. Not equal, so $B$
   and $C$ are dependent.

**Answer.** Ace and heart: independent. Heart and red: dependent.

**Check.** In a deck, every suit has the same ranks, so knowing the suit tells you
nothing about the rank. But knowing the color cuts the possible suits in half.

### Example 2: Two Dice, Two Questions

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice. Let $E$ = "the first die is even". Is $E$ independent
of $S_7$ = "the sum is 7"? Is it independent of $S_8$ = "the sum is 8"?

**Solution.**

1. $P(E) = \frac{18}{36} = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. $S_7$ has 6 outcomes: $(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)$. So
   $P(S_7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.
3. $E \cap S_7$ has $(2,5), (4,3), (6,1)$: 3 outcomes. So
   $P(E \cap S_7) = \frac{3}{36} = \frac{1}{12}$.
4. Compare: $P(E) P(S_7) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{12}$. Equal:
   **independent**.
5. $S_8$ has 5 outcomes: $(2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2)$. So $P(S_8) = \frac{5}{36}$.
6. $E \cap S_8$ has $(2,6), (4,4), (6,2)$: 3 outcomes, so $P(E \cap S_8) = \frac{3}{36} = \frac{1}{12}$.
7. Compare: $P(E) P(S_8) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{5}{36} = \frac{5}{72} \approx 0.069$,
   but $\frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083$. Not equal: **dependent**.

**Answer.** "First die even" is independent of "sum is 7" but dependent on "sum is 8".

**Check.** For any first die, exactly one second-die value makes the sum 7, so the
first die tells you nothing. But a sum of 8 is impossible if the first die is 1, so
the first die does carry information about $S_8$.

### Example 3: Backup Servers

**Problem.** A website runs on two servers. Each is down on a given day with
probability $0.01$. The site is down only if **both** servers are down. Find the
chance the site is down if the servers fail independently. Then suppose instead
that both servers share one power supply that fails with probability $0.005$ and
takes both down. What can you say now?

**Solution.**

1. Independent failures: $P(\text{both down}) = 0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0001$.
2. So the site is up with probability $1 - 0.0001 = 0.9999$.
3. Shared power: whenever the power supply fails, both servers are down. So
   $P(\text{both down}) \ge P(\text{power fails}) = 0.005$.

**Answer.** With independent failures, the site is down 1 day in 10,000. With the
shared power supply, it is down at least 1 day in 200, which is **50 times** worse.

**Check.** The servers did not get worse; only the independence was lost. This is
why engineers place backup servers in different buildings or regions. Hidden shared
causes are the enemy of the product rule.

## Simulate It

The code rolls two dice many times and compares $P(E \cap S)$ with $P(E) P(S)$ for
the two cases in Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

die1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
die2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
E = (die1 % 2 == 0)                      # first die is even

for target in [7, 8]:
    S = (die1 + die2 == target)
    both = np.mean(E & S)
    product = np.mean(E) * np.mean(S)
    print(f"sum = {target}: P(E and S) = {both:.4f}, P(E) P(S) = {product:.4f}, "
          f"P(E | S) = {E[S].mean():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
sum = 7: P(E and S) = 0.0833, P(E) P(S) = 0.0834, P(E | S) = 0.4995
sum = 8: P(E and S) = 0.0836, P(E) P(S) = 0.0696, P(E | S) = 0.6010
```

For the sum 7, both columns agree and $P(E \mid S) \approx 0.5 = P(E)$. For the sum 8,
they do not: given a sum of 8, the first die is even with chance $\frac{3}{5} = 0.6$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up independent and disjoint.** Disjoint events with positive probability
  are always dependent. Independent events must be able to happen together.
- **Assuming independence without a reason.** Ask: "Is there a shared cause?" Same
  power supply, same weather, same economy, same family. If yes, the product rule
  can be badly wrong.
- **Trying to see independence in a Venn diagram.** The shape does not tell you.
  Independence is about the **sizes** of the regions: overlap must equal the product.
- **The gambler's fallacy.** After five reds in roulette, black is not "due".
  Independent spins have no memory
  ([22.5 Gambler's Fallacy](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.5-Gamblers-Fallacy.md)).
- **Checking only one direction of a complement.** If $A$ and $B$ are independent,
  then so are $A$ and $B^c$, $A^c$ and $B$, and $A^c$ and $B^c$. Use whichever form
  is easiest.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** most training methods assume the data points are
  independent draws (the "i.i.d." assumption,
  [10.6 IID](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.6-IID.md)). When they are not, such as time
  series, models can fool themselves.
- **Computer science:** randomized algorithms use independent random bits, and
  hashing assumes keys land in buckets independently
  ([21.3.2 Hashing and Bloom Filters](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.2-Hashing-and-Bloom-Filters.md)).
- **Engineering:** reliability of systems with backups, where the product rule
  holds only if failures are independent.
- **Cryptography:** each bit of a good key must be independent of the others;
  otherwise an attacker learns something from a partial leak.
- **Everyday life:** separate lottery draws, separate coin flips, and the question
  "does this new fact really tell me anything?"

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $A$ and $B$ are independent, with $P(A) = 0.3$ and $P(B) = 0.5$. Find
$P(A \cap B)$ and $P(A \cup B)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(A \cap B) = 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15$$

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.3 + 0.5 - 0.15 = 0.65$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Roll one fair die. Let $A$ = "even" $= \lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$ and
$B = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, 4 \rbrace$. Are $A$ and $B$ independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(A) = \frac{1}{2}$, $P(B) = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$, and $A \cap B = \lbrace 2, 4 \rbrace$:

$$P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} = P(A) P(B)$$

Yes, they are independent, even though they are about one roll of one die.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Prove that if $P(A) = 0$ or $P(A) = 1$, then $A$ is independent of
every event $B$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If $P(A) = 0$: since $A \cap B \subseteq A$, $0 \le P(A \cap B) \le P(A) = 0$. So
$P(A \cap B) = 0 = P(A) P(B)$.

If $P(A) = 1$: then $P(A^c) = 0$, so by the first case $A^c$ is independent of $B$.
By the complement result in "Why It Is True", $A$ is independent of $B$ too.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** You flip a fair coin 3 times. Let $A$ = "the first flip is heads" and
$B$ = "there are exactly 2 heads in total". Are $A$ and $B$ independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The 8 outcomes are equally likely. $P(A) = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2}$.
$B = \lbrace HHT, HTH, THH \rbrace$, so $P(B) = \frac{3}{8}$.
$A \cap B = \lbrace HHT, HTH \rbrace$, so $P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}$.

$$P(A) P(B) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{3}{16} \ne \frac{1}{4}$$

So they are dependent. Given exactly 2 heads, the first flip is heads with chance
$\frac{2}{3}$, not $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A data pipeline has 100 stages. Each stage works with probability
$0.99$, independently. The pipeline works only if every stage works. What is the
chance it works? What if each stage instead had 3 independent copies, and a stage
works when at least one copy works?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With one copy per stage, by independence:

$$P(\text{works}) = 0.99^{100} \approx 0.366$$

With 3 copies, a stage fails only if all 3 copies fail, which has chance
$0.01^3 = 10^{-6}$. So each stage works with chance $1 - 10^{-6} = 0.999999$, and:

$$P(\text{works}) = 0.999999^{100} \approx 0.9999$$

Many reliable parts in a row make an unreliable system; copies in parallel fix it.
Both answers depend completely on independence.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Draw a random card many times and check that "ace" and "heart" are
independent, while "heart" and "red" are not.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
card = rng.integers(0, 52, size=n_trials)
suit, rank = card // 13, card % 13          # suit 0 = hearts, suit 1 = diamonds
ace, heart, red = rank == 0, suit == 0, suit <= 1
print(f"ace & heart: {np.mean(ace & heart):.4f} vs product {ace.mean() * heart.mean():.4f}")
print(f"heart & red: {np.mean(heart & red):.4f} vs product {heart.mean() * red.mean():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
ace & heart: 0.0191 vs product 0.0192
heart & red: 0.2503 vs product 0.1252
```

</details>

## Summary

- $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$, which means learning
  one does not change the chance of the other.
- Independence is about information, not about physical separation. Ace and heart
  on the same card are independent.
- Disjoint events (with positive probabilities) are always dependent.
- If $A$ and $B$ are independent, so are their complements.
- Before you multiply probabilities, look for a hidden shared cause.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{A \text{ and } B \text{ independent} \iff P(A \cap B) = P(A) \thinspace P(B)}
$$
