# 4.10 Tree Diagrams

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.10 Tree Diagrams

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [4.9 Conditional Independence](4.9-Conditional-Independence.md)

> **Big idea:** A tree draws an experiment stage by stage: multiply along a path to get the chance of that path, add paths to get the chance of an event, and divide paths to condition.

---

## Why It Matters

The rules of this module, the chain rule, total probability and Bayes, are easy to
state and easy to misuse. A **tree diagram** puts all three on one picture, so you
can see every case and check that nothing is missing. When a problem happens in
stages (draw a card, then another; play game 1, then game 2), a tree is usually the
fastest and safest way to solve it.

## Intuition

A bag has 3 red balls and 2 blue balls. You draw one ball, keep it out, and draw a
second ball. What can happen?

First stage: red (3 chances in 5) or blue (2 in 5). Second stage: the bag now has 4
balls, and what is in it depends on the first draw. If the first was red, the bag
has 2 red and 2 blue; if the first was blue, it has 3 red and 1 blue. Draw this as a
tree: one split for the first draw, and from each branch, one split for the second.

Each complete path from the start to an end is one possible story, like "red, then
blue". To get its chance, you need the first step **and** the second step, so you
multiply. To get the chance of an event made of several stories, like "the second
ball is red", you add the stories that belong to it.

## Picture

```text
   1st draw          2nd draw        path    probability of the path
                     +-- 2/4 --> R    RR     3/5 x 2/4 = 6/20
       +-- 3/5 --> R +
       |             +-- 2/4 --> B    RB     3/5 x 2/4 = 6/20
 start +
       |             +-- 3/4 --> R    BR     2/5 x 3/4 = 6/20
       +-- 2/5 --> B +
                     +-- 1/4 --> B    BB     2/5 x 1/4 = 2/20
                                                         -----
                                             all paths:  20/20 = 1
```

Each branch carries a conditional probability; each path's chance is the product of its branches, and all paths add up to 1.

## Definition

A **probability tree** (or **tree diagram**) shows an experiment in stages:

- The **root** (start) is on the left. Each split shows the possible results of one
  stage.
- Each **branch** is labeled with the conditional probability of that result,
  **given everything on the path before it**. The branches leaving any one point add
  up to 1.
- Each **leaf** (end of a path) is one complete outcome.

Three rules turn the tree into answers. For a path with branches
$A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$:

$$
P(\text{path}) = P(A_1) \thinspace P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1})
$$

**In words (multiply along):** the chance of a path is the product of the numbers on
its branches. This is the [chain rule](4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md).

$$
P(E) = \sum_{\text{paths in } E} P(\text{path})
$$

**In words (add across):** the chance of an event is the sum of the chances of the
paths where it happens. This is the [law of total probability](4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md).

$$
P(F \mid E) = \frac{\sum_{\text{paths in both } E \text{ and } F} P(\text{path})}{\sum_{\text{paths in } E} P(\text{path})}
$$

**In words (divide to condition):** keep only the paths where the evidence $E$
happened, and find what share of their total chance also has $F$. This is
[Bayes' theorem](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md).

## Why It Is True

**Multiply along.** A path is the event $A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n$. Each branch
label is $P(A_k \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{k-1})$, so the product is the chain rule.

**Add across.** Different leaves are different complete outcomes, so they are
disjoint: two paths split at some point and then disagree there. So the chance of an
event made of several leaves is the sum of their chances (axiom 3).

**All leaves add up to 1.** Here is why, one stage at a time. At the root, the first
branches add up to 1. Suppose the paths up to some stage add up to 1. Each such path
splits into branches whose labels add up to 1, so the path's chance is shared out
among its children without loss:

$$
P(\text{path}) \times (q_1 + q_2 + \dots + q_m) = P(\text{path}) \times 1
$$

So the paths after the next stage still add up to 1. This gives a quick check on
every tree.

**Divide to condition.** This is the definition $P(F \mid E) = \frac{P(F \cap E)}{P(E)}$,
with both parts computed by adding leaves.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Draws From the Bag

**Problem.** For the bag with 3 red and 2 blue balls (two draws without replacement),
find $P(\text{2nd red})$ and $P(\text{1st red} \mid \text{2nd red})$.

**Solution.**

1. Read the leaves from the picture: RR $= \frac{6}{20}$, RB $= \frac{6}{20}$,
   BR $= \frac{6}{20}$, BB $= \frac{2}{20}$.
2. "2nd red" is the paths RR and BR. Add across:

$$
P(\text{2nd red}) = \frac{6}{20} + \frac{6}{20} = \frac{12}{20} = \frac{3}{5}
$$

3. "1st red and 2nd red" is only RR. Divide:

$$
P(\text{1st red} \mid \text{2nd red}) = \frac{6/20}{12/20} = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

**Answer.** $P(\text{2nd red}) = \frac{3}{5}$ and $P(\text{1st red} \mid \text{2nd red}) = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Check.** $P(\text{2nd red}) = \frac{3}{5}$ is the same as for the first draw, by the
symmetry we met in [4.3](4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md). And given the 2nd is red,
the 1st is one of the other 4 balls (2 red, 2 blue), so $\frac{1}{2}$ makes sense.

### Example 2: A Best-of-Three Cricket Series

**Problem.** Team A plays team B in a best-of-three series: the first team to win 2
matches wins the series. Team A wins each match with probability $0.6$,
independently. What is the chance that team A wins the series?

**Solution.**

1. Draw the tree. The series stops as soon as a team has 2 wins, so some paths are
   shorter than others.

```text
  match 1      match 2       match 3      path   chance               winner
                     +-0.6-> W              WW    0.6 x 0.6       = 0.360  A
         +-0.6-> W --+
         |           |            +-0.6-> W WLW   0.6 x 0.4 x 0.6 = 0.144  A
         |           +-0.4-> L ---+
         |                        +-0.4-> L WLL   0.6 x 0.4 x 0.4 = 0.096  B
  start -+
         |                        +-0.6-> W LWW   0.4 x 0.6 x 0.6 = 0.144  A
         |           +-0.6-> W ---+
         |           |            +-0.4-> L LWL   0.4 x 0.6 x 0.4 = 0.096  B
         +-0.4-> L --+
                     +-0.4-> L              LL    0.4 x 0.4       = 0.160  B
```

2. Multiply along each path (shown in the "chance" column).
3. Add the paths where A wins: WW, WLW, LWW.

$$
P(\text{A wins}) = 0.360 + 0.144 + 0.144 = 0.648
$$

**Answer.** $0.648$. A best-of-three series turns a 60% match winner into a 64.8%
series winner.

**Check.** All six leaves add up to $0.360 + 0.144 + 0.096 + 0.144 + 0.096 + 0.160 = 1$.
Longer series help the stronger team even more: best-of-five gives about $0.683$
(Practice 3).

### Example 3: Flipping the Medical Tree

**Problem.** A disease has prevalence $0.01$. The test is positive for 99% of sick
people and 5% of healthy people. The natural tree splits first on disease, then on
the test. Redraw it so that it splits first on the **test result**, and read off
$P(D \mid +)$ and $P(D \mid -)$.

**Solution.**

1. Natural order. Multiply along to get the four leaves:

```text
                         +-- 0.99 --> +   D and +    0.01 x 0.99 = 0.0099
         +-- 0.01 --> D -+
         |               +-- 0.01 --> -   D and -    0.01 x 0.01 = 0.0001
  start -+
         |               +-- 0.05 --> +   Dc and +   0.99 x 0.05 = 0.0495
         +-- 0.99 --> Dc +
                         +-- 0.95 --> -   Dc and -   0.99 x 0.95 = 0.9405
```

2. First stage of the flipped tree: the test result. Add across:
   $P(+) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$ and $P(-) = 0.0001 + 0.9405 = 0.9406$.
3. Second stage: divide. Given $+$: $P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167$.
   Given $-$: $P(D \mid -) = \frac{0.0001}{0.9406} \approx 0.00011$.
4. The flipped tree has the **same four leaves**, in a different order:

```text
                            +-- 0.167 ----> D     0.0594 x 0.167   = 0.0099
         +-- 0.0594 --> + --+
         |                  +-- 0.833 ----> Dc    0.0594 x 0.833   = 0.0495
  start -+
         |                  +-- 0.00011 --> D     0.9406 x 0.00011 = 0.0001
         +-- 0.9406 --> - --+
                            +-- 0.99989 --> Dc    0.9406 x 0.99989 = 0.9405
```

**Answer.** $P(D \mid +) \approx 0.167$ and $P(D \mid -) \approx 0.00011$.

**Check.** Flipping a tree is Bayes' theorem as a picture: the leaves (the joint
probabilities) stay the same, only the order of the questions changes. The flipped
tree is the one a doctor needs, because the doctor sees the test result first.

## Simulate It

The code plays many best-of-three series and checks the answer $0.648$ from
Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_series = 1_000_000
p = 0.6

# Play all 3 matches; a series that is already decided just ignores match 3.
# This gives the same winner as stopping early, and keeps the code short.
wins = rng.random((n_series, 3)) < p
a_wins_series = wins.sum(axis=1) >= 2

exact = p**2 + 2 * p**2 * (1 - p)        # WW + WLW + LWW
print(f"Simulation: {a_wins_series.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.6485
Exact:      0.6480
```

> **Note:** Playing a "useless" third match does not change who wins, because in a
> decided series the third match cannot change the count of 2 wins. This trick, adding
> stages that do not matter, often makes trees and simulations simpler.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing unconditional numbers on later branches.** Every branch after the first
  is a conditional probability, given the path so far. In the bag, the second-stage
  numbers change with the first draw.
- **Branches that do not add up to 1.** At each split, the labels must add to 1. If
  they do not, a case is missing.
- **Adding along a path or multiplying across paths.** Multiply **along** (and), add
  **across** (or). Mixing these up is the most common tree error.
- **Forgetting that the leaves add up to 1.** Always check this. It catches most
  arithmetic mistakes.
- **Making the tree too big.** For many stages, trees grow fast ($2^{10} = 1024$ leaves
  for 10 coin flips). Then use symmetry, counting or a formula instead.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** decision trees and game trees (chess, Go) are close
  cousins; Monte Carlo tree search samples paths in a tree of moves. Probabilistic
  programs build a tree of random choices.
- **Computer science:** analyzing randomized algorithms stage by stage, and protocols
  with retries (send, fail, resend).
- **Medicine:** decision trees for diagnosis and treatment choices.
- **Sports and games:** chances of winning a series, a tie-break or a tournament.
- **Everyday life:** planning with "if this happens, then ..." branches, such as
  travel with a possible missed connection.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Flip a fair coin twice. Draw the tree and find the probability of
exactly one head.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Four paths, each $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$: HH, HT, TH, TT. Exactly one
head is HT or TH:

$$P(\text{exactly one head}) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** It rains with probability $0.3$. If it rains, the bus is late with
probability $0.5$; if not, with probability $0.2$. Draw the tree. Find
$P(\text{rain and late})$, $P(\text{late})$ and $P(\text{rain} \mid \text{late})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Leaves: rain and late $0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15$; rain and on time $0.15$; dry and late
$0.7 \times 0.2 = 0.14$; dry and on time $0.56$. They add to 1.

$$P(\text{late}) = 0.15 + 0.14 = 0.29$$

$$P(\text{rain} \mid \text{late}) = \frac{0.15}{0.29} \approx 0.517$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Example 2, the series is changed to best-of-five (first to 3
wins), with $p = 0.6$. Find $P(\text{A wins})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A wins in 3, 4 or 5 matches. A win in match $3 + k$ means the last match is a win and
exactly $k$ of the first $2 + k$ matches are losses, which can happen in
$\binom{2+k}{k}$ orders:

$$P = 0.6^3 \left(1 + \binom{3}{1} 0.4 + \binom{4}{2} 0.4^2\right) = 0.216 \times (1 + 1.2 + 0.96) = 0.216 \times 3.16 \approx 0.683$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Flip the tree of Problem 2: make the first stage "late or on time".
Find all four branch labels of the flipped tree.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

First stage: $P(\text{late}) = 0.29$, $P(\text{on time}) = 0.71$. Second stage:

$$P(\text{rain} \mid \text{late}) = \frac{0.15}{0.29} \approx 0.517, \qquad P(\text{dry} \mid \text{late}) = \frac{0.14}{0.29} \approx 0.483$$

$$P(\text{rain} \mid \text{on time}) = \frac{0.15}{0.71} \approx 0.211, \qquad P(\text{dry} \mid \text{on time}) = \frac{0.56}{0.71} \approx 0.789$$

The leaves are the same four numbers as before.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A tennis player's first serve goes in with probability $0.6$, and then
she wins the point with probability $0.7$. If the first serve is out, she serves
again: the second serve goes in with probability $0.9$, and then she wins the point
with probability $0.5$. If both serves are out (a double fault), she loses the point.
Find the chance she wins the point, and the chance that her first serve was in,
given that she won the point.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Winning paths: first in and win, $0.6 \times 0.7 = 0.42$; first out, second in, win,
$0.4 \times 0.9 \times 0.5 = 0.18$.

$$P(\text{win}) = 0.42 + 0.18 = 0.60$$

$$P(\text{first in} \mid \text{win}) = \frac{0.42}{0.60} = 0.7$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the two draws from the bag (3 red, 2 blue) and check
$P(\text{2nd red}) = 0.6$ and $P(\text{1st red} \mid \text{2nd red}) = 0.5$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 500_000
bag = np.array([1, 1, 1, 0, 0])                    # 1 = red, 0 = blue
draws = rng.permuted(np.tile(bag, (n_trials, 1)), axis=1)[:, :2]
first_red, second_red = draws[:, 0] == 1, draws[:, 1] == 1
print(f"P(2nd red):           simulation {second_red.mean():.4f}, exact 0.6000")
print(f"P(1st red | 2nd red): simulation {first_red[second_red].mean():.4f}, exact 0.5000")
```

Output:

```text
P(2nd red):           simulation 0.5995, exact 0.6000
P(1st red | 2nd red): simulation 0.4996, exact 0.5000
```

</details>

## Summary

- A tree shows an experiment stage by stage. Branch labels are conditional
  probabilities given the path so far, and they add to 1 at each split.
- Multiply along a path (chain rule). Add across paths (total probability). Divide to
  condition (Bayes).
- All leaves add up to 1. Check it every time.
- Flipping a tree means asking the questions in the other order. The leaves stay the
  same; this is Bayes' theorem as a picture.
- Stopping rules (like "first to 2 wins") make paths of different lengths; trees
  handle them well.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(F \mid E) = \frac{\text{sum of paths in } E \text{ and } F}{\text{sum of paths in } E}}
$$
