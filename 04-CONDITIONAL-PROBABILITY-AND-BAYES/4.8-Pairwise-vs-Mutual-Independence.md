# 4.8 Pairwise vs Mutual Independence

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.8 Pairwise vs Mutual Independence

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [4.7 Independence](4.7-Independence.md)

> **Big idea:** For three or more events, checking every pair is not enough: events can be independent two at a time and still be completely dependent when you look at them all together.

---

## Why It Matters

With two events, independence has one meaning. With three or more, there are two,
and they are not the same. When we say "the coin flips are independent" we mean the
strong kind, **mutual independence**, which allows us to multiply any number of
probabilities. The weak kind, **pairwise independence**, can hide a strong
dependence. This difference matters in cryptography, in hashing, and in every
proof that multiplies many probabilities.

## Intuition

Flip two fair coins. Look at three events:

- $A$ = "the first coin is heads",
- $B$ = "the second coin is heads",
- $C$ = "the two coins show the same side".

Each has probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Check any two of them. Knowing the first coin
tells you nothing about the second, so $A$ and $B$ are independent. What about $A$
and $C$? If you know the first coin is heads, the coins match exactly when the second
is also heads, which still has chance $\frac{1}{2}$. So knowing $A$ tells you nothing
about $C$. The same is true for $B$ and $C$. Every pair is independent.

But now suppose you know **both** $A$ and $B$: both coins are heads. Then you know
for sure that they match, so $C$ is certain. Two of the events together tell you
everything about the third. The events are independent in pairs, but not as a
group.

## Picture

```text
   outcome    A: 1st is H    B: 2nd is H    C: same side
   -------    -----------    -----------    ------------
     HH            x              x               x
     HT            x
     TH                           x
     TT                                           x

   each event:      2 of 4 outcomes  -> P = 1/2
   each pair:       1 of 4 outcomes  -> P = 1/4 = 1/2 x 1/2     (pairs OK)
   all three:       1 of 4 outcomes  -> P = 1/4, but 1/2 x 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/8
```

Every pair overlaps in exactly one outcome, as independence needs, but the triple overlaps in that same outcome, which is too much.

## Definition

Events $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ are **pairwise independent** if every pair is
independent:

$$
P(A_i \cap A_j) = P(A_i) \thinspace P(A_j) \quad \text{for all } i \ne j
$$

**In words:** any two of the events are independent.

They are **mutually independent** (or just "independent") if the product rule holds
for **every** group of two or more of them: for every set $S$ of indices with at
least two elements,

$$
P\left(\bigcap_{i \in S} A_i\right) = \prod_{i \in S} P(A_i)
$$

**In words:** for any choice of the events, the chance that all of the chosen
events happen is the product of their chances.

For three events $A, B, C$, mutual independence means these **four** equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \cap B) &= P(A) \thinspace P(B), \qquad P(A \cap C) = P(A) \thinspace P(C), \qquad P(B \cap C) = P(B) \thinspace P(C), \\
P(A \cap B \cap C) &= P(A) \thinspace P(B) \thinspace P(C)
\end{aligned}
$$

For $n$ events there are $2^n - n - 1$ equations: one for each subset of size at
least 2. (There are $2^n$ subsets in all; we skip the $n$ subsets of size 1 and the
empty set.)

## Why It Is True

**Mutual implies pairwise.** The pair equations are part of the list, so mutually
independent events are always pairwise independent.

**Pairwise does not imply mutual.** The two-coin example is a counterexample. All
three pair equations hold, but:

$$
P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(\lbrace HH \rbrace) = \frac{1}{4} \ne \frac{1}{8} = P(A) \thinspace P(B) \thinspace P(C)
$$

**The triple equation alone does not imply the pairs either.** Roll two fair dice.
Let $A$ = "the first die is 1, 2 or 3", $B$ = "the first die is 3, 4 or 5", and $C$ =
"the sum is 9". Then $P(A) = P(B) = \frac{1}{2}$, and $C = \lbrace (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) \rbrace$
so $P(C) = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9}$. The only outcome in all three is $(3,6)$:

$$
P(A \cap B \cap C) = \frac{1}{36} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{9}
$$

But $A \cap B$ = "the first die is 3", so $P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{6} \ne \frac{1}{4}$.
That is why the definition needs **every** subset.

**Why mutual independence is the useful kind.** If $A, B, C$ are mutually
independent, then $A$ is independent of any event made from $B$ and $C$, for example
$B \cup C$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \cap (B \cup C)) &= P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap C) - P(A \cap B \cap C) && \text{(addition rule)} \\
&= P(A) P(B) + P(A) P(C) - P(A) P(B) P(C) && \text{(mutual independence, all 3 equations)} \\
&= P(A) \thinspace \big(P(B) + P(C) - P(B) P(C)\big) && \text{(factor out } P(A) \text{)} \\
&= P(A) \thinspace P(B \cup C) && \text{(addition rule, with } B \perp C \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line needed the triple equation. In the two-coin example it fails, and
so does this result: $B \cup C = \lbrace HH, TH, TT \rbrace$, and
$P(A \cap (B \cup C)) = P(\lbrace HH \rbrace) = \frac{1}{4}$, but
$P(A) P(B \cup C) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{8}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Checking All Four Equations

**Problem.** For the two coins and events $A, B, C$ from the Intuition section, check
all four equations for mutual independence.

**Solution.**

1. The 4 outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT each have probability $\frac{1}{4}$.
2. $A = \lbrace HH, HT \rbrace$, $B = \lbrace HH, TH \rbrace$, $C = \lbrace HH, TT \rbrace$. Each has probability $\frac{1}{2}$.
3. $A \cap B = \lbrace HH \rbrace$: $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$. Holds.
4. $A \cap C = \lbrace HH \rbrace$: $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$. Holds.
5. $B \cap C = \lbrace HH \rbrace$: $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$. Holds.
6. $A \cap B \cap C = \lbrace HH \rbrace$: $\frac{1}{4}$, but the product is $\frac{1}{8}$. Fails.

**Answer.** Pairwise independent, but not mutually independent.

**Check.** $P(C \mid A \cap B) = 1$, while $P(C) = \frac{1}{2}$. Knowing two of the
events changes the third completely.

### Example 2: Sharing a Secret With XOR

**Problem.** Alice has a secret bit $S$ (0 or 1, each with chance $\frac{1}{2}$). She
picks a random key bit $K$, independent of $S$, also 0 or 1 with chance $\frac{1}{2}$.
She gives Bob the bit $K$ and gives Carol the bit $M = S \text{ XOR } K$. (XOR gives 1
when the two bits differ and 0 when they are equal.) Show that Bob alone learns nothing
about $S$, Carol alone learns nothing about $S$, but together they learn $S$ exactly.

**Solution.**

1. Bob's bit $K$ is independent of $S$ by construction.
2. Carol's bit: for each value of $S$, $M$ is 0 or 1 with chance $\frac{1}{2}$, because
   $K$ is. For example $P(M = 1 \mid S = 0) = P(K = 1) = \frac{1}{2}$ and
   $P(M = 1 \mid S = 1) = P(K = 0) = \frac{1}{2}$. So for both values of $S$, the
   chance of $M = 1$ is $\frac{1}{2} = P(M = 1)$: $M$ is independent of $S$.
3. Also $K$ and $M$ are independent of each other (same argument with the roles
   swapped).
4. Together: $K \text{ XOR } M = K \text{ XOR } S \text{ XOR } K = S$, because XOR with
   the same bit twice cancels. So the pair determines $S$.

**Answer.** The three events $S = 1$, $K = 1$ and $M = 1$ are pairwise independent but
not mutually independent. Each share alone is useless; two shares reveal everything.

**Check.** This is exactly the two-coin example: write heads as 1 and tails as 0, let
$K$ be the first coin and $M$ the second. Then the event $S = 1$ is "the coins
differ", which is $C^c$. The idea is the basis of
**secret sharing** and of the one-time pad in cryptography.

### Example 3: Three Truly Independent Events

**Problem.** A server checks three things every night. The disk check fails with
probability $0.1$, the memory check with $0.05$, and the network check with $0.02$.
The checks fail mutually independently. Find the chance that all three pass, and the
chance that at least one fails.

**Solution.**

1. Let $F_1, F_2, F_3$ be the three failures. Mutual independence of the
   $F_i$ also gives mutual independence of the complements $F_i^c$ (Practice 5 shows
   one step of the proof).
2. All three pass:

$$
P(F_1^c \cap F_2^c \cap F_3^c) = 0.9 \times 0.95 \times 0.98 = 0.8379
$$

3. At least one fails is the complement:

$$
P(\text{at least one fails}) = 1 - 0.8379 = 0.1621
$$

**Answer.** All pass with chance $0.8379$; at least one fails with chance $0.1621$.

**Check.** The answer is a little below $0.1 + 0.05 + 0.02 = 0.17$, the
[union bound](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md), as it must be. With only
pairwise independence, the step "multiply all three" would not be allowed.

## Simulate It

The code flips two coins many times and checks the four equations of Example 1.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

coin1 = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.5    # True = heads
coin2 = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.5
A, B, C = coin1, coin2, (coin1 == coin2)

pA, pB, pC = A.mean(), B.mean(), C.mean()
print(f"P(A∩B)   = {np.mean(A & B):.4f}   P(A)P(B)     = {pA * pB:.4f}")
print(f"P(A∩C)   = {np.mean(A & C):.4f}   P(A)P(C)     = {pA * pC:.4f}")
print(f"P(B∩C)   = {np.mean(B & C):.4f}   P(B)P(C)     = {pB * pC:.4f}")
print(f"P(A∩B∩C) = {np.mean(A & B & C):.4f}   P(A)P(B)P(C) = {pA * pB * pC:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A∩B)   = 0.2503   P(A)P(B)     = 0.2503
P(A∩C)   = 0.2503   P(A)P(C)     = 0.2502
P(B∩C)   = 0.2503   P(B)P(C)     = 0.2502
P(A∩B∩C) = 0.2503   P(A)P(B)P(C) = 0.1252
```

The first three lines agree; the last one is off by a factor of 2.

## Common Mistakes

- **Checking only the pairs.** Pairwise independence is not enough to multiply three
  or more probabilities. You need mutual independence.
- **Checking only the big product.** $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A) P(B) P(C)$ alone does
  not make the events independent, as the dice example shows.
- **Saying "independent" without saying which kind.** For three or more events,
  "independent" in this book means **mutually** independent. Say "pairwise" when you
  mean the weak kind.
- **Believing that every event built from $B$ and $C$ is independent of $A$ under
  pairwise independence.** That needs mutual independence. In the two-coin example,
  $A$ is not independent of $B \cup C$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** **pairwise independent hash functions** are cheap to compute
  and good enough for many hashing and load-balancing proofs, which only need pairs.
  Some algorithms are made deterministic by replacing fully random bits with
  pairwise independent ones.
- **Cryptography:** XOR secret sharing (Example 2) relies on shares being pairwise
  independent of the secret while not being mutually independent with it.
- **AI and machine learning:** a model with many inputs may have features that look
  unrelated one pair at a time but together predict the target perfectly, like XOR.
  Simple pairwise checks miss this.
- **Reliability and statistics:** computing the chance that "all parts work" or "at
  least one test fails" with a product needs mutual independence.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** How many equations must you check to show that 4 events are mutually
independent? How many for pairwise independence?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Mutual: $2^4 - 4 - 1 = 11$ (6 pairs, 4 triples, 1 group of all four).
Pairwise: only the $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $A, B, C$ are mutually independent with $P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.4$,
$P(C) = 0.2$. Find the chance that all three happen and the chance that none of them
happens.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = 0.5 \times 0.4 \times 0.2 = 0.04$$

The complements are also mutually independent, so:

$$P(A^c \cap B^c \cap C^c) = 0.5 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.24$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Roll a fair die twice. Let $A$ = "the first roll is odd", $B$ = "the
second roll is odd", $C$ = "the sum is odd". Are these events pairwise independent?
Mutually independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each has probability $\frac{1}{2}$. The sum is odd exactly when one roll is odd and
the other is even.

$A \cap B$: both odd, chance $\frac{1}{4}$. Holds.
$A \cap C$: first odd and second even, chance $\frac{1}{4}$. Holds.
$B \cap C$: second odd and first even, chance $\frac{1}{4}$. Holds.

So they are pairwise independent. But if both rolls are odd, the sum is even, so
$A \cap B \cap C = \varnothing$:

$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = 0 \ne \frac{1}{8}$$

They are not mutually independent. This is the same structure as the two-coin example.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that if $A, B, C$ are mutually independent, then $A$ and
$B \cap C$ are independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the triple equation, then the pair equation for $B$ and $C$:

$$P(A \cap (B \cap C)) = P(A) P(B) P(C) = P(A) \thinspace P(B \cap C)$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that if $A, B, C$ are mutually independent, then $A^c, B, C$ are
mutually independent too.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need four equations. The pair $B, C$ is unchanged. The pairs $A^c, B$ and
$A^c, C$ follow from the complement result in [4.7](4.7-Independence.md). For the
triple, split $B \cap C$ into the part inside $A$ and the part inside $A^c$:

$$P(A^c \cap B \cap C) = P(B \cap C) - P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(B) P(C) - P(A) P(B) P(C) = P(A^c) P(B) P(C)$$

Repeating the argument (complement one event at a time) shows that any mix of the
events and their complements is mutually independent.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the XOR secret sharing of Example 2. Estimate
$P(S = 1 \mid M = 1)$ (Carol alone) and $P(S = 1 \mid K = 1, M = 0)$ (Bob and Carol
together).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
S = rng.integers(0, 2, size=n_trials)      # secret bit
K = rng.integers(0, 2, size=n_trials)      # random key, Bob's share
M = S ^ K                                  # Carol's share
print(f"P(S=1)             = {np.mean(S == 1):.4f}")
print(f"P(S=1 | M=1)       = {np.mean(S[M == 1] == 1):.4f}   (Carol alone learns nothing)")
print(f"P(S=1 | K=1, M=0)  = {np.mean(S[(K == 1) & (M == 0)] == 1):.4f}   (together: S = 1 for sure)")
```

Output:

```text
P(S=1)             = 0.4998
P(S=1 | M=1)       = 0.4999   (Carol alone learns nothing)
P(S=1 | K=1, M=0)  = 1.0000   (together: S = 1 for sure)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Pairwise independence: every **pair** of events is independent.
- Mutual independence: the product rule holds for **every** group of two or more
  events, which is $2^n - n - 1$ equations for $n$ events.
- Mutual implies pairwise, but pairwise does not imply mutual (two coins, XOR).
- The single equation for all the events together is not enough either.
- Only mutual independence lets you multiply many probabilities and treat events
  built from separate groups as independent.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{mutual independence: } P\left(\bigcap_{i \in S} A_i\right) = \prod_{i \in S} P(A_i) \text{ for every } S \text{ with at least 2 indices}}
$$
