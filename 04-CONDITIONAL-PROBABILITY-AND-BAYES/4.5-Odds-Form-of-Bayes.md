# 4.5 Odds Form of Bayes

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](README.md) / 4.5 Odds Form of Bayes

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)

> **Big idea:** Written with odds, Bayes' theorem becomes one multiplication: posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times the prior odds.

---

## Why It Matters

The probability form of Bayes needs a denominator, $P(E)$, which takes work. The
**odds form** removes it. You can update beliefs in your head, see at once how
strong a piece of evidence is, and combine several pieces of evidence by
multiplying (or, with logarithms, by adding). This "adding up evidence" view is
exactly what logistic regression and Naive Bayes classifiers do inside.

## Intuition

Go back to the taxi from [4.4](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md). 15 taxis in 100 are blue and
85 are green. So before the witness speaks, blue against green is **15 to 85**,
which simplifies to **3 to 17**.

The witness says "blue". A blue taxi makes the witness say "blue" 80% of the time;
a green taxi makes the witness say "blue" 20% of the time. So the report "blue" is
$\frac{0.8}{0.2} = 4$ times as likely if the taxi is blue. That number, 4, is the
**strength** of the evidence.

Now multiply. Blue against green was 3 to 17. The evidence favors blue by 4, so it
becomes $3 \times 4 = 12$ to 17. That is the answer: 12 chances for blue against 17
for green, so $P(\text{blue}) = \frac{12}{12 + 17} = \frac{12}{29} \approx 0.41$. No
denominator, no tree, one multiplication.

## Picture

```text
  log10(odds):   -3       -2       -1        0       +1       +2       +3
                  |--------|--------|--------|--------|--------|--------|
  probability:  0.001    0.01     0.09      0.5     0.91     0.99    0.999

  Medical test (Example 1):
                           ^ prior odds 1 : 99  (about -2.0)
                           |
                           +==========>  times 19.8  (adds about +1.3)
                                       ^
                                       posterior odds 1 : 5  (about -0.7)
                                       so p = 1/6
```

On a log-odds scale, each piece of evidence moves your belief by a fixed step: multiplying odds is adding logs.

## Definition

The **odds** of an event $A$ compare the chance that it happens with the chance
that it does not:

$$
\text{odds}(A) = \frac{P(A)}{P(A^c)} = \frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)}
$$

**In words:** odds say how many times more likely $A$ is to happen than not to
happen. Odds of 3 (often written "3 to 1" or 3:1) mean $P(A) = \frac{3}{4}$.

To go back from odds $o$ to a probability:

$$
P(A) = \frac{o}{1 + o}
$$

**In words:** odds of $a$ to $b$ mean $a$ chances for and $b$ against, so the
probability is $\frac{a}{a + b}$.

| Probability | Odds | Said as |
|---|---|---|
| $0.5$ | $1$ | 1 to 1, "even odds" |
| $0.75$ | $3$ | 3 to 1 for |
| $0.2$ | $0.25$ | 1 to 4, or 4 to 1 against |
| $0.01$ | $\frac{1}{99} \approx 0.0101$ | 1 to 99 |
| $0.99$ | $99$ | 99 to 1 for |

The **likelihood ratio** (LR) of evidence $E$ for a hypothesis $H$ is:

$$
\text{LR} = \frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}
$$

**In words:** how many times more likely the evidence is if $H$ is true than if $H$
is false. LR above 1 supports $H$, LR below 1 supports $H^c$, and LR equal to 1
means the evidence is useless for this question.

**Odds form of Bayes' theorem.** If $P(E) \gt 0$ and $P(E \mid H^c) \gt 0$:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(H^c \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)}}_{\text{prior odds}}
$$

**In words:** posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times the prior odds.

Take logarithms and the product becomes a sum:

$$
\log \text{(posterior odds)} = \log \text{(prior odds)} + \log \text{LR}
$$

**In words:** in log-odds, evidence **adds**. A strong piece of evidence is a big
step, a weak piece is a small step, and useless evidence ($\text{LR} = 1$,
$\log \text{LR} = 0$) is no step at all.

The same works for any two hypotheses $H_1$ and $H_2$, even if they are not
complements. Then the likelihood ratio $\frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)}$ is
called the **Bayes factor**.

## Why It Is True

Write Bayes' theorem for $H$ and for $H^c$. Both have the **same** denominator $P(E)$:

$$
P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H)}{P(E)}, \qquad P(H^c \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H^c) \thinspace P(H^c)}{P(E)}
$$

Divide the first by the second:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(H^c \mid E)} &= \frac{P(E \mid H) \thinspace P(H) \thinspace / \thinspace P(E)}{P(E \mid H^c) \thinspace P(H^c) \thinspace / \thinspace P(E)} && \text{(Bayes for each)} \\
&= \frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)} \cdot \frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)} && \text{(} P(E) \text{ cancels)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The hard part of Bayes, $P(E)$, has disappeared. It is still hidden in the step
from odds back to probability: $\frac{o}{1 + o}$ does the normalizing.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Medical Test in Your Head

**Problem.** A disease has prevalence 1%. The test detects 99% of sick people and
gives a false positive for 5% of healthy people. Use odds to find
$P(D \mid +)$.

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds: $\frac{0.01}{0.99} = \frac{1}{99}$, that is 1 to 99.
2. Likelihood ratio of a positive test:

$$
\text{LR} = \frac{P(+ \mid D)}{P(+ \mid D^c)} = \frac{0.99}{0.05} = 19.8
$$

3. Posterior odds:

$$
\frac{1}{99} \times 19.8 = \frac{19.8}{99} = 0.2 = \frac{1}{5}
$$

4. Back to probability: odds 1 to 5 means $\frac{1}{1 + 5} = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Answer.** $P(D \mid +) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$.

**Check.** Same as Example 4 of [4.4](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md). And it explains the
surprise: the test is strong (LR about 20), but the prior was 1 to 99, and 20 times
1 to 99 is still only about 1 to 5.

### Example 2: A Loaded Die in the Bag

**Problem.** A bag has 10 dice. Nine are fair, and one is loaded so that it shows 6
half of the time. You pick a die at random and roll it once. It shows 6. What is
the chance you picked the loaded die?

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds, loaded against fair: 1 to 9.
2. Likelihood ratio of a 6:

$$
\text{LR} = \frac{P(6 \mid \text{loaded})}{P(6 \mid \text{fair})} = \frac{1/2}{1/6} = 3
$$

3. Posterior odds: $1 \times 3$ to 9, that is 3 to 9, or 1 to 3.
4. Probability: $\frac{1}{1 + 3} = \frac{1}{4}$.

**Answer.** $P(\text{loaded} \mid 6) = 0.25$.

**Check.** With the full Bayes formula:
$\frac{0.5 \times 0.1}{0.5 \times 0.1 + \frac{1}{6} \times 0.9} = \frac{0.05}{0.05 + 0.15} = 0.25$.
The simulation below agrees.

### Example 3: Log-Odds and the Spam Filter

**Problem.** In Example 3 of [4.4](4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md), 40% of email is spam, and
the word "lottery" is 20 times more common in spam than in normal email. Use
natural-log odds to find $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{lottery})$.

**Solution.**

1. Prior log-odds: $\ln \frac{0.4}{0.6} \approx -0.405$.
2. Log likelihood ratio: $\ln 20 \approx 2.996$.
3. Posterior log-odds: $-0.405 + 2.996 = 2.590$ (with unrounded values).
4. Back to probability. Odds are $e^{2.590}$, so the probability is
   $\frac{e^{2.590}}{1 + e^{2.590}}$. Divide top and bottom by $e^{2.590}$:

$$
P(\text{spam} \mid \text{lottery}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2.590}} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 0.0750} \approx 0.930
$$

**Answer.** About 93%, the same as in 4.4.

**Check.** The function $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ that turns log-odds $x$ into a
probability is the **sigmoid** (or logistic) function. A logistic regression model
computes a log-odds score as a sum of terms, one per feature, and then applies the
sigmoid. So it is "adding evidence in log-odds", exactly like this example. See
[21.1.1 Probabilistic Classifiers and Naive Bayes](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md).

## Simulate It

The code simulates the bag of dice from Example 2 and checks the posterior
probability and the posterior odds.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Prior: 1 loaded die among 10, so P(loaded) = 0.1 (odds 1 to 9).
loaded = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.1

# Likelihood: a loaded die shows 6 with probability 1/2, a fair die with 1/6.
p_six = np.where(loaded, 1 / 2, 1 / 6)
six = rng.random(n_trials) < p_six

post_prob = loaded[six].mean()
post_odds = post_prob / (1 - post_prob)
exact_odds = 3 * (1 / 9)              # likelihood ratio x prior odds

print(f"P(loaded | 6):    simulation {post_prob:.4f}, exact {0.25:.4f}")
print(f"Posterior odds:   simulation {post_odds:.4f}, exact {exact_odds:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(loaded | 6):    simulation 0.2493, exact 0.2500
Posterior odds:   simulation 0.3321, exact 0.3333
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up odds and probability.** Odds of 1 to 5 mean a probability of
  $\frac{1}{6}$, not $\frac{1}{5}$. Always add both sides to get the denominator.
- **Judging evidence by one likelihood only.** A test with 99% sensitivity is not
  strong evidence if it also fires for half of healthy people: the LR would be only
  $\frac{0.99}{0.5} \approx 2$. Strength is the **ratio** of the two likelihoods.
- **Treating the likelihood ratio as the answer.** LR = 20 does not mean "20 times
  more likely to be spam than not". That is true only if the prior odds were 1 to 1.
- **Multiplying likelihood ratios of evidence that is not independent.** Two
  positive results from the same faulty lab are not two independent pieces of
  evidence. Multiplying their LRs counts the same thing twice. The correct
  condition is [conditional independence](4.9-Conditional-Independence.md).
- **Confusing "odds against" with "odds for".** Betting odds of "4 to 1" usually mean
  4 to 1 **against**, so the probability is $\frac{1}{5}$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** logistic regression outputs log-odds; Naive Bayes
  adds log likelihood ratios of the words; the sigmoid turns log-odds into a
  probability.
- **Medicine:** doctors use the LR of a test (LR+ for a positive result, LR- for a
  negative result) to update the chance of disease without redoing a full table.
- **Law and forensics:** experts report the strength of DNA or fingerprint evidence
  as a likelihood ratio, and leave the prior odds to the court
  ([4.11.3 Prosecutor's Fallacy](4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.3-Prosecutors-Fallacy.md)).
- **Science:** the Bayes factor compares two models or theories on the same data.
- **Everyday life and betting:** bookmakers quote odds; good forecasters think in
  "how much should this news move me?", which is a likelihood ratio.

**1. (Easy)** Convert $P(A) = 0.2$ into odds. Convert odds of 3 to 2 into a
probability.

<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{odds}(A) = \frac{0.2}{0.8} = 0.25 \text{, that is 1 to 4}$$

Odds of 3 to 2 mean 3 chances for and 2 against:

$$P = \frac{3}{3 + 2} = 0.6$$

**2. (Easy)** Your prior odds for a hypothesis are 1 to 4. You see evidence with
likelihood ratio 8. What are the posterior odds and the posterior probability?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{posterior odds} = 8 \times \frac{1}{4} = 2 \text{, that is 2 to 1}$$

So the posterior probability is $\frac{2}{2 + 1} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A test has sensitivity $P(+ \mid D) = 0.9$ and false positive rate
$P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.1$. The prior is $P(D) = 0.05$. Find the LR of a positive
result and of a negative result, and the posterior probability after each.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{LR}_{+} = \frac{0.9}{0.1} = 9, \qquad \text{LR}_{-} = \frac{P(- \mid D)}{P(- \mid D^c)} = \frac{0.1}{0.9} = \frac{1}{9}$$

Prior odds are $\frac{0.05}{0.95} = \frac{1}{19}$.

After a positive test, the odds are $\frac{9}{19}$, so $P(D \mid +) = \frac{9}{9 + 19} = \frac{9}{28} \approx 0.321$.

After a negative test, the odds are $\frac{1}{19} \times \frac{1}{9} = \frac{1}{171}$, so $P(D \mid -) = \frac{1}{172} \approx 0.0058$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** You think a hypothesis has a 1% chance. How large must the
likelihood ratio of a piece of evidence be to raise it to 50%? To 90%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds are $\frac{1}{99}$. For 50% you need posterior odds 1, so
$\text{LR} \times \frac{1}{99} = 1$ and $\text{LR} = 99$.

For 90% you need posterior odds $\frac{0.9}{0.1} = 9$, so $\text{LR} = 9 \times 99 = 891$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $H_1$ and $H_2$ be any two hypotheses with positive prior
probability (not necessarily complements), and $P(E \mid H_2) \gt 0$. Prove that
$\frac{P(H_1 \mid E)}{P(H_2 \mid E)} = \frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)} \cdot \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)}$.
Why is this useful when there are many hypotheses?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By Bayes, $P(H_k \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_k) P(H_k)}{P(E)}$ for $k = 1, 2$, with the
same $P(E) \gt 0$. Divide the two equations; $P(E)$ cancels:

$$\frac{P(H_1 \mid E)}{P(H_2 \mid E)} = \frac{P(E \mid H_1) P(H_1) / P(E)}{P(E \mid H_2) P(H_2) / P(E)} = \frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)} \cdot \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)}$$

It is useful because you can compare two hypotheses without knowing all the
others. Computing $P(E)$ needs a sum over **every** hypothesis, which can be
impossible when there are millions of them (for example, all possible parameter
values of a model).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Problem 3: draw people with $P(D) = 0.05$, test them,
and estimate $P(D \mid +)$ and $P(D \mid -)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

```

```text

P(D | -): simulation 0.0059, exact 0.0058
```

## Summary

- Odds are $\frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)}$; turn odds $o$ back into a probability with
  $\frac{o}{1 + o}$.
- The likelihood ratio $\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}$ measures how strong the
  evidence is. It needs **both** likelihoods.
- Posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times the prior odds. No $P(E)$ needed.
- In log-odds, evidence adds. This is the idea behind logistic regression and the
  sigmoid function.
- A strong likelihood ratio can still leave a rare hypothesis unlikely.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{posterior odds} = \text{likelihood ratio} \times \text{prior odds}}
$$
