# 4.11.5 Gambler's Ruin

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](../README.md) / [4.11 Classic Puzzles](README.md) / 4.11.5 Gambler's Ruin

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [4.11.4 Simpson's Paradox](4.11.4-Simpsons-Paradox.md) and [4.3 Law of Total Probability](../4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)

> **Big idea:** Condition on the first bet, and the law of total probability turns a game of unlimited length into a simple equation: in a fair game your chance of reaching your goal is your fraction of the total money, and in an unfair game it collapses fast.

---

## Why It Matters

**Gambler's ruin** asks: if you keep betting until you either reach a goal or lose
everything, how likely is each ending? The game can last any number of steps, so we
cannot draw the whole tree. The trick, called **first-step analysis**, is to
condition on what happens in the first step only. It is the law of total probability
used on a process, and it is the main tool for random walks and Markov chains in
[Module 18](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/README.md). It also explains why casinos always
win in the long run.

## Intuition

You have 3 taka. Your friend has 7 taka. You flip a fair coin again and again: heads,
your friend pays you 1 taka; tails, you pay your friend 1 taka. The game stops when
one of you has all 10 taka. What is your chance of winning everything?

Here is a clean argument. The game is fair, so on average no money moves. When the
game ends, you have either 10 taka (with chance $w$) or 0 taka. Your average final
money is $10 w$, and it should equal your starting money, 3 taka. So $10 w = 3$ and
$w = 0.3$. **In a fair game, your chance of winning is your share of the total money.**

Now make the coin slightly unfair: you win each flip with chance $0.45$. It feels like a
small change. But over a long game the small edge adds up again and again, and your
chance of winning everything drops from $0.3$ to about $0.128$. With a casino's edge
and a long game, ruin becomes almost certain.

## Picture

```text
         q        q        q        q              q        q
      <-----   <-----   <-----   <-----         <-----   <-----
   [0]     [1]      [2]      [3]      [4]  ...  [N-2]    [N-1]    [N]
      ----->   ----->   ----->   ----->         ----->   ----->
         p        p        p        p              p        p

   ruin: stop                start at k                goal: stop
   w(0) = 0                                            w(N) = 1

   first step from k:  w(k) = p w(k+1) + q w(k-1)
```

From each amount you move up 1 with chance $p$ or down 1 with chance $q = 1 - p$; the walk stops at 0 or at $N$.

## Definition

A gambler starts with $k$ units of money, where $0 \le k \le N$. Each round, they win
1 unit with probability $p$ and lose 1 unit with probability $q = 1 - p$,
independently of other rounds. They stop when their money reaches 0 (**ruin**) or $N$
(the goal). Let $w_k$ be the probability of reaching $N$ before 0, starting from $k$.

**Gambler's ruin formula.** For a fair game ($p = \frac{1}{2}$):

$$
w_k = \frac{k}{N}
$$

**In words:** in a fair game, your chance of reaching the goal is your starting money
divided by the goal.

For an unfair game ($p \ne \frac{1}{2}$), with $r = \frac{q}{p}$:

$$
w_k = \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}
$$

**In words:** each step's odds $r = \frac{q}{p}$ are raised to the power of how far you
must travel, so a small edge against you ($r$ a little above 1) grows into a huge
disadvantage over a long game.

The chance of ruin is $1 - w_k$. (One can show that the game ends with probability 1,
so these two endings cover everything; see
[18.2.2 Gambler's Ruin](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md),
which also finds how long the game lasts.)

## Why It Is True

**First-step analysis.** Start at $k$ with $0 \lt k \lt N$. The first round is a win
(chance $p$) or a loss (chance $q$). These two cases form a partition, so by the
[law of total probability](../4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md):

$$
w_k = p \thinspace w_{k+1} + q \thinspace w_{k-1}, \qquad w_0 = 0, \quad w_N = 1
$$

Why is $P(\text{goal} \mid \text{first round won}) = w_{k+1}$? Because the rounds are
independent, after a win the game is exactly a new game that starts from $k + 1$. The
past does not matter.

**Solve the equation.** Since $p + q = 1$, write $w_k = p w_k + q w_k$. Put this on the
left side and rearrange:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p w_k + q w_k &= p w_{k+1} + q w_{k-1} && \text{(since } p + q = 1 \text{)} \\
p (w_{k+1} - w_k) &= q (w_k - w_{k-1}) && \text{(collect terms)} \\
d_k &= r \thinspace d_{k-1} && \text{(with } d_k = w_{k+1} - w_k \text{ and } r = q/p \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the steps $d_k$ form a geometric sequence: $d_k = r^k d_0$, and $d_0 = w_1 - w_0 = w_1$.
Add up the steps from 0 to $k$:

$$
w_k = d_0 + d_1 + \dots + d_{k-1} = w_1 (1 + r + r^2 + \dots + r^{k-1})
$$

**Fair game ($r = 1$).** The sum is $k$, so $w_k = k w_1$. Use $w_N = 1$: $N w_1 = 1$, so
$w_1 = \frac{1}{N}$ and $w_k = \frac{k}{N}$.

**Unfair game ($r \ne 1$).** The geometric sum is $\frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r}$, so
$w_k = w_1 \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r}$. Use $w_N = 1$: $w_1 = \frac{1 - r}{1 - r^N}$. Then:

$$
w_k = \frac{1 - r}{1 - r^N} \cdot \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r} = \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}
$$

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Fair Coin Game

**Problem.** You start with 3 taka, your friend with 7 taka, and you play the fair coin
game until one of you has all 10. Find your chance of winning, and check that the game
is fair "on average".

**Solution.**

1. Here $k = 3$, $N = 10$, $p = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. Use the fair formula:

$$
w_3 = \frac{3}{10} = 0.3
$$

3. Average final money: $10 \times 0.3 + 0 \times 0.7 = 3$ taka, the same as the start.

**Answer.** You win everything with chance $0.3$ and lose everything with chance $0.7$.

**Check.** The richer player is more likely to win even in a perfectly fair game, simply
because they can survive longer losing streaks. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Playing Roulette

**Problem.** In American roulette, a bet on red wins with probability $\frac{18}{38}$
(there are 18 red, 18 black and 2 green pockets). You have 50 taka and want to reach
100 taka by betting 1 taka on red each time. What is your chance? What if you bet all
50 taka on red at once?

**Solution.**

1. $p = \frac{18}{38} \approx 0.474$, $q = \frac{20}{38}$, so $r = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{20}{18} = \frac{10}{9} \approx 1.111$.
2. Use the unfair formula with $k = 50$, $N = 100$:

$$
w_{50} = \frac{1 - (10/9)^{50}}{1 - (10/9)^{100}} \approx \frac{1 - 194.0}{1 - 37{,}648} \approx 0.0051
$$

3. One big bet of 50 taka: you double up exactly when red comes up, chance
   $\frac{18}{38} \approx 0.474$.

**Answer.** Betting 1 taka at a time: about 0.5%. One bold bet: about 47%.

**Check.** In a fair game both strategies would give $0.5$. The edge of
$q - p = \frac{2}{38} \approx 0.053$ per bet looks small, but over the hundreds of bets of a long
game it nearly guarantees ruin. In an unfavorable game, fewer and bigger bets give the
house fewer chances to use its edge.

### Example 3: Against a Bank With Unlimited Money

**Problem.** A trader wins each round with probability $p = 0.6$ and starts with 3
units. The other side (the market, or a casino) never runs out of money, so there is
no upper goal: the trader just tries to avoid ruin forever. What is the chance the
trader is never ruined? What if $p = \frac{1}{2}$?

**Solution.**

1. Never being ruined is the limit of "reach $N$ before 0" as $N \to \infty$.
2. Here $r = \frac{0.4}{0.6} = \frac{2}{3} \lt 1$, so $r^N \to 0$ as $N$ grows:

$$
w_3 \to \frac{1 - (2/3)^3}{1 - 0} = 1 - \frac{8}{27} = \frac{19}{27} \approx 0.704
$$

3. For $p = \frac{1}{2}$: $w_k = \frac{k}{N} \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$.

**Answer.** With an edge ($p = 0.6$), about 70% chance of never being ruined. In a fair
game against unlimited money, ruin is **certain**.

**Check.** This is why "playing until I am ahead" against a casino fails: even a fair
game against an opponent with unlimited money ends in ruin with probability 1.

## Simulate It

The code plays many games at once, step by step, until every game has hit 0 or 10, for
a fair coin and for $p = 0.45$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_games, start, goal = 100_000, 3, 10

for p in [0.5, 0.45]:
    money = np.full(n_games, start)
    active = np.ones(n_games, dtype=bool)
    while active.any():
        # Each active game moves +1 with chance p, otherwise -1.
        step = np.where(rng.random(active.sum()) < p, 1, -1)
        money[active] += step
        active &= (money > 0) & (money < goal)
    if p == 0.5:
        exact = start / goal
    else:
        r = (1 - p) / p
        exact = (1 - r**start) / (1 - r**goal)
    print(f"p = {p}: P(reach {goal}) simulation {np.mean(money == goal):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
p = 0.5: P(reach 10) simulation 0.3008, exact 0.3000
p = 0.45: P(reach 10) simulation 0.1294, exact 0.1283
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking a fair game gives equal chances to both players.** It gives each player a
  chance equal to their **share of the money**. The poorer player usually loses
  everything.
- **Underestimating a small edge.** A 5% edge per bet seems small, but the formula
  raises $r = \frac{q}{p}$ to large powers. Over long play, the edge dominates.
- **Forgetting the independence assumption.** First-step analysis needs "after the first
  round, the game starts fresh". This uses the independence of the rounds.
- **Mixing up $w_k$ and the ruin probability.** $w_k$ is the chance of reaching the goal;
  ruin has chance $1 - w_k$.
- **Believing a betting system can beat a fair or unfair game.** Changing bet sizes
  changes the risk, not the long-run average. In a fair game the average money stays
  the same; in an unfair game it goes down.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance and insurance:** the chance that a company with a given amount of capital
  goes bankrupt before it grows (ruin theory in insurance).
- **Computer science:** random walks in algorithms, such as the randomized algorithm for
  2-SAT, are analyzed with the same first-step equations
  ([21.3.1 Randomized Algorithms](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.3-Computer-Science/21.3.1-Randomized-Algorithms.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** in reinforcement learning, the value of a state is found
  by conditioning on the next step, the same idea as first-step analysis
  ([21.1.5 Reinforcement Learning](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)).
- **Biology:** whether a new gene variant spreads through a population or dies out is a
  random walk with two absorbing ends.
- **Everyday life:** gambling, and any repeated risk with a small edge against you
  ([22.12 Compounding Small Risks](../../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.12-Compounding-Small-Risks.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You have 20 taka, the goal is 100 taka, and each 1-taka bet is a fair coin
flip. What is your chance of reaching 100 taka? What is your chance of ruin?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$w_{20} = \frac{20}{100} = 0.2, \qquad P(\text{ruin}) = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Write the first-step equation for $w_1$ in a game with $N = 3$ and $p = \frac{1}{2}$,
and solve it together with the equation for $w_2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $w_0 = 0$ and $w_3 = 1$:

$$w_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} w_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0, \qquad w_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} w_1$$

Put the first into the second: $w_2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} w_2$, so $\frac{3}{4} w_2 = \frac{1}{2}$
and $w_2 = \frac{2}{3}$. Then $w_1 = \frac{1}{3}$. This matches $\frac{k}{N}$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You start with 3 units, the goal is 10, and you win each round with
probability $0.45$. Find $w_3$. Then find it for $p = 0.55$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $p = 0.45$: $r = \frac{0.55}{0.45} = \frac{11}{9} \approx 1.222$.

$$w_3 = \frac{1 - (11/9)^3}{1 - (11/9)^{10}} \approx \frac{1 - 1.826}{1 - 7.439} \approx 0.128$$

For $p = 0.55$: $r = \frac{9}{11}$, and $w_3 = \frac{1 - (9/11)^3}{1 - (9/11)^{10}} \approx \frac{0.4523}{0.8656} \approx 0.523$.
A 5% edge for you or against you changes the answer from $0.3$ to $0.523$ or $0.128$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In Example 2 (roulette, $p = \frac{18}{38}$), you want to double 10 taka to 20
taka with 1-taka bets. Find your chance and compare with doubling 50 to 100.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $r = \frac{10}{9}$:

$$w_{10} = \frac{1 - (10/9)^{10}}{1 - (10/9)^{20}} = \frac{1}{1 + (10/9)^{10}} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 2.868} \approx 0.259$$

(The middle step uses $1 - x^2 = (1 - x)(1 + x)$ with $x = (10/9)^{10}$.) Doubling 50 to 100
had chance about $0.005$. The longer the game, the more the edge hurts.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove, without solving the equations, that in a fair game $w_k = \frac{k}{N}$
is the **only** solution of $w_k = \frac{1}{2} w_{k+1} + \frac{1}{2} w_{k-1}$ with $w_0 = 0$,
$w_N = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Suppose $u$ and $v$ are two solutions, and let $e_k = u_k - v_k$. Then $e$ solves the same
equation with $e_0 = e_N = 0$. Let $M$ be the largest value of $e_k$, reached at some $k$.
If $0 \lt k \lt N$, then $e_k = \frac{1}{2}(e_{k+1} + e_{k-1})$ is the average of two numbers
that are both at most $M$, so both must equal $M$. Repeating this, every value out to the
ends equals $M$, so $M = e_0 = 0$. The same argument for the smallest value shows it is
0 too. So $e_k = 0$ for all $k$, and the solution is unique. Since $\frac{k}{N}$ is a
solution (check: $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{k + 1}{N} + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{k - 1}{N} = \frac{k}{N}$), it is the
only one.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the roulette game of Practice 4 (10 taka to 20 taka with
1-taka bets on red) and compare with $0.259$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_games, start, goal, p = 400_000, 10, 20, 18 / 38

```

```text

```

</details>

## Summary

- First-step analysis: condition on the first round and use the law of total
  probability, $w_k = p w_{k+1} + q w_{k-1}$.
- In a fair game, $w_k = \frac{k}{N}$: your chance is your share of the total money.
- In an unfair game, $w_k = \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}$ with $r = \frac{q}{p}$, and a small edge
  becomes a huge one over a long game.
- Against an opponent with unlimited money, a fair game ends in ruin with probability 1.
- In an unfavorable game, bold play (few big bets) beats timid play (many small bets).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{w_k = \frac{1 - (q/p)^k}{1 - (q/p)^N} \quad (p \ne q), \qquad w_k = \frac{k}{N} \quad (p = q)}
$$
