# 4.11.2 Medical Testing

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](../README.md) / [4.11 Classic Puzzles](README.md) / 4.11.2 Medical Testing

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.11.1 Monty Hall](4.11.1-Monty-Hall.md) and [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](../4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)

> **Big idea:** A positive result from an accurate test can still mean you are probably healthy, because when a disease is rare, the false positives from the many healthy people outnumber the true positives from the few sick ones.

---

## Why It Matters

Every year millions of people get a positive screening result and are told "the
test is 99% accurate". Many of them, and even many doctors, then believe they are
99% likely to be sick. Often the true chance is far lower. This is the clearest
real-life case of Bayes' theorem, and the same mistake appears in fraud alerts,
security scanners, spam filters and any detector of rare events.

## Intuition

A disease affects 1 person in 100. A test for it has two error rates:

- It catches 99% of sick people (it misses 1%).
- It wrongly says "positive" for 5% of healthy people.

You test positive. Imagine 10,000 people like you taking the test:

- 100 are sick. The test catches 99 of them.
- 9,900 are healthy. The test wrongly flags 5% of them: 495 people.

So $99 + 495 = 594$ people get a positive result, and only 99 of them are sick. Your
chance of being sick is $\frac{99}{594} \approx 0.167$, about 1 in 6. Not 99%.

The test did its job: it raised your chance from 1% to about 17%, a big jump. But
it started from a low base. The 5% error rate applies to a **huge** group (the
healthy), while the 99% success rate applies to a **tiny** group (the sick).

## Picture

```text
                            10,000 people
                        /                   \
                 100 sick                 9,900 healthy
                /        \               /              \
        99 test +      1 test -     495 test +      9,405 test -
     (true positive) (false neg.) (false positive) (true negative)

   positive tests:  99 + 495 = 594
         sick among them:      99 / 594       = 0.167   (16.7%)
   negative tests:  1 + 9,405 = 9,406
         healthy among them:   9,405 / 9,406  = 0.9999  (99.99%)
```

Natural frequencies show the answer at a glance: the 495 false positives swamp the 99 true positives.

## Definition

Let $D$ = "the person has the disease", and $+$ / $-$ be the test results. Medical
tests use these names:

| Name | Formula | Meaning | Here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prevalence** | $P(D)$ | how common the disease is in the tested group | $0.01$ |
| **Sensitivity** | $P(+ \mid D)$ | share of sick people the test catches | $0.99$ |
| **Specificity** | $P(- \mid D^c)$ | share of healthy people the test clears | $0.95$ |
| **False positive rate** | $P(+ \mid D^c)$ | $1 - \text{specificity}$ | $0.05$ |
| **PPV** | $P(D \mid +)$ | positive predictive value: chance of disease after a positive | $0.167$ |
| **NPV** | $P(D^c \mid -)$ | negative predictive value: chance of health after a negative | $0.9999$ |

Sensitivity and specificity describe the **test**. PPV and NPV describe **you**, after
the result. Bayes' theorem connects them. With prevalence $p$, sensitivity $s$ and
specificity $c$:

$$
\text{PPV} = P(D \mid +) = \frac{s \thinspace p}{s \thinspace p + (1 - c)(1 - p)}
$$

**In words:** the chance you are sick after a positive is the share of all positives
that are true positives.

$$
\text{NPV} = P(D^c \mid -) = \frac{c \thinspace (1 - p)}{c \thinspace (1 - p) + (1 - s) \thinspace p}
$$

**In words:** the chance you are healthy after a negative is the share of all
negatives that are true negatives.

## Why It Is True

**PPV.** Apply [Bayes' theorem](../4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md) with hypothesis $D$ and evidence $+$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(D \mid +) &= \frac{P(+ \mid D) \thinspace P(D)}{P(+)} && \text{(Bayes)} \\
&= \frac{P(+ \mid D) \thinspace P(D)}{P(+ \mid D) P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c) P(D^c)} && \text{(total probability)} \\
&= \frac{s \thinspace p}{s \thinspace p + (1 - c)(1 - p)} && \text{(the names above)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The NPV comes the same way with hypothesis $D^c$ and evidence $-$.

**Why the prevalence matters so much.** Divide the top and bottom of the PPV by $sp$:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{1 - c}{s} \cdot \dfrac{1 - p}{p}}
$$

The first fraction, $\frac{1 - c}{s}$, is fixed by the test. The second, $\frac{1 - p}{p}$,
is the odds **against** disease, and it explodes when the disease is rare. For
$p = 0.01$ it is 99; for $p = 0.001$ it is 999. So as $p$ goes toward 0, the PPV goes
toward 0, no matter how good the test is (unless it has no false positives at all).

| Prevalence $p$ | 0.001 | 0.01 | 0.05 | 0.1 | 0.3 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PPV (same test) | 0.019 | 0.167 | 0.510 | 0.688 | 0.895 | 0.952 |

The same test, with the same sensitivity and specificity, gives very different
answers in different groups of people.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Screening the General Population

**Problem.** The test above (sensitivity $0.99$, specificity $0.95$) is used to screen
everyone, with prevalence $0.01$. Find the PPV and the NPV.

**Solution.**

1. True positives: $s p = 0.99 \times 0.01 = 0.0099$.
2. False positives: $(1 - c)(1 - p) = 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.0495$.
3. PPV:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0099 + 0.0495} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167
$$

4. True negatives: $c (1 - p) = 0.95 \times 0.99 = 0.9405$. False negatives:
   $(1 - s) p = 0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0001$.
5. NPV:

$$
\text{NPV} = \frac{0.9405}{0.9405 + 0.0001} = \frac{0.9405}{0.9406} \approx 0.99989
$$

**Answer.** After a positive, about a 17% chance of disease. After a negative, about a
99.99% chance of being healthy.

**Check.** A negative result is very reassuring; a positive result is a reason for a
second test, not a diagnosis. That is exactly how screening programs are designed.

### Example 2: The Same Test in a Clinic

**Problem.** Now the test is used only on patients who come to a clinic with
symptoms. Among them, the prevalence is $0.1$. What is the PPV?

**Solution.**

1. True positives: $0.99 \times 0.1 = 0.099$.
2. False positives: $0.05 \times 0.9 = 0.045$.
3. PPV:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{0.099}{0.099 + 0.045} = \frac{0.099}{0.144} = 0.6875
$$

**Answer.** About 69%, four times higher than in screening.

**Check.** In odds form ([4.5](../4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)), the likelihood ratio of a
positive is $\frac{0.99}{0.05} = 19.8$ in both cases. Only the prior odds changed: from
1 to 99 (screening) to 1 to 9 (clinic). And $19.8 \times \frac{1}{9} = 2.2$, so
$\text{PPV} = \frac{2.2}{3.2} = 0.6875$. A symptom is evidence too; it raised the prior.

### Example 3: Better Sensitivity or Better Specificity

**Problem.** For screening (prevalence $0.01$), you can buy test A (sensitivity $0.99$,
specificity $0.95$) or test B (sensitivity $0.90$, specificity $0.99$). Which gives a
more useful positive result?

**Solution.**

1. Test A: PPV $\approx 0.167$, from Example 1.
2. Test B: true positives $0.90 \times 0.01 = 0.009$; false positives
   $0.01 \times 0.99 = 0.0099$.

$$
\text{PPV}_B = \frac{0.009}{0.009 + 0.0099} = \frac{0.009}{0.0189} \approx 0.476
$$

**Answer.** Test B's positive result is almost three times as informative (about 48%
against 17%), even though B misses more sick people.

**Check.** The likelihood ratios tell the same story: A has $\frac{0.99}{0.05} = 19.8$,
B has $\frac{0.90}{0.01} = 90$. For a rare disease, the false positive rate (1 minus
specificity) controls the PPV, because it multiplies the huge healthy group. But B
has a cost: it misses 10% of sick people instead of 1%.

## Simulate It

The code screens a million people with the test of Example 1 and estimates the PPV
and NPV directly by counting.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 1_000_000
prevalence, sensitivity, specificity = 0.01, 0.99, 0.95

sick = rng.random(n_people) < prevalence
p_positive = np.where(sick, sensitivity, 1 - specificity)
positive = rng.random(n_people) < p_positive

print(f"True positives:  {np.sum(sick & positive):,}")
print(f"False positives: {np.sum(~sick & positive):,}")
print(f"PPV = P(D | +):  simulation {sick[positive].mean():.4f}, exact {0.0099 / 0.0594:.4f}")
print(f"NPV = P(Dc | -): simulation {(~sick[~positive]).mean():.5f}, exact {0.9405 / 0.9406:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
True positives:  9,951
False positives: 49,332
PPV = P(D | +):  simulation 0.1679, exact 0.1667
NPV = P(Dc | -): simulation 0.99988, exact 0.99989
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading sensitivity as the PPV.** "The test catches 99% of sick people" is
  $P(+ \mid D)$. The patient needs $P(D \mid +)$. They can be very different.
- **Ignoring the prevalence (base rate neglect).** The same test gives a PPV of 2% in
  one group and 95% in another. Always ask: "Who is being tested?"
- **Saying a test is "99% accurate" without saying how.** A test with 99% sensitivity
  can still have a 50% false positive rate. You need **both** sensitivity and
  specificity.
- **Treating repeated tests as independent when they are not.** If a false positive
  is caused by something in your body, retesting with the same test will likely be
  positive again. Confirm with a different test
  ([4.6 Sequential Bayesian Updating](../4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md)).
- **Forgetting that a negative is also evidence.** In screening, a negative result
  usually makes disease very unlikely. The NPV is often the most useful number.

## Where It Is Used

- **Medicine:** interpreting screening tests for cancers, infections and genetic
  conditions; deciding who should be tested at all
  ([21.6 Medicine and Diagnostic Testing](../../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** sensitivity is **recall**, PPV is **precision**. For a
  rare class (fraud, disease, defects), a classifier with high recall can still have
  terrible precision. This is why accuracy alone is a bad metric for rare classes.
- **Computer security:** intrusion detection and malware scanners face the same
  problem: real attacks are rare, so most alerts are false alarms.
- **Airport security and policing:** screening many innocent people for a rare
  threat produces mostly false alarms.
- **Everyday life:** before you panic about a scary result, ask about the base rate
  ([22.1 Base Rates](../../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.1-Base-Rates.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Use the test of Example 1 (sensitivity $0.99$, specificity $0.95$) in a
group where half the people are sick. What is the PPV?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{0.99 \times 0.5}{0.99 \times 0.5 + 0.05 \times 0.5} = \frac{0.495}{0.52} \approx 0.952$$

With a 50% prior, a positive test is strong evidence.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A test has sensitivity $0.8$ and specificity $0.9$. Out of 1,000 people,
200 are sick. Fill in the four counts (true positives, false negatives, false
positives, true negatives) and find the PPV.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Sick: $200 \times 0.8 = 160$ true positives, $40$ false negatives. Healthy: 800 people,
$800 \times 0.1 = 80$ false positives, $720$ true negatives.

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{160}{160 + 80} = \frac{160}{240} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the test of Example 1, at what prevalence is a positive result a
coin flip, that is, $\text{PPV} = 0.5$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

PPV is $0.5$ when true positives equal false positives:

$$0.99 p = 0.05 (1 - p) \implies 1.04 p = 0.05 \implies p = \frac{0.05}{1.04} \approx 0.048$$

So the prevalence must be about 4.8% before a positive result makes disease more
likely than not.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** After a positive result in screening (PPV $\frac{1}{6}$), the patient takes
test B from Example 3, which is independent of test A given the disease status. Test
B is also positive. What is the chance of disease now?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use odds. After test A the odds are $\frac{1/6}{5/6} = \frac{1}{5}$. Test B has likelihood
ratio $\frac{0.90}{0.01} = 90$:

$$\text{odds} = \frac{1}{5} \times 90 = 18, \qquad P(D \mid +_A, +_B) = \frac{18}{19} \approx 0.947$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Assume $0 \lt s \le 1$ and $0 \le c \lt 1$. Prove that the PPV increases with
the prevalence $p$, that it tends to 0 as $p \to 0$, and that it tends to 1 as $p \to 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From "Why It Is True", for $0 \lt p \lt 1$:

$$\text{PPV}(p) = \frac{1}{1 + k \cdot \frac{1 - p}{p}}, \qquad k = \frac{1 - c}{s} \gt 0$$

The odds against, $\frac{1 - p}{p} = \frac{1}{p} - 1$, strictly decrease as $p$ grows. So the
denominator strictly decreases, and the PPV strictly increases. As $p \to 0$,
$\frac{1 - p}{p} \to \infty$, so the PPV tends to 0. As $p \to 1$, $\frac{1 - p}{p} \to 0$, so
the PPV tends to 1.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate tests A and B of Example 3 on the same million people
(prevalence $0.01$) and compare their PPVs.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 1_000_000
sick = rng.random(n_people) < 0.01
for name, sens, spec, exact in [("A", 0.99, 0.95, 0.0099 / 0.0594), ("B", 0.90, 0.99, 0.009 / 0.0189)]:
    positive = rng.random(n_people) < np.where(sick, sens, 1 - spec)
    print(f"Test {name}: PPV simulation {sick[positive].mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Test A: PPV simulation 0.1679, exact 0.1667
Test B: PPV simulation 0.4754, exact 0.4762
```

</details>

## Summary

- Sensitivity $P(+ \mid D)$ and specificity $P(- \mid D^c)$ describe the test; the PPV
  $P(D \mid +)$ and NPV $P(D^c \mid -)$ describe the patient.
- Bayes' theorem connects them, and the prevalence (the prior) matters as much as
  the test.
- For a rare disease, most positives are false positives, even for an excellent test.
- For a rare disease, specificity drives the PPV; the likelihood ratio
  $\frac{s}{1 - c}$ measures the strength of a positive.
- Think in natural frequencies: out of 10,000 people, how many test positive, and how
  many of those are sick?

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{PPV} = \frac{s \thinspace p}{s \thinspace p + (1 - c)(1 - p)}}
$$
