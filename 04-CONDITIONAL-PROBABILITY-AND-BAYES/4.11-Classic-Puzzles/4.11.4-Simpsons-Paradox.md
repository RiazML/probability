# 4.11.4 Simpson's Paradox

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](../README.md) / [4.11 Classic Puzzles](README.md) / 4.11.4 Simpson's Paradox

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.11.3 Prosecutor's Fallacy](4.11.3-Prosecutors-Fallacy.md) and [4.3 Law of Total Probability](../4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)

> **Big idea:** One option can be better inside every group and still look worse when the groups are combined, because each overall rate is a weighted average, and the two options can have very different weights.

---

## Why It Matters

**Simpson's paradox** shows that a trend in every part of the data can reverse when
you add the parts together. It appears in medicine, school results, sports, salaries,
and the evaluation of machine-learning models. If you compare overall rates without
looking at how the groups are mixed, you can choose the worse treatment or the worse
model. The fix is the [law of total probability](../4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md).

## Intuition

A hospital compares two treatments for kidney stones. These are real numbers from a
1986 medical study (treatment A was open surgery, B a less invasive method):

| | Small stones | Large stones | All patients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treatment A | 81 of 87 = 93% | 192 of 263 = 73% | 273 of 350 = 78% |
| Treatment B | 234 of 270 = 87% | 55 of 80 = 69% | 289 of 350 = 83% |

For small stones, A is better (93% against 87%). For large stones, A is better again
(73% against 69%). But over all patients, B is better (83% against 78%). How can A
win both groups and lose overall?

Look at **who** got each treatment. Doctors gave A mostly to the hard cases: 263 of
A's 350 patients had large stones. B got mostly easy cases: 270 of its 350 patients
had small stones. Large stones are harder for every treatment. So B's overall rate is
pulled up by its many easy cases, and A's is pulled down by its many hard ones. The
comparison of overall rates is really a comparison of case mixes.

## Picture

```text
  rate
    0.65      0.70      0.75      0.80      0.85      0.90      0.95
      |---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
             ^        ^         ^        ^       ^            ^
          B large           A overall         B small
          (0.69)             (0.78)           (0.87)
                      |                  |                    |
                   A large           B overall             A small
                   (0.73)             (0.83)               (0.93)

   A overall = 0.25 x 0.93 + 0.75 x 0.73   (mostly large stones: near 0.73)
   B overall = 0.77 x 0.87 + 0.23 x 0.69   (mostly small stones: near 0.87)
```

Each overall rate sits between its two group rates, but A's sits near its low group rate and B's near its high one.

## Definition

Let $S$ be "success", let $T$ be the treatment (A or B), and let the groups $G_1, G_2$
(small, large) form a partition. **Simpson's paradox** happens when:

$$
P(S \mid A \cap G_i) \gt P(S \mid B \cap G_i) \text{ for every group } i, \quad \text{but} \quad P(S \mid A) \lt P(S \mid B)
$$

**In words:** A is better in every group, but worse overall (or the reverse).

It is possible because each overall rate is a weighted average, by the law of total
probability applied inside the world of each treatment:

$$
P(S \mid A) = P(S \mid A \cap G_1) \thinspace P(G_1 \mid A) + P(S \mid A \cap G_2) \thinspace P(G_2 \mid A)
$$

**In words:** A's overall success rate is its rate in each group, weighted by the share
of A's patients in that group. The same holds for B, but **with B's own weights**
$P(G_i \mid B)$.

The paradox needs two things: the groups have different success rates (large stones
are harder), and the treatments have different mixes of groups
($P(G_1 \mid A) \ne P(G_1 \mid B)$).

## Why It Is True

**The reversal is possible.** The kidney-stone numbers prove it. Check A's overall rate
with the weighted average: A's weights are $\frac{87}{350} \approx 0.249$ and
$\frac{263}{350} \approx 0.751$, so

$$
P(S \mid A) = \frac{81}{87} \cdot \frac{87}{350} + \frac{192}{263} \cdot \frac{263}{350} = \frac{81 + 192}{350} = \frac{273}{350} = 0.78
$$

**With equal mixes, the reversal is impossible.** Suppose both treatments have the
same weights: $P(G_1 \mid A) = P(G_1 \mid B) = w$. Write $a_i = P(S \mid A \cap G_i)$ and
$b_i = P(S \mid B \cap G_i)$, with $a_1 \gt b_1$ and $a_2 \gt b_2$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(S \mid A) - P(S \mid B) &= \big(w a_1 + (1 - w) a_2\big) - \big(w b_1 + (1 - w) b_2\big) && \text{(same weights)} \\
&= w (a_1 - b_1) + (1 - w)(a_2 - b_2) && \text{(group the terms)} \\
&\gt 0 && \text{(both differences are positive, } 0 \le w \le 1 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the paradox comes **only** from different case mixes. This is why good experiments
assign treatments **at random**: randomization makes the mix of easy and hard cases
about the same in both arms, and then the overall comparison is fair.

**Which table should you trust?** Probability shows that both tables are correct; it
cannot by itself say which one answers your question. That depends on how the data was
produced. Here, stone size influenced which treatment doctors chose **and** the
chance of success, so the fair comparison is within each size group: A is better. If
instead the grouping variable were a **result** of the treatment, the overall table
could be the right one. This is a question about causes, discussed in
[22.9 Simpson's Paradox](../../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.9-Simpsons-Paradox.md) and
[22.8 Correlation vs Causation](../../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.8-Correlation-vs-Causation.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Kidney Stones by the Formula

**Problem.** Using the table in the Intuition section, compute each treatment's overall
success rate as a weighted average of its group rates.

**Solution.**

1. A's weights: $P(\text{small} \mid A) = \frac{87}{350} \approx 0.249$,
   $P(\text{large} \mid A) = \frac{263}{350} \approx 0.751$.
2. A's group rates: $\frac{81}{87} \approx 0.931$ and $\frac{192}{263} \approx 0.730$.

$$
P(S \mid A) \approx 0.931 \times 0.249 + 0.730 \times 0.751 \approx 0.232 + 0.548 = 0.780
$$

3. B's weights: $P(\text{small} \mid B) = \frac{270}{350} \approx 0.771$,
   $P(\text{large} \mid B) = \frac{80}{350} \approx 0.229$.
4. B's group rates: $\frac{234}{270} \approx 0.867$ and $\frac{55}{80} = 0.6875$.

$$
P(S \mid B) \approx 0.867 \times 0.771 + 0.6875 \times 0.229 \approx 0.669 + 0.157 = 0.826
$$

**Answer.** $P(S \mid A) = 0.78$ and $P(S \mid B) \approx 0.826$ (exactly $\frac{289}{350}$).

**Check.** If both treatments had A's mix (25% small), B would score
$0.867 \times 0.249 + 0.6875 \times 0.751 \approx 0.732$, below A's $0.78$. With the same
mix, A wins, as the proof says.

### Example 2: Comparing Two Machine-Learning Models

**Problem.** Two image classifiers are tested on different test sets. Model X gets 95
of 100 easy images right and 200 of 400 hard images right. Model Y gets 360 of 400 easy
images right and 40 of 100 hard images right. Which model is better?

**Solution.**

1. Easy images: X has $\frac{95}{100} = 0.95$; Y has $\frac{360}{400} = 0.90$. X is better.
2. Hard images: X has $\frac{200}{400} = 0.50$; Y has $\frac{40}{100} = 0.40$. X is better.
3. Overall: X has $\frac{95 + 200}{500} = \frac{295}{500} = 0.59$; Y has
   $\frac{360 + 40}{500} = \frac{400}{500} = 0.80$. Y looks better.

**Answer.** X is better on both kinds of image. Y's higher overall accuracy only comes
from being tested on mostly easy images (80% easy, against 20% for X).

**Check.** On a common test set with half easy and half hard images, X would score
$0.5 \times 0.95 + 0.5 \times 0.50 = 0.725$ and Y would score
$0.5 \times 0.90 + 0.5 \times 0.40 = 0.65$. Always compare models on the **same** test set.

## Simulate It

The code creates a large population with the kidney-stone group sizes (each group
1,000 times bigger) and success chances, then computes the rates. The reversal is not
an accident of small numbers.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
scale = 1_000
# (number of patients, success chance) for each treatment and stone size
groups = {"A small": (87, 81 / 87), "A large": (263, 192 / 263),
          "B small": (270, 234 / 270), "B large": (80, 55 / 80)}

successes = {}
for name, (n, p) in groups.items():
    successes[name] = rng.binomial(n * scale, p)
    print(f"{name}: success rate {successes[name] / (n * scale):.4f}")

for t in ["A", "B"]:
    total = successes[f"{t} small"] + successes[f"{t} large"]
    print(f"{t} overall: success rate {total / (350 * scale):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
A small: success rate 0.9326
A large: success rate 0.7306
B small: success rate 0.8673
B large: success rate 0.6874
A overall: success rate 0.7808
B overall: success rate 0.8262
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Comparing overall rates when the groups are mixed differently.** Always ask: does
  each option have the same mix of easy and hard cases?
- **Thinking the paradox means probability is broken.** Both tables are correct
  arithmetic. The overall rate is a weighted average, and different weights can reverse
  the order.
- **Always trusting the split table.** Splitting by a variable that is caused by the
  treatment can mislead too. Which table to use depends on how the data was produced.
- **Testing two models on different data.** Accuracy on different test sets is not
  comparable. Use the same test set, or compare within each type of input.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** model evaluation across data slices; fairness audits,
  where a model can look fair overall and unfair in every group, or the reverse.
- **Computer science:** A/B tests of websites, where traffic from mobile and desktop
  users may be split unevenly between versions.
- **Medicine:** observational studies, where doctors choose treatments based on how
  sick the patient is. Randomized trials avoid the problem.
- **Education and business:** comparing schools, hospitals or sales teams that serve
  different kinds of people.
- **Everyday life:** headlines like "group X does worse overall" may hide that X does
  better in every comparable subgroup
  ([22.9 Simpson's Paradox](../../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.9-Simpsons-Paradox.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In a test of a new drug, 90 of 100 young patients and 20 of 100 old
patients recover. What is the overall recovery rate? If instead 160 young and 40 old
patients were treated, with the same group rates, what would it be?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

First case: $\frac{90 + 20}{200} = 0.55$. Second case: the weights are $0.8$ and $0.2$:

$$0.9 \times 0.8 + 0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.72 + 0.04 = 0.76$$

The same drug looks much better with younger patients. The mix matters.

</details>

**2. (Medium)** Two cricket batters are compared by the share of balls they hit for a
boundary. Batter P: season 1, 4 of 10 balls; season 2, 30 of 100. Batter Q: season 1,
35 of 100; season 2, 2 of 10. Who is better each season, and who is better overall?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Season 1: P has $0.40$, Q has $0.35$. Season 2: P has $0.30$, Q has $0.20$. P is better
in both seasons. Overall:

$$P: \frac{4 + 30}{110} = \frac{34}{110} \approx 0.309, \qquad Q: \frac{35 + 2}{110} = \frac{37}{110} \approx 0.336$$

Q is better overall, because most of Q's balls came in the "easy" season 1.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Explain why randomly assigning patients to treatments prevents Simpson's
paradox (for large trials).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With random assignment, the chance of getting treatment A does not depend on the
group, so the mix of groups is about the same in both arms:
$P(G_i \mid A) \approx P(G_i \mid B) \approx P(G_i)$. With equal weights, "Why It Is True"
shows that if A is better in every group, it is also better overall. So the overall
comparison cannot reverse.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that in Example 2, X's overall accuracy must lie between $0.50$ and
$0.95$, whatever mix of easy and hard images it is tested on.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

X's overall accuracy is $0.95 w + 0.50 (1 - w)$, where $w$ is the share of easy images.
This is a weighted average, so it lies between the two group rates
([4.3](../4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)). At $w = 0$ it is $0.50$; at $w = 1$ it is $0.95$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose group 1 is easier for both options: $a_1 \gt a_2$ and $b_1 \gt b_2$,
and A is better in each group: $a_1 \gt b_1$, $a_2 \gt b_2$. Prove that a reversal
($P(S \mid A) \lt P(S \mid B)$ for some choice of mixes) is possible if and only if
$b_1 \gt a_2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A's overall rate is a weighted average, so it lies in $[a_2, a_1]$; B's lies in
$[b_2, b_1]$.

If $b_1 \le a_2$: then $P(S \mid B) \le b_1 \le a_2 \le P(S \mid A)$, so no reversal.

If $b_1 \gt a_2$: give A only group-2 cases ($w_A = 0$) and B only group-1 cases
($w_B = 1$). Then $P(S \mid A) = a_2 \lt b_1 = P(S \mid B)$, a reversal. (Mixes close to
these extremes work too.) In the kidney data, $b_1 \approx 0.867 \gt a_2 \approx 0.730$, so a
reversal was possible.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Example 2: give model X 100 easy and 400 hard images, model Y
400 easy and 100 hard, with the stated accuracies, scaled up 1,000 times. Print the
accuracies by group and overall.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
scale = 1_000
tests = {"X": [(100, 0.95), (400, 0.50)], "Y": [(400, 0.90), (100, 0.40)]}
for model, parts in tests.items():
    correct = [rng.binomial(n * scale, p) for n, p in parts]
    easy, hard = correct[0] / (parts[0][0] * scale), correct[1] / (parts[1][0] * scale)
    overall = sum(correct) / (500 * scale)
    print(f"Model {model}: easy {easy:.3f}, hard {hard:.3f}, overall {overall:.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
Model X: easy 0.951, hard 0.500, overall 0.590
Model Y: easy 0.900, hard 0.400, overall 0.800
```

</details>

## Summary

- Simpson's paradox: an option better in every group can be worse overall.
- The cause: overall rates are weighted averages, and each option has its own weights
  (its own mix of easy and hard cases).
- With the same mix of groups, no reversal can happen. Randomized experiments give
  equal mixes.
- Both the split and the combined tables are correct numbers; which one answers your
  question depends on how the data was produced.
- Compare like with like: same groups, same test set.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(S \mid A) = \sum_{i} P(S \mid A \cap G_i) \thinspace P(G_i \mid A)}
$$
