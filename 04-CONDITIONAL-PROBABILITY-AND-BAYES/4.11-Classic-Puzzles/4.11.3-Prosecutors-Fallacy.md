# 4.11.3 Prosecutor's Fallacy

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](../README.md) / [4.11 Classic Puzzles](README.md) / 4.11.3 Prosecutor's Fallacy

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.11.2 Medical Testing](4.11.2-Medical-Testing.md) and [4.5 Odds Form of Bayes](../4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)

> **Big idea:** "An innocent person would almost never match this evidence" does not mean "this person is almost surely guilty"; to get the chance of guilt you also need to know how many innocent people could have matched.

---

## Why It Matters

The **prosecutor's fallacy** is the mistake of treating $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$
as if it were $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$. It is the same swap as in
[medical testing](4.11.2-Medical-Testing.md), but in a courtroom, where it has
helped send innocent people to prison. Its mirror image, the **defense attorney's
fallacy**, throws away strong evidence. Knowing both makes you a better judge of
any argument built on "this would be so unlikely by chance".

## Intuition

A crime happens in a city of 10 million people. The criminal left DNA at the
scene. The DNA profile is rare: only 1 person in a million has it. The police search
a DNA database, find a man whose profile matches, and arrest him. There is no other
evidence against him.

The prosecutor tells the jury: "The chance that an innocent person matches is 1 in a
million. So the chance that he is innocent is 1 in a million. He is guilty beyond
doubt."

Let us count. In a city of 10 million, about $10{,}000{,}000 \times \frac{1}{1{,}000{,}000} = 10$
**innocent** people also have this profile, plus the one real criminal. So about 11
people in the city match. Without other evidence, the arrested man is just one of
these 11. His chance of being the criminal is about $\frac{1}{11} \approx 0.09$, not
$0.999999$.

The evidence is still strong: it moved him from 1 in 10 million to 1 in 11. But
"1 in a million by chance" was answering the wrong question.

## Picture

```text
   City of 10,000,000 people.  The profile is shared by 1 in 1,000,000.

   innocent people   9,999,999  -- x 1/1,000,000 -->  about 10 match
   the criminal              1  -- x 1 ----------->         1 matches
                                                     ----------------
                                         all matches:  about 11

   P(guilty | match)    =  1 / 11  ≈  0.09        (the right question)
   P(match | innocent)  =  1 / 1,000,000          (the prosecutor's number)
```

The prosecutor's number is about the row of innocent people; the jury's question is about the column of matches.

## Definition

Let $G$ be the event that the defendant is guilty, $I = G^c$ the event that the
defendant is innocent, and $E$ the event that the evidence matches the defendant.

The **prosecutor's fallacy** is to claim:

$$
P(I \mid E) \approx P(E \mid I) \qquad \text{(WRONG in general)}
$$

**In words:** "the evidence would be very rare if he were innocent, so he is very
unlikely to be innocent." This skips the prior.

The correct rule is Bayes' theorem in odds form ([4.5](../4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)):

$$
\frac{P(G \mid E)}{P(I \mid E)} = \frac{P(E \mid G)}{P(E \mid I)} \times \frac{P(G)}{P(I)}
$$

**In words:** the odds of guilt after the evidence are the likelihood ratio of the
evidence times the odds of guilt before it.

For a DNA match, $P(E \mid G) = 1$ and $P(E \mid I) = m$, the **random match
probability**. So the likelihood ratio is $\frac{1}{m}$, often huge. The court must
combine it with the prior odds, which come from all the other evidence.

The **defense attorney's fallacy** is the opposite mistake: "since about 10 other
people also match, the DNA evidence is worthless". It ignores that the evidence
multiplied the odds of guilt by $\frac{1}{m}$.

## Why It Is True

Suppose that, before the DNA test, the defendant is just one of $N$ people who could
have done it, each equally likely. So $P(G) = \frac{1}{N}$. The criminal matches for
sure, and each innocent person matches with probability $m$. By Bayes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(G \mid E) &= \frac{P(E \mid G) \thinspace P(G)}{P(E \mid G) \thinspace P(G) + P(E \mid I) \thinspace P(I)} && \text{(Bayes, two hypotheses)} \\
&= \frac{1 \cdot \frac{1}{N}}{1 \cdot \frac{1}{N} + m \cdot \frac{N - 1}{N}} && \text{(the numbers above)} \\
&= \frac{1}{1 + (N - 1) m} && \text{(multiply top and bottom by } N \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**In words:** $(N - 1) m$ is the expected number of innocent people who match. The
chance of guilt is 1 divided by "1 plus the number of innocent matches".

With $N = 10{,}000{,}000$ and $m = 10^{-6}$: $(N - 1) m \approx 10$, so
$P(G \mid E) \approx \frac{1}{11} \approx 0.0909$. The prosecutor's claim, $1 - m = 0.999999$,
would be right only if $(N - 1) m$ were tiny, that is, if almost nobody else could
have matched.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Cold Hit in a Big City

**Problem.** Use the city above: $N = 10{,}000{,}000$ possible suspects, random match
probability $m = \frac{1}{1{,}000{,}000}$, no other evidence. Find $P(G \mid E)$ with the
odds form.

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds of guilt: $\frac{1}{N - 1} = \frac{1}{9{,}999{,}999}$.
2. Likelihood ratio: $\frac{P(E \mid G)}{P(E \mid I)} = \frac{1}{10^{-6}} = 1{,}000{,}000$.
3. Posterior odds:

$$
\frac{1{,}000{,}000}{9{,}999{,}999} \approx 0.1
$$

4. Probability: $\frac{0.1}{1 + 0.1} \approx 0.0909$.

**Answer.** About 9%. Without other evidence, the man is probably innocent.

**Check.** Same as the count of 11 matches. The simulation below gets the same answer
in a smaller city.

### Example 2: The Defense Attorney's Fallacy

**Problem.** Now there is other evidence. Before the DNA test, witnesses and phone
records narrow the suspects to 1,000 people, and the defendant is one of them, so his
prior is $\frac{1}{1000}$. The defense lawyer says: "In the whole city, about 10 innocent
people match. So the DNA only shows a 1 in 11 chance. It means nothing." Is this right?

**Solution.**

1. The DNA evidence must be combined with the **actual** prior, not the city-wide one.
2. Prior odds: $\frac{1}{999}$. Likelihood ratio: $1{,}000{,}000$.
3. Posterior odds:

$$
\frac{1{,}000{,}000}{999} \approx 1001
$$

4. Probability: $\frac{1001}{1002} \approx 0.999$.

**Answer.** About 99.9%. Combined with the other evidence, the DNA is close to decisive.
The lawyer's argument is a fallacy.

**Check.** Among the 999 other suspects, the expected number of innocent matches is
$999 \times 10^{-6} \approx 0.001$. So it is very unlikely that anyone else in the
suspect group matches. The same DNA evidence gives 9% in Example 1 and 99.9% here; only
the prior differs.

### Example 3: Two Errors in One Number

**Problem.** In a well-known British case, Sally Clark was convicted in 1999 of
murdering her two baby sons, who had died suddenly. A medical expert told the court
that the chance of one sudden infant death (SIDS) in a family like hers was about
1 in 8,543, so the chance of two was $\left(\frac{1}{8543}\right)^2$, about 1 in 73 million.
Find the two probability errors. (Her conviction was overturned in 2003.)

**Solution.**

1. **The squaring assumes independence.** $8543^2 = 72{,}982{,}849$, so the number is
   "1 in 73 million" only if the two deaths are independent. But families share genes
   and home conditions, so after one SIDS death, a second is **more** likely than
   1 in 8,543. For illustration only: if the second chance were 1 in 100, the joint
   chance would be $\frac{1}{8543} \times \frac{1}{100} = \frac{1}{854{,}300}$, about 85 times
   larger.
2. **The number answers the wrong question.** "1 in 73 million" is
   $P(\text{two deaths} \mid \text{innocent})$. The court needs
   $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{two deaths})$. Two babies did die, so the right comparison
   is between the two explanations, **both** very rare: two natural deaths, or two
   murders by a mother. In odds form, what matters is the **ratio** of the chances of
   the two explanations, not how small one of them is.

**Answer.** An unjustified independence assumption, and the prosecutor's fallacy.

**Check.** In 2001 the Royal Statistical Society publicly criticized the use of this
number in court. A rare event under innocence is evidence only if it is **much rarer**
than under guilt.

## Simulate It

A city of 10 million is too big for a quick simulation, so the code uses a city of
10,000 people with a profile shared by 1 in 1,000. Then $(N - 1) m = 9.999$ and the
formula gives $\frac{1}{1 + 9.999} \approx 0.0909$, the same as Example 1.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_cases = 10_000_000
city_size, match_prob = 10_000, 1 / 1_000

# The police pick one random person; with chance 1/city_size it is the criminal.
guilty = rng.random(n_cases) < 1 / city_size
# The criminal always matches; an innocent person matches by chance.
matches = guilty | (rng.random(n_cases) < match_prob)

estimate = guilty[matches].mean()
exact = 1 / (1 + (city_size - 1) * match_prob)
print(f"Cases with a match: {matches.sum():,}")
print(f"P(guilty | match):  simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"Prosecutor's claim: {1 - match_prob:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Cases with a match: 10,930
P(guilty | match):  simulation 0.0909, exact 0.0909
Prosecutor's claim: 0.9990
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Swapping the condition.** $P(E \mid I)$ small does not make $P(I \mid E)$ small. Always
  ask: "How many innocent people could have produced this evidence?"
- **Throwing the evidence away (defense attorney's fallacy).** A likelihood ratio of a
  million is very strong. It must be combined with the other evidence, not ignored.
- **Multiplying probabilities that are not independent.** Squaring "1 in 8,543" assumed
  independence between deaths in the same family. Rare events in the same family,
  building or computer system are often linked.
- **Forgetting other sources of error.** If the lab mixes up samples 1 time in 1,000,
  then a "1 in a million" match is really closer to "1 in 1,000" evidence (Practice 5).
- **Searching large databases and then quoting the match probability.** Comparing a
  sample with a million profiles makes a chance match likely (Practice 3).

## Where It Is Used

- **Law and forensics:** DNA, fingerprints, fibers and handwriting are reported as
  likelihood ratios. Courts are taught to combine them with prior odds and to avoid
  both fallacies.
- **AI and machine learning:** an anomaly detector says "this login is very unusual
  for a normal user". That is $P(\text{data} \mid \text{normal})$. Whether it is an
  attack also depends on how rare attacks are.
- **Computer science:** hash collisions and checksums: "two files with the same hash
  are almost surely identical" is fine for one comparison, but with billions of
  comparisons, chance collisions appear (the birthday effect).
- **Science:** a small p-value says the data would be rare if there were no effect.
  It is not the probability that there is no effect.
- **Everyday life:** "What are the chances?" stories. With millions of people, rare
  coincidences happen to someone every day.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A blood type found at a crime scene is shared by 1 in 10,000 people. A
city has 500,000 possible suspects, and a man is arrested only because his blood type
matches. With no other evidence, what is $P(G \mid E)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expected innocent matches: $(500000 - 1) \times \frac{1}{10000} \approx 50$.

$$P(G \mid E) = \frac{1}{1 + 49.9999} \approx \frac{1}{51} \approx 0.0196$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A news story says: "If the coin were fair, getting 9 or more heads in 10
flips would happen only about 1% of the time, so there is a 99% chance the coin is
biased." Name the mistake.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is the prosecutor's fallacy. "About 1%" is $P(\text{data} \mid \text{fair})$, but the
claim is about $P(\text{fair} \mid \text{data})$. That needs the prior chance that a coin is
biased and the chance of the data under the biased hypothesis. Most coins are fair, so
the posterior chance of bias may be much lower than 99%.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Police compare a crime-scene profile with a database of 1,000,000
innocent people. Each matches by chance with probability $10^{-6}$, independently.
What is the chance that at least one innocent person in the database matches?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (1 - 10^{-6})^{1000000} \approx 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$$

A "one in a million" match is likely to happen by chance when you search a million
profiles.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Other evidence narrows the suspects to 500 people, each equally likely,
and the defendant's DNA matches with $m = 10^{-6}$. Find $P(G \mid E)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prior odds $\frac{1}{499}$, likelihood ratio $10^6$:

$$\text{odds} = \frac{10^6}{499} \approx 2004, \qquad P(G \mid E) = \frac{2004}{2005} \approx 0.9995$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** In Practice 4, suppose the lab also reports a false match with probability
$10^{-3}$ because of sample mix-ups, independently of the DNA itself. Then an innocent
person "matches" with probability about $m' = 10^{-6} + 10^{-3}$. Find the new
$P(G \mid E)$ and explain what this shows.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The likelihood ratio drops to $\frac{1}{m'} = \frac{1}{0.001001} \approx 999$:

$$\text{odds} = \frac{999}{499} \approx 2.00, \qquad P(G \mid E) = \frac{2.00}{3.00} \approx 0.667$$

The evidence is only as strong as its **weakest** error source. A one-in-a-million
profile is worth little if the lab makes a mistake one time in a thousand.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Practice 3: for many databases of 1,000,000 innocent people,
count how often at least one person matches by chance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_databases = 1_000_000
# The number of chance matches in one database is Bin(1,000,000, 1e-6).
chance_matches = rng.binomial(1_000_000, 1e-6, size=n_databases)
print(f"P(at least one match): simulation {np.mean(chance_matches >= 1):.4f}, "
      f"exact {1 - (1 - 1e-6) ** 1_000_000:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(at least one match): simulation 0.6323, exact 0.6321
```

</details>

## Summary

- The prosecutor's fallacy swaps $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$ with
  $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$.
- The fix is Bayes in odds form: posterior odds = likelihood ratio x prior odds.
- With $N$ possible suspects and match probability $m$, a match alone gives
  $P(G \mid E) = \frac{1}{1 + (N - 1) m}$: count the innocent people who would also match.
- The defense attorney's fallacy throws away strong evidence; the same likelihood ratio
  can be decisive when combined with other evidence.
- Check independence and other error sources before quoting tiny numbers.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\frac{P(G \mid E)}{P(I \mid E)} = \frac{P(E \mid G)}{P(E \mid I)} \times \frac{P(G)}{P(I)}}
$$
