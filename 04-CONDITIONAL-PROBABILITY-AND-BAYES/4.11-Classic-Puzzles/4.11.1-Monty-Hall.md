# 4.11.1 Monty Hall

[Probability](../../README.md) / [Module 04: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes](../README.md) / [4.11 Classic Puzzles](README.md) / 4.11.1 Monty Hall

**Level:** 1 (Zero) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [4.10 Tree Diagrams](../4.10-Tree-Diagrams.md) and [4.4 Bayes' Theorem](../4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)

> **Big idea:** When the host knowingly opens a door with a goat, switching wins 2 times out of 3, because the host's choice carries information about where the car is.

---

## Why It Matters

The **Monty Hall problem** is the most famous puzzle in conditional probability.
When it appeared in a popular magazine in 1990, thousands of readers, including
many with doctorates, wrote in to say the correct answer was wrong. It teaches one
lesson you will use forever: **to update correctly, you must model how the evidence
was produced**, not just what you saw.

## Intuition

You are on a game show. There are three closed doors. Behind one is a car; behind
the other two are goats. You pick door 1. The host, who **knows** where the car is,
opens door 3 and shows a goat. Then he asks: "Do you want to switch to door 2?"

Most people think: "Two doors are left, so it is 50-50. Switching does not matter."
But think about your first pick. It was right with chance $\frac{1}{3}$ and wrong with
chance $\frac{2}{3}$. The host's action cannot change that.

- If your first pick was **right** (chance $\frac{1}{3}$), switching loses.
- If your first pick was **wrong** (chance $\frac{2}{3}$), the car is behind one of the
  other two doors, and the host **must** open the other one, the goat. So the closed
  door he leaves is the car. Switching wins.

So switching wins with chance $\frac{2}{3}$. To feel it, imagine 100 doors. You pick
one. The host, who knows, opens 98 of the other 99 doors, all goats, and leaves one
closed. Would you stay with your random first pick, or take the one door he
carefully avoided?

## Picture

```text
  car is     host opens                              stick   switch  chance
  behind     (you picked door 1)
                    +-- 1/2 --> door 2               WIN     lose    1/6
     +-- 1/3 --> 1 -+
     |              +-- 1/2 --> door 3               WIN     lose    1/6
     |
 ----+-- 1/3 --> 2 ---- 1 ----> door 3 (must)        lose    WIN     1/3
     |
     +-- 1/3 --> 3 ---- 1 ----> door 2 (must)        lose    WIN     1/3
                                                     ----    ----
                                     total chance:   1/3     2/3
```

Sticking wins only on the top two paths, worth $\frac{1}{3}$ in total; switching wins on the bottom two, worth $\frac{2}{3}$.

## Definition

The answer depends on the exact rules, so we state them as assumptions:

1. The car is placed behind one of the 3 doors at random, each with chance $\frac{1}{3}$.
2. You pick a door (say door 1).
3. The host knows where the car is. He **always** opens a door that you did not pick
   **and** that has a goat.
4. If he has two such doors (your pick was the car), he chooses between them at
   random, each with chance $\frac{1}{2}$.
5. He **always** offers you the switch.

Let $C_i$ be the event that the car is behind door $i$, and $O_3$ the event that the
host opens door 3. Under
these rules:

$$
P(C_1 \mid O_3) = \frac{1}{3}, \qquad P(C_2 \mid O_3) = \frac{2}{3}
$$

**In words:** after the host opens door 3, your door still has its old chance
$\frac{1}{3}$, and the remaining closed door has $\frac{2}{3}$. Switching doubles your
chance of winning.

## Why It Is True

Use [Bayes' theorem](../4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md) with the three hypotheses $C_1, C_2, C_3$
and the evidence $O_3$. The likelihoods come from the host's rules:

- $P(O_3 \mid C_1) = \frac{1}{2}$: the car is behind your door, so the host picks door 2
  or 3 at random.
- $P(O_3 \mid C_2) = 1$: he cannot open your door 1 or the car door 2, so he **must**
  open door 3.
- $P(O_3 \mid C_3) = 0$: he never opens the car door.

The Bayes table:

| Hypothesis | Prior | Likelihood $P(O_3 \mid C_i)$ | Prior x likelihood | Posterior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $C_1$ (your door) | $\frac{1}{3}$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $\frac{1}{6}$ | $\frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3}$ |
| $C_2$ (other closed door) | $\frac{1}{3}$ | $1$ | $\frac{1}{3}$ | $\frac{1/3}{1/2} = \frac{2}{3}$ |
| $C_3$ (opened door) | $\frac{1}{3}$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| **Sum** | 1 | | $\frac{1}{2}$ | 1 |

The sum of the "prior x likelihood" column is $P(O_3) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} + 0 = \frac{1}{2}$.
Dividing gives the posteriors. In odds form ([4.5](../4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)): the
prior odds of door 2 against door 1 are 1 to 1, and the likelihood ratio is
$\frac{1}{1/2} = 2$, so the posterior odds are 2 to 1.

**Why your door stays at $\frac{1}{3}$.** The host can always open a goat door that is
not yours, whether or not you picked the car. So the fact that he opened **some**
goat door tells you nothing about your own door. But **which** door he opened tells
you something about the other two: he avoided door 2, which is more likely if door 2
has the car.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: 300 Shows

**Problem.** Imagine 300 shows where the contestant picks door 1. Use natural
frequencies to find how often switching wins, among the shows where the host opens
door 3.

**Solution.**

1. The car is behind door 1 in about 100 shows, door 2 in 100, door 3 in 100.
2. Car behind door 1: the host opens door 3 in half of them, **50** shows.
3. Car behind door 2: the host must open door 3, **100** shows.
4. Car behind door 3: the host never opens door 3, **0** shows.
5. So the host opens door 3 in $50 + 100 = 150$ shows. Switching (to door 2) wins in
   the 100 where the car is behind door 2.

$$
P(\text{switch wins} \mid O_3) = \frac{100}{150} = \frac{2}{3}
$$

**Answer.** Switching wins in 100 of the 150 shows, which is $\frac{2}{3}$.

**Check.** The same count works for "the host opens door 2", by symmetry. So switching
wins $\frac{2}{3}$ of all shows.

### Example 2: Monty Falls (the Host Does Not Know)

**Problem.** Change rule 3. The host does **not** know where the car is. He opens one
of doors 2 and 3 at random, and it **happens** to show a goat. Should you switch now?

**Solution.**

1. Let $G_3$ = "the host opens door 3 and it shows a goat".
2. Likelihoods: $P(G_3 \mid C_1) = \frac{1}{2}$ (he picks door 3 with chance $\frac{1}{2}$; it has
   a goat). $P(G_3 \mid C_2) = \frac{1}{2}$ (he picks door 3 with chance $\frac{1}{2}$; it has a
   goat). $P(G_3 \mid C_3) = 0$ (door 3 would show the car).
3. Priors are equal, so the posteriors are proportional to the likelihoods
   $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, 0$:

$$
P(C_1 \mid G_3) = \frac{1/2}{1/2 + 1/2} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(C_2 \mid G_3) = \frac{1}{2}
$$

**Answer.** Now it really is 50-50. Switching neither helps nor hurts.

**Check.** You saw exactly the same thing (door 3, a goat) as in the real game, but the
answer changed. The only difference is **how** the evidence was produced. A host who
knows gives you information by avoiding the car; a host who guesses does not.

### Example 3: A Host With a Favorite Door

**Problem.** Back to the knowing host, but now, when your door 1 has the car, he
opens door 3 with probability $q$ (and door 2 with probability $1 - q$), for some
$0 \le q \le 1$. He opens door 3. What is $P(C_1 \mid O_3)$?

**Solution.**

1. Likelihoods: $P(O_3 \mid C_1) = q$, $P(O_3 \mid C_2) = 1$, $P(O_3 \mid C_3) = 0$.
2. Equal priors cancel:

$$
P(C_1 \mid O_3) = \frac{q}{q + 1}, \qquad P(C_2 \mid O_3) = \frac{1}{q + 1}
$$

3. For $q = \frac{1}{2}$ (the usual host): $\frac{1/2}{3/2} = \frac{1}{3}$. For $q = 1$ (he always
   opens door 3 when he can): $\frac{1}{2}$. For $q = 0$ (he opens door 3 only when forced):
   $0$, so the car is surely behind door 2.

**Answer.** $P(C_1 \mid O_3) = \frac{q}{1 + q}$, which is never more than $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Check.** Since $\frac{q}{1 + q} \le \frac{1}{2}$ for every $q$ in $[0, 1]$, switching is
never worse than sticking, whatever the host's habit.

## Simulate It

The code plays many shows under both rule sets: the knowing host, and the random
host of Example 2 (keeping only the shows where he happens to reveal a goat).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_shows = 1_000_000
car = rng.integers(1, 4, size=n_shows)          # you always pick door 1

# Knowing host: if car is at 1, open 2 or 3 at random; else open the goat door.
random_23 = rng.integers(2, 4, size=n_shows)
opened = np.where(car == 1, random_23, 5 - car)  # 5 - 2 = 3 and 5 - 3 = 2
switch_to = 5 - opened                           # the other closed door
print(f"Knowing host: stick {np.mean(car == 1):.4f}, switch {np.mean(car == switch_to):.4f}"
      f"   (exact 0.3333, 0.6667)")

# Random host: opens 2 or 3 at random; keep only shows where a goat appears.
opened = rng.integers(2, 4, size=n_shows)
goat = opened != car
switch_to = 5 - opened
print(f"Random host:  stick {np.mean(car[goat] == 1):.4f}, "
      f"switch {np.mean(car[goat] == switch_to[goat]):.4f}   (exact 0.5000, 0.5000)")
```

Output:

```text
Knowing host: stick 0.3337, switch 0.6663   (exact 0.3333, 0.6667)
Random host:  stick 0.5005, switch 0.4995   (exact 0.5000, 0.5000)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **"Two doors left, so 50-50."** Two possible outcomes are not always equally likely.
  The equal-chance rule from [1.1](../../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.1-What-is-Probability.md)
  needs a reason for symmetry, and here the host's rules break the symmetry.
- **Ignoring how the evidence was produced.** "A goat behind door 3" means different
  things for a host who knows and a host who guesses (Example 2). Always ask: what
  process created this information?
- **Thinking the host's action changes your door's chance.** Your door's chance stays
  $\frac{1}{3}$; the host's choice moves the other $\frac{2}{3}$ onto the one closed door
  he left.
- **Changing the rules without noticing.** If the host only offers a switch when you
  picked the car (a trick host), switching always loses. State the rules first.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the main lesson, "model how the data was produced", is
  the core of handling **missing data** and **selection bias**. A dataset of only the
  emails a user opened, or only the loans a bank approved, is evidence filtered by a
  "host".
- **Computer science:** in A/B tests and recommendation systems, the system chooses
  what to show, so the data you collect depends on its choices.
- **Games and poker:** an opponent's action (a bet, a check) is evidence whose meaning
  depends on their strategy, exactly like the host's choice of door.
- **Everyday life:** when someone shows you some information, ask why they chose to
  show **that** piece. A salesperson who knows the facts is a "knowing host".

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** There are 4 doors and one car. You pick door 1. The knowing host opens
one goat door (not yours) and offers you a switch to **one of the two** other closed
doors, which you pick at random. Find the chance of winning if you stick and if you
switch.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Stick: you win only if your first pick was right, chance $\frac{1}{4}$.

Switch: your first pick was wrong with chance $\frac{3}{4}$. Then the car is behind one of
the 2 other closed doors, and you pick one of them at random:

$$P(\text{switch wins}) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{8}$$

Switching still helps: $\frac{3}{8} \gt \frac{2}{8}$.

</details>

**2. (Medium)** With 100 doors, you pick one, and the knowing host opens 98 goat doors
among the other 99, leaving one closed. What is the chance that switching wins?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Switching loses only if your first pick was the car, which has chance $\frac{1}{100}$.
Otherwise the host must leave the car door closed:

$$P(\text{switch wins}) = 1 - \frac{1}{100} = \frac{99}{100}$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Example 3, find $P(C_1 \mid O_2)$, the chance that your door has the
car given that the host opens door 2.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Now $P(O_2 \mid C_1) = 1 - q$, $P(O_2 \mid C_3) = 1$ (forced), $P(O_2 \mid C_2) = 0$. Equal
priors cancel:

$$P(C_1 \mid O_2) = \frac{1 - q}{(1 - q) + 1} = \frac{1 - q}{2 - q}$$

This is also at most $\frac{1}{2}$, so switching is never worse.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Use the law of total probability to show that, under the standard rules,
the chance that the always-switch strategy wins is $\frac{2}{3}$, without using Bayes.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Split on where the car is. If it is behind your door (chance $\frac{1}{3}$), switching
loses. If it is behind either other door (chance $\frac{2}{3}$ in total), the host must open
the remaining goat door, and switching wins:

$$P(\text{switch wins}) = 0 \times \frac{1}{3} + 1 \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** **Three prisoners.** Prisoners A, B and C know that one of them, chosen
at random, will be freed. A asks the guard, who knows, to name one of B and C who
will **not** be freed (if both will not, the guard picks one at random). The guard
says "B". A thinks: "Now it is me or C, so my chance went up to $\frac{1}{2}$." Is A right?
What is C's chance?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

This is Monty Hall in disguise. Let $F_X$ = "X is freed" and $N_B$ = "the guard names B".
Likelihoods: $P(N_B \mid F_A) = \frac{1}{2}$, $P(N_B \mid F_B) = 0$, $P(N_B \mid F_C) = 1$.
Equal priors cancel:

$$P(F_A \mid N_B) = \frac{1/2}{1/2 + 0 + 1} = \frac{1}{3}, \qquad P(F_C \mid N_B) = \frac{1}{3/2} = \frac{2}{3}$$

A is wrong: his chance stays $\frac{1}{3}$, because the guard could always name someone.
C's chance rose to $\frac{2}{3}$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the 4-door game of Problem 1 and check $\frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{3}{8}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_shows = 200_000
wins_stick = wins_switch = 0
for _ in range(n_shows):                           # a plain loop, for clarity
    car = rng.integers(4)                          # doors 0..3; you pick door 0
    goats = [d for d in (1, 2, 3) if d != car]     # goat doors you did not pick
    opened = goats[rng.integers(len(goats))]
    options = [d for d in (1, 2, 3) if d != opened]  # the two other closed doors
    new_pick = options[rng.integers(2)]
    wins_stick += car == 0
    wins_switch += car == new_pick
print(f"stick:  {wins_stick / n_shows:.4f}  (exact {1 / 4:.4f})")
print(f"switch: {wins_switch / n_shows:.4f}  (exact {3 / 8:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
stick:  0.2510  (exact 0.2500)
switch: 0.3740  (exact 0.3750)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Under the standard rules (a knowing host who always opens a goat door and always
  offers a switch), switching wins with chance $\frac{2}{3}$.
- Bayes with likelihoods $\frac{1}{2}, 1, 0$ gives posteriors $\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, 0$.
- Your own door keeps its chance $\frac{1}{3}$, because the host could always open some
  goat door.
- If the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat, the answer
  becomes $\frac{1}{2}$: the same sight, a different process, a different answer.
- The lesson: always model how the evidence was produced.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(C_2 \mid O_3) = \frac{P(O_3 \mid C_2) P(C_2)}{\sum_{i} P(O_3 \mid C_i) P(C_i)} = \frac{1 \cdot \frac{1}{3}}{\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 1 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 0} = \frac{2}{3}}
$$
