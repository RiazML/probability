# 12.3 Mahalanobis Distance

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.3 Mahalanobis Distance

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [12.2 Geometry and Contours](12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md)

> **Big idea:** The Mahalanobis distance measures how far a point is from the mean in units of the data's own spread and correlation, and for a Gaussian its square follows a chi-square distribution.

---

## Why It Matters

"How unusual is this point?" is one of the most common questions in data
work: fraud detection, broken sensors, strange network traffic, outliers in
a dataset. Ordinary (Euclidean) distance gives the wrong answer when the
variables have different spreads or are correlated. The **Mahalanobis
distance** fixes both problems, and because its distribution is known
exactly, it also tells you **how rare** a point is.

## Intuition

In one dimension you already know the answer: the **z-score**. A
temperature of 30 degrees in a city with mean 25 and SD 2 is
$\frac{30 - 25}{2} = 2.5$ SDs above the mean: unusual. The same 30 degrees in
a city with SD 10 is only 0.5 SDs away: normal. Distance must be measured in
SDs, not in degrees.

In two dimensions, correlation matters too. A server's CPU use and memory
use go up and down together (correlation 0.8). Server B has high CPU and high
memory: that is the normal pattern, just a busy day. Server A has high CPU but
**low** memory: that combination almost never happens, so something is
wrong. Server A is closer to the average in plain distance, yet it is far more
suspicious. The Mahalanobis distance captures this: it is the "number of SDs"
measured along the shape of the data cloud.

## Definition

Let $\mathbf{X}$ have mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$
(positive definite). The **Mahalanobis distance** of a point $\mathbf{x}$ from
the mean is

$$
D(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})}
$$

where $\Sigma^{-1}$ is the inverse of the covariance matrix (see the matrix
tools in [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md)). Usually we work
with its square, $D^2(\mathbf{x})$.

**In words:** it is the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to the center, after the
data has been reshaped so that every direction has SD 1 and no correlation.

Three special cases show what it means:

| Case | Mahalanobis distance |
|---|---|
| $d = 1$, variance $\sigma^2$ | $\lvert x - \mu \rvert / \sigma$, the z-score |
| diagonal $\Sigma$ (independent) | $\sqrt{z_1^2 + \dots + z_d^2}$ with $z_i = (x_i - \mu_i)/\sigma_i$ |
| $\Sigma = I$ | the ordinary Euclidean distance $\lVert \mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} \rVert$ |

The Gaussian PDF of [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md) can be
written as $f(\mathbf{x}) \propto e^{-D^2(\mathbf{x})/2}$. So points with the
same Mahalanobis distance have the same density: the contours of
[12.2](12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md) are exactly the sets $D(\mathbf{x}) = c$.

**The key probability fact.** If $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$
in $d$ dimensions, then

$$
D^2(\mathbf{X}) \sim \chi^2(d)
$$

the **chi-square distribution** with $d$ degrees of freedom: the distribution
of $Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_d^2$ for independent standard normals $Z_i$ (see
[9.6 Chi-Square, Student-t and F](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)).

**In words:** the squared Mahalanobis distance of a Gaussian point behaves
like a sum of $d$ squared standard normals, so its average is $d$.

In two dimensions this has a simple closed form:

$$
P\big( D^2(\mathbf{X}) \le c^2 \big) = 1 - e^{-c^2/2} \qquad (d = 2)
$$

## Why It Is True

**Step 1: whitening.** Choose a matrix $L$ with $L L^\top = \Sigma$ (for
example the Cholesky factor of
[12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)) and set
$\mathbf{z} = L^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$. This is called
**whitening**: it removes the stretch and tilt. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D^2(\mathbf{x}) &= (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top (L L^\top)^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(since } \Sigma = LL^\top \text{)} \\
&= (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top (L^{-1})^\top L^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(inverse of a product)} \\
&= \big( L^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \big)^\top \big( L^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \big) = \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{z} && \text{(since } (M\mathbf{v})^\top = \mathbf{v}^\top M^\top \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the Mahalanobis distance is the plain Euclidean length of the whitened
vector $\mathbf{z}$.

**Step 2: the whitened vector is standard normal.** If
$\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$ with $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$,
then $L^{-1}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \mathbf{Z}$. So
$D^2(\mathbf{X}) = Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_d^2$ with independent standard normals.
That is the definition of $\chi^2(d)$. Its mean is
$E[Z_1^2] + \dots + E[Z_d^2] = 1 + \dots + 1 = d$.

**Step 3: the 2-D formula.** For $d = 2$, the probability that
$Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 \le c^2$ is the integral of the round density over a disk of
radius $c$. In polar coordinates ($z_1 = s\cos\theta$, $z_2 = s\sin\theta$,
area element $s \thinspace ds \thinspace d\theta$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 \le c^2) &= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{c} \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-s^2/2} \thinspace s \thinspace ds \thinspace d\theta && \text{(polar coordinates)} \\
&= \int_0^{c} e^{-s^2/2} \thinspace s \thinspace ds && \text{(the } \theta \text{ integral gives } 2\pi \text{)} \\
&= \Big[ -e^{-s^2/2} \Big]_0^{c} = 1 - e^{-c^2/2} && \text{(antiderivative of } s e^{-s^2/2} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Useful values, in two dimensions and in general:

| $d$ | inside $D \le 1$ | inside $D \le 2$ | inside $D \le 3$ | $D^2$ cut-off for 95% | $D^2$ cut-off for 99% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.683 | 0.954 | 0.997 | 3.84 | 6.63 |
| 2 | 0.393 | 0.865 | 0.989 | 5.99 | 9.21 |
| 3 | 0.199 | 0.739 | 0.971 | 7.81 | 11.34 |
| 10 | 0.0002 | 0.053 | 0.468 | 18.31 | 23.21 |

The more dimensions, the farther a typical point is from the center. In 10
dimensions almost no point lies within distance 1.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Same Euclidean Distance, Different Mahalanobis Distance

**Problem.** Let $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$ and let $\Sigma$ have
variances 2 and covariance 1, as in [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md).
Compare the points $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$.

**Solution.**

1. Euclidean distance: both are $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.
2. $\Sigma^{-1}$ is $\frac{1}{3}$ times the matrix with rows $(2, -1)$ and $(-1, 2)$.
3. For $(1, 1)$: $D^2 = \frac{1}{3}(2 - 2 + 2) = \frac{2}{3}$, so $D = \sqrt{2/3} \approx 0.816$.
4. For $(1, -1)$: $D^2 = \frac{1}{3}(2 + 2 + 2) = 2$, so $D = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.

**Answer.** $(1, 1)$ is at Mahalanobis distance $0.816$, and $(1, -1)$ at
$1.414$. The second point is "farther" in the sense that matters.

**Check.** $(1, 1)$ lies along the long axis of the ellipses (the direction
with variance 3), $(1, -1)$ along the short axis (variance 1). Along the short
axis, the same step is more SDs: $\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{1} = 1.414$ versus
$\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{3} = 0.816$. These are exactly the numbers above.

### Example 2: Finding a Broken Server

**Problem.** Healthy servers have CPU use and memory use (in percent)
distributed as a bivariate normal with the mean and covariance below (both SDs
are 10, correlation 0.8). Server A shows $(70, 50)$ and server B shows
$(70, 75)$. Flag a server if its point is outside the 99% contour.

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 50 \cr 60 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 100 & 80 \cr 80 & 100 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. $\det \Sigma = 100 \cdot 100 - 80 \cdot 80 = 3600$, so $\Sigma^{-1}$ is
   $\frac{1}{3600}$ times the matrix with rows $(100, -80)$ and $(-80, 100)$.
2. The 99% cut-off for $d = 2$ solves $1 - e^{-c^2/2} = 0.99$, so
   $c^2 = -2\ln(0.01) \approx 9.21$.
3. Server A: $\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} = (20, -10)$.

$$
D^2 = \frac{100 \cdot 20^2 - 2 \cdot 80 \cdot 20 \cdot (-10) + 100 \cdot (-10)^2}{3600} = \frac{40000 + 32000 + 10000}{3600} = \frac{82000}{3600} \approx 22.78
$$

4. Server B: $\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} = (20, 15)$.

$$
D^2 = \frac{100 \cdot 20^2 - 2 \cdot 80 \cdot 20 \cdot 15 + 100 \cdot 15^2}{3600} = \frac{40000 - 48000 + 22500}{3600} = \frac{14500}{3600} \approx 4.03
$$

5. Euclidean distances: A is $\sqrt{400 + 100} \approx 22.4$, B is $\sqrt{400 + 225} = 25$.

**Answer.** Server A has $D \approx 4.77$ and $D^2 \approx 22.78 \gt 9.21$:
flag it. A healthy server is this far out with probability
$e^{-22.78/2} \approx 0.00001$. Server B has $D \approx 2.01$ and
$D^2 \approx 4.03 \lt 9.21$: it is fine (a healthy server is at least this far
out with probability $e^{-4.03/2} \approx 0.13$).

**Check.** Euclidean distance would call B more unusual than A, which is
backwards. B is just a busy server: both numbers are high, which matches the
positive correlation. A breaks the pattern.

## Simulate It

Check that $D^2(\mathbf{X})$ has mean $d$ and that the chi-square 95% cut-off
really holds 95% of the points, for $d = 2$ and $d = 5$, using random
covariance matrices.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 1_000_000

for d in [2, 5]:
    # A random valid covariance matrix: A A^T plus a little on the diagonal.
    A = rng.normal(size=(d, d))
    Sigma = A @ A.T + 0.5 * np.eye(d)
    mu = rng.normal(size=d)
    X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=n_samples)

    # Squared Mahalanobis distance of every sample.
    diff = X - mu
    D2 = np.sum(diff @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma) * diff, axis=1)

    cutoff = stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, d)          # 95% point of chi-square(d)
    print(f"d = {d}: mean D^2 = {D2.mean():.3f} (exact {d}), "
          f"P(D^2 <= {cutoff:.3f}) = {np.mean(D2 <= cutoff):.4f} (exact 0.95)")
```

Output:

```text
d = 2: mean D^2 = 1.999 (exact 2), P(D^2 <= 5.991) = 0.9499 (exact 0.95)
d = 5: mean D^2 = 4.997 (exact 5), P(D^2 <= 11.070) = 0.9501 (exact 0.95)
```

The result does not depend on which $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\Sigma$ we pick:
whitening always turns the problem into $d$ standard normals.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using $\Sigma$ instead of $\Sigma^{-1}$.** Directions with a big variance
  must count **less**. Only the inverse does that.
- **Using the 68-95-99.7 rule in many dimensions.** In 2-D only 39% of points
  are within distance 1 and 86% within distance 2. Use the chi-square table.
- **Comparing $D$ with a $\chi^2$ cut-off.** The chi-square law is for
  $D^2$, the **squared** distance. Square $D$ first, or take the square root
  of the cut-off.
- **Trusting the probabilities for non-Gaussian data.** $D$ is always a
  sensible distance, but the law $D^2 \sim \chi^2(d)$ needs a Gaussian. Heavy tails
  give many more large distances.
- **Estimating $\Sigma$ from data full of outliers.** The outliers inflate
  $\Sigma$ and hide themselves. Estimate $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\Sigma$ from
  clean data when you can.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** anomaly and out-of-distribution detection
  compare a new input's features with the training data using Mahalanobis
  distance. Gaussian classifiers assign a point to the class with the
  smallest distance (adjusted for $\det \Sigma$).
- **Computer science and security:** intrusion detection flags network
  traffic whose combination of features is rare, as in Example 2.
- **Robotics:** a Kalman filter rejects a sensor reading whose Mahalanobis
  distance from the prediction is too big ("gating").
- **Finance and manufacturing:** multivariate quality control charts and
  fraud checks flag unusual combinations, not just unusual single numbers.
- **Everyday life:** a child's height and weight are checked together on a
  growth chart; a combination far from the pattern matters more than either
  number alone.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** IQ scores have mean 100 and SD 15. What is the Mahalanobis
distance of a score of 130?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In one dimension it is the z-score:

$$D = \frac{\lvert 130 - 100 \rvert}{15} = 2$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\Sigma$ diagonal with
entries 4 and 9. Find $D^2$ for the point $(2, 3)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a diagonal $\Sigma$, add the squared z-scores:

$$D^2 = \frac{2^2}{4} + \frac{3^2}{9} = 1 + 1 = 2$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (1, 1)^\top$ and let $\Sigma$ have
rows $(4, 2)$ and $(2, 5)$. Is the point $(3, 0)$ outside the 95% contour?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\det \Sigma = 16$ and $\Sigma^{-1}$ is $\frac{1}{16}$ times the matrix with
rows $(5, -2)$ and $(-2, 4)$. The difference is $(2, -1)$:

$$D^2 = \frac{5 \cdot 2^2 - 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) + 4 \cdot (-1)^2}{16} = \frac{20 + 8 + 4}{16} = 2$$

The 95% cut-off for $d = 2$ is $-2\ln(0.05) \approx 5.99$. Since $2 \lt 5.99$,
the point is inside. It is not unusual.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In two dimensions, find $c$ so that the contour $D = c$
holds 95% of the probability. Compare with the 1-D value 1.96.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$1 - e^{-c^2/2} = 0.95 \implies c^2 = -2\ln(0.05) \approx 5.991 \implies c \approx 2.448$$

It is bigger than 1.96, because in two dimensions there are two directions in
which to be far away.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $E[D^2(\mathbf{X})] = d$ for any random vector
$\mathbf{X}$ with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and positive definite covariance
$\Sigma$, even if $\mathbf{X}$ is **not** Gaussian.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $\Sigma = LL^\top$ and $\mathbf{W} = L^{-1}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$.
By [11.7](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md),
$E[\mathbf{W}] = \mathbf{0}$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{W}) = L^{-1} \Sigma (L^{-1})^\top = L^{-1} L L^\top (L^\top)^{-1} = I$.
So each $W_i$ has mean 0 and variance 1, which gives $E[W_i^2] = 1$. By Step 1
of "Why It Is True", $D^2(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{W}$, so

$$E[D^2(\mathbf{X})] = E[W_1^2] + \dots + E[W_d^2] = 1 + \dots + 1 = d$$

Only the mean and covariance were used. The full chi-square law, however,
needs the Gaussian assumption.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate one million healthy servers from Example 2. What
fraction is flagged by the 99% rule? It should be about 0.01.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
mu = np.array([50.0, 60.0])
Sigma = np.array([[100.0, 80.0], [80.0, 100.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=1_000_000)
diff = X - mu
D2 = np.sum(diff @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma) * diff, axis=1)
cutoff = -2 * np.log(0.01)                 # 9.21, the 99% point of chi-square(2)
print(f"Flagged by Mahalanobis: {np.mean(D2 > cutoff):.4f}   (exact 0.0100)")
```

Output:

```text
Flagged by Mahalanobis: 0.0100   (exact 0.0100)
```

</details>

## Summary

- The Mahalanobis distance is the number of "SDs" from the mean, measured
  along the shape of the distribution. In 1-D it is the z-score.
- It equals the ordinary length of the whitened vector $L^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$.
- Gaussian contours are the sets of equal Mahalanobis distance.
- For a Gaussian in $d$ dimensions, $D^2 \sim \chi^2(d)$ with mean $d$. In
  2-D, $P(D \le c) = 1 - e^{-c^2/2}$.
- Use it to find outliers: compare $D^2$ with a chi-square cut-off.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{D^2(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \sim \chi^2(d) \text{ when } \mathbf{x} \text{ is drawn from } \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)}
$$
