# 12.9 Sums of Gaussians

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.9 Sums of Gaussians

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [12.8 Uncorrelated Implies Independent](12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md)

> **Big idea:** The sum of independent normal variables is normal; the means add and the variances add (the standard deviations do not).

---

## Why It Matters

Totals are everywhere: the total time of a trip with several legs, the total
weight of a package, the total error after several measurements, the total
return of many trades. If each part is normal and the parts are independent,
the total is **exactly** normal, and you can find any probability about it
with one table lookup. This is also the exact, small-sample version of the
Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)).

## Intuition

Your trip to the university has two legs. The bus takes about 30 minutes,
give or take 4 (SD 4). The train takes about 45 minutes, give or take 3 (SD
3). The two delays are independent. How long is the trip?

The average is easy: $30 + 45 = 75$ minutes. The spread is the interesting
part. Is it $4 + 3 = 7$ minutes? No. A slow bus is often balanced by a fast
train, so the errors partly cancel. Independent errors combine like the sides
of a right triangle: the SD of the total is $\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$ minutes.
Variances add; SDs add "with Pythagoras".

And the shape? Bell plus bell, independently, gives a bell again. That is not
true for most distributions (two uniforms give a triangle), and it is the
reason sums of normals are so easy.

## Picture

```text
   Bus: N(30, 4^2)        Train: N(45, 3^2)      Trip: N(75, 5^2)

        .-.                     .-.                     .--.
       /   \                   /   \                  .'    '.
      /     \        +        /     \        =      .'        '.
   __/       \__           __/       \__        __.'            '.__
   ------+------           ------+------        ---------+----------
        30                      45                      75
     SD 4                    SD 3               SD sqrt(16 + 9) = 5
```

Adding two independent normals gives another normal. Its center is the sum
of the centers; its width grows, but less than the sum of the widths.

## Definition

**Two variables first.** If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and
$Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are **independent**, then

$$
X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)
$$

**In words:** add the means, add the variances, and the result is still
normal.

The same holds for a difference, with the variances still **added**:
$X - Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 - \mu_2, \ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$.

**Many variables.** If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with
$X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$, and $a_1, \dots, a_n$ are numbers, then

$$
a_1 X_1 + \dots + a_n X_n \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mu_i, \ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^2 \sigma_i^2 \right)
$$

Two special cases:

- **Sum of n i.i.d. normals:** $X_1 + \dots + X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$.
- **Sample mean:** $\bar{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$, so its SD is
  $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

**Correlated but jointly Gaussian.** If $(X, Y)$ is bivariate normal with
correlation $\rho$, then the sum is still normal, with an extra covariance
term:

$$
X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)
$$

## Why It Is True

**Proof with the affine rule.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent normals, the
vector $(X, Y)$ is jointly Gaussian with a diagonal covariance matrix (its
PDF is the product of the two normal PDFs, which is the bivariate normal PDF
with $\Sigma_{12} = 0$, see [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md),
Practice 5). Now $X + Y = A(X, Y)^\top$ with $A$ the single row $(1, 1)$.
By [12.5 Affine Transformations](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X + Y] &= 1 \cdot \mu_1 + 1 \cdot \mu_2 = \mu_1 + \mu_2 && \text{(} A\boldsymbol{\mu} \text{)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) &= 1^2 \sigma_1^2 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 + 1^2 \sigma_2^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 && \text{(} A\Sigma A^\top \text{ with } \Sigma_{12} = 0 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

and $X + Y$ is normal. With correlation, the middle term is
$2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$ instead of 0. The weighted sum of $n$ variables is the
same calculation with $A = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and a diagonal $\Sigma$.

**A second proof with MGFs.** The MGF of $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is
$e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}$. For independent variables the MGF of the sum is
the product of the MGFs ([15.3 MGF of Sums](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)):

$$
M_{X+Y}(t) = e^{\mu_1 t + \sigma_1^2 t^2/2} \cdot e^{\mu_2 t + \sigma_2^2 t^2/2} = e^{(\mu_1 + \mu_2)t + (\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)t^2/2}
$$

This is the MGF of $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$, and
an MGF fixes the distribution. (A third proof, with the convolution
integral, is in [13.5 Sums of Known Distributions](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md).)

**Why "jointly" matters.** Independence makes $(X, Y)$ jointly Gaussian for
free. Without independence or joint normality the rule can fail: with
$Y = SX$ from [12.6](12.6-Marginals.md), both $X$ and $Y$ are
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, but $X + Y$ is 0 half of the time, which no normal
variable does.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Two-Leg Trip

**Problem.** The bus time is $\mathcal{N}(30, 16)$ and the train time is
$\mathcal{N}(45, 9)$ (in minutes; second parameter is the variance),
independent. You must arrive within 85 minutes. What is the chance you are
late?

**Solution.**

1. Let $T$ be the total: bus time plus train time. Mean: $30 + 45 = 75$.
2. Variance: $16 + 9 = 25$, so the SD is $\sqrt{25} = 5$.
3. So $T \sim \mathcal{N}(75, 25)$, and

$$
P(T \gt 85) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{85 - 75}{5} \right) = P(Z \gt 2) \approx 0.0228
$$

**Answer.** About $0.023$, or roughly 1 trip in 44.

**Check.** If you wrongly added the SDs ($4 + 3 = 7$), you would get
$P(Z \gt 10/7) = P(Z \gt 1.43) \approx 0.077$, more than three times too big.

### Example 2: Package Weight Control

**Problem.** A factory packs 4 items in a box. Each item weighs
$\mathcal{N}(250, 25)$ grams, the empty box weighs $\mathcal{N}(100, 9)$
grams, all independent. Packages under 1080 grams are rejected. What fraction
is rejected?

**Solution.**

1. Mean of the total: $4 \cdot 250 + 100 = 1100$ grams.
2. Variance: $4 \cdot 25 + 9 = 109$, so the SD is $\sqrt{109} \approx 10.44$ grams.
3. Standardize:

$$
P(\text{total} \lt 1080) = \Phi\left( \frac{1080 - 1100}{10.44} \right) = \Phi(-1.916) \approx 0.0277
$$

**Answer.** About $0.028$, or 2.8% of packages.

**Check.** Note the variance of 4 items is $4 \cdot 25 = 100$, not
$4^2 \cdot 25 = 400$. The 4 items are **different** independent items, not one
item counted 4 times. The simulation below confirms $0.0277$, and shows that
adding the SDs (giving SD 23) would wrongly predict about $0.19$.

## Simulate It

Simulate one million packages from Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_boxes = 1_000_000

# Four items, each N(250, 25) grams, plus a box N(100, 9) grams, all independent.
items = rng.normal(250, 5, size=(n_boxes, 4))     # SD = sqrt(25) = 5
box = rng.normal(100, 3, size=n_boxes)            # SD = sqrt(9) = 3
total = items.sum(axis=1) + box

print(f"Mean of total: {total.mean():.2f}   (exact 1100)")
print(f"Var of total:  {total.var():.2f}   (exact 109)")
exact = stats.norm.cdf(1080, loc=1100, scale=np.sqrt(109))
wrong = stats.norm.cdf(1080, loc=1100, scale=4 * 5 + 3)   # adding SDs: wrong
print(f"P(total < 1080): simulation {np.mean(total < 1080):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"Adding SDs would say:       {wrong:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Mean of total: 1100.02   (exact 1100)
Var of total:  109.11   (exact 109)
P(total < 1080): simulation 0.0275, exact 0.0277
Adding SDs would say:       0.1923
```

Note that `rng.normal` takes the **SD** as its scale, while
$\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ in this book lists the **variance**.

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding standard deviations.** For independent variables, variances add.
  The SD of the sum is $\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$, which is smaller than
  $\sigma_1 + \sigma_2$.
- **Subtracting variances for a difference.** $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2$
  for independent $X, Y$. Uncertainty never cancels out by subtracting.
- **Confusing $2X$ with $X_1 + X_2$.** $2X$ has variance $4\sigma^2$, but the
  sum of two **independent** copies has variance $2\sigma^2$. One item doubled
  is riskier than two separate items.
- **Forgetting the covariance.** If the parts are correlated, add
  $2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$. Correlated delays (both legs slowed by the same
  rain) make the total much more spread out.
- **Assuming normal plus normal is always normal.** It needs independence or
  joint normality. $X + SX$ is a counterexample.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** diffusion models add Gaussian noise step by
  step; because Gaussians add, many small noise steps equal one big step with
  the summed variance, which makes training fast
  ([21.1.4 Generative Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
- **Computer science:** the total latency of a request through several
  independent services, each roughly normal, is roughly normal with summed
  variances.
- **Engineering:** tolerance stacking: the length of an assembly of parts has
  the sum of the parts' variances, so a stack of 4 parts has twice (not four
  times) the SD of one part.
- **Physics and finance:** Brownian motion is built from independent normal
  steps whose variances add over time
  ([18.8 Brownian Motion](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.8-Brownian-Motion.md)).
- **Everyday life:** averaging several measurements: the average of $n$ has
  SD $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so 4 measurements halve the error.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(1, 4)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(2, 5)$ are
independent. Find the distributions of $X + Y$ and $X - Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(1 + 2, 4 + 5) = \mathcal{N}(3, 9), \qquad X - Y \sim \mathcal{N}(1 - 2, 4 + 5) = \mathcal{N}(-1, 9)$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A sensor reading is $\mathcal{N}(\mu, 100)$. You average 25
independent readings. What is the SD of the average, and what is the chance
that the average is more than 4 away from $\mu$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\bar{X}_{25} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 100/25) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, 4)$, so its SD is 2.

$$P(\lvert \bar{X}_{25} - \mu \rvert \gt 4) = P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt 2) \approx 0.0455$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Runner A's time is $\mathcal{N}(50, 9)$ minutes and runner
B's is $\mathcal{N}(52, 16)$, independent. What is the chance that A finishes
first?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $D = A - B \sim \mathcal{N}(50 - 52, 9 + 16) = \mathcal{N}(-2, 25)$, with SD 5.
A finishes first when $D \lt 0$:

$$P(D \lt 0) = \Phi\left( \frac{0 - (-2)}{5} \right) = \Phi(0.4) \approx 0.6554$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X, X_1, X_2 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
Compare $P(2X \gt 2)$ with $P(X_1 + X_2 \gt 2)$. Explain the difference.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$2X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$, so $P(2X \gt 2) = P(Z \gt 1) \approx 0.1587$.
$X_1 + X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$, so

$$P(X_1 + X_2 \gt 2) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} \right) = P(Z \gt 1.414) \approx 0.0786$$

Doubling one variable doubles its SD. Adding two independent copies only
multiplies the SD by $\sqrt{2}$, because their errors partly cancel.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove with MGFs that if $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with
$X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$, then
$\sum a_i X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\sum a_i \mu_i, \sum a_i^2 \sigma_i^2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The MGF of $a_i X_i$ is $M_{X_i}(a_i t) = e^{\mu_i a_i t + \sigma_i^2 a_i^2 t^2/2}$.
The terms $a_i X_i$ are independent, so the MGF of their sum is the product:

$$M(t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} e^{a_i \mu_i t + a_i^2 \sigma_i^2 t^2/2} = \exp\left( \Big( \sum_{i} a_i \mu_i \Big) t + \Big( \sum_{i} a_i^2 \sigma_i^2 \Big) \frac{t^2}{2} \right)$$

This is the MGF of $\mathcal{N}(\sum a_i \mu_i, \sum a_i^2 \sigma_i^2)$. By the
uniqueness of MGFs ([15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)),
that is the distribution of the sum.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the answer of Practice 3 by simulating one million
races.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_races = 1_000_000
A = rng.normal(50, 3, size=n_races)      # runner A: N(50, 9) minutes
B = rng.normal(52, 4, size=n_races)      # runner B: N(52, 16) minutes
print(f"P(A finishes first): {np.mean(A < B):.4f}   (exact 0.6554)")
```

Output:

```text
P(A finishes first): 0.6559   (exact 0.6554)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Independent normals add to a normal: means add, variances add.
- SDs combine like the sides of a right triangle: $\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$.
- Differences also **add** variances.
- The sum of $n$ i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is $\mathcal{N}(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$;
  the average is $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$, exactly.
- With correlation (jointly Gaussian), add $2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$ to the variance.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2), \ Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2) \text{ independent} \implies X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)}
$$
