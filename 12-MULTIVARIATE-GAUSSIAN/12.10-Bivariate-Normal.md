# 12.10 Bivariate Normal

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.10 Bivariate Normal

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [12.9 Sums of Gaussians](12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md)

> **Big idea:** Two jointly normal variables are fully described by five numbers (two means, two SDs and one correlation $\rho$), and every question about them, from the density to predictions and quadrant probabilities, has a closed-form answer in terms of $\rho$.

---

## Why It Matters

The bivariate normal is the multivariate normal you can draw, compute by
hand, and feel. It is the standard model for two related measurements:
height of parent and child, midterm and final, two stock returns, two
sensors. It is where regression, regression to the mean and correlation all
become concrete. Every result of this module shows up here in its simplest
form, written with $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y$ and $\rho$ instead of matrices.

## Intuition

Fathers and sons. Heights of fathers have mean 175 cm and SD 7 cm, and so do
heights of sons. The correlation is $0.5$. A father is 189 cm, 2 SDs above
average. How tall is his son?

The son is probably tall, but not as tall: in SD units we expect
$\rho \times 2 = 1$ SD above average, which is 182 cm. And the son of a very
short father is expected to be short, but closer to average. This pull toward
the middle, discovered by Francis Galton with exactly this kind of data, is
**regression to the mean**. It is not a force of nature; it is a property of
the bivariate normal (and many other distributions) whenever $\lvert \rho \rvert \lt 1$.

## Definition

A pair $(X, Y)$ has the **bivariate normal** distribution with means
$\mu_X, \mu_Y$, standard deviations $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y \gt 0$ and correlation
$\rho$ with $-1 \lt \rho \lt 1$, if it is $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$
with

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_X \cr \mu_Y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_X^2 & \rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y \cr \rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y & \sigma_Y^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Write $z_x = \frac{x - \mu_X}{\sigma_X}$ and $z_y = \frac{y - \mu_Y}{\sigma_Y}$
for the z-scores. The joint PDF is

$$
f(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_X\sigma_Y\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{z_x^2 - 2\rho z_x z_y + z_y^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \right)
$$

**In words:** a bell-shaped hill whose height depends on the two z-scores;
the cross term $-2\rho z_x z_y$ rewards points where the z-scores have the same
sign (if $\rho \gt 0$).

All the facts of this module, in this notation:

| Fact | Formula | Topic |
|---|---|---|
| Marginals | $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)$, $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)$ | [12.6](12.6-Marginals.md) |
| Conditional mean | $E[Y \mid X = x] = \mu_Y + \rho \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X)$ | [12.7](12.7-Conditionals.md) |
| Conditional variance | $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x) = \sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$ | [12.7](12.7-Conditionals.md) |
| Construction | $X = \mu_X + \sigma_X Z_1$, $Y = \mu_Y + \sigma_Y(\rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} Z_2)$ | [12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md) |
| Independence | $X \perp Y$ exactly when $\rho = 0$ | [12.8](12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md) |
| Linear combinations | $aX + bY$ is normal, variance $a^2\sigma_X^2 + 2ab\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y + b^2\sigma_Y^2$ | [12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md) |
| Quadrant probability | $P(X \gt \mu_X, Y \gt \mu_Y) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{\operatorname{arcsin}(\rho)}{2\pi}$ | below |

In z-units the conditional mean is simply $E[Z_Y \mid Z_X = z] = \rho z$:
**predict $\rho$ times as many SDs**.

## Why It Is True

**The PDF from the matrix formula.** Use the $2 \times 2$ rules of
[12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det \Sigma &= \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2 - \rho^2\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2 = \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2) && \text{(} ad - bc \text{)} \\
\Sigma^{-1} &= \frac{1}{\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_Y^2 & -\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y \cr -\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y & \sigma_X^2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(swap, change signs, divide)} \\
(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) &= \frac{\sigma_Y^2 (x - \mu_X)^2 - 2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y (x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y) + \sigma_X^2 (y - \mu_Y)^2}{\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)} && \text{(quadratic form)} \\
&= \frac{z_x^2 - 2\rho z_x z_y + z_y^2}{1 - \rho^2} && \text{(divide each term by } \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\sqrt{\det \Sigma} = \sigma_X\sigma_Y\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$, this gives the PDF above.

**The conditional distribution, by dividing densities.** Complete the square
in $z_y$:

$$
z_x^2 - 2\rho z_x z_y + z_y^2 = (z_y - \rho z_x)^2 + (1 - \rho^2) z_x^2
$$

So the joint PDF splits into two factors:

$$
f(x, y) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_X} e^{-z_x^2/2}}_{f_X(x)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_Y\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(z_y - \rho z_x)^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \right)}_{f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)}
$$

The first factor is the $\mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)$ density. The second
factor is $f(x, y) / f_X(x)$, the conditional density
([10.4](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md)). Since
$\sigma_Y(z_y - \rho z_x) = y - (\mu_Y + \rho\sigma_Y z_x)$, it is a normal
density in $y$ with mean $\mu_Y + \rho\sigma_Y z_x = \mu_Y + \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X)$
and variance $\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$.

**The quadrant probability.** Standardize, so $X = Z_1$ and
$Y = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} Z_2$. Write $\rho = \cos\alpha$ with
$\alpha = \operatorname{arccos}(\rho)$ between 0 and $\pi$. Then
$Y = \cos\alpha \cdot Z_1 + \sin\alpha \cdot Z_2$. The point $(Z_1, Z_2)$ comes
from a round hill, so its direction angle $\Theta$ is uniform on a full circle
of $2\pi$.

1. $X \gt 0$ means $\Theta$ is within $\frac{\pi}{2}$ of angle 0.
2. $Y \gt 0$ means $(Z_1, Z_2)$ points within $\frac{\pi}{2}$ of the direction
   $(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$, so $\Theta$ is within $\frac{\pi}{2}$ of angle $\alpha$.
3. Two half-circles whose centers are $\alpha$ apart overlap in an arc of
   length $\pi - \alpha$. So:

$$
P(X \gt 0, Y \gt 0) = \frac{\pi - \alpha}{2\pi} = \frac{\pi - (\frac{\pi}{2} - \operatorname{arcsin}\rho)}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{\operatorname{arcsin}(\rho)}{2\pi}
$$

using $\operatorname{arccos}\rho = \frac{\pi}{2} - \operatorname{arcsin}\rho$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Fathers and Sons

**Problem.** Father's height $X$ and son's height $Y$ are bivariate normal,
both with mean 175 cm and SD 7 cm, with $\rho = 0.5$. A father is 189 cm.
Predict his son's height, and find the chance that the son is taller than his
father.

**Solution.**

1. Z-score of the father: $z_x = \frac{189 - 175}{7} = 2$.
2. Conditional mean: $175 + 0.5 \cdot \frac{7}{7} \cdot (189 - 175) = 175 + 7 = 182$ cm.
3. Conditional SD: $7\sqrt{1 - 0.5^2} = 7\sqrt{0.75} \approx 6.062$ cm.
4. Probability:

$$
P(Y \gt 189 \mid X = 189) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{189 - 182}{6.062} \right) = P(Z \gt 1.155) \approx 0.124
$$

**Answer.** The son is $\mathcal{N}(182, 36.75)$: expected 182 cm, and only
about a 12% chance of being taller than his 189 cm father.

**Check.** A random son (no information) is taller than 189 cm with chance
$P(Z \gt 2) \approx 0.023$. Knowing the father raises it to $0.124$, but the
son is still more likely to be shorter than the father: regression to the
mean. The simulation agrees.

### Example 2: Both Above Average

**Problem.** Two stocks have daily returns that are bivariate normal with
mean 0 and correlation $\rho$. What is the chance that both go up on the same
day, for $\rho = 0.5$, $\rho = 0$ and $\rho = -0.5$?

**Solution.**

1. "Up" means above the mean 0, so use the quadrant formula.
2. $\rho = 0.5$: $\operatorname{arcsin}(0.5) = \frac{\pi}{6}$, so $P = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{\pi/6}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{3}$.
3. $\rho = 0$: $P = \frac{1}{4} + 0 = \frac{1}{4}$, the independent answer $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2}$.
4. $\rho = -0.5$: $\operatorname{arcsin}(-0.5) = -\frac{\pi}{6}$, so $P = \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Answer.** $\frac{1}{3}$, $\frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{6}$.

**Check.** As $\rho \to 1$ the formula gives $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{\pi/2}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2}$
(the stocks always move together), and as $\rho \to -1$ it gives 0. The value
$\frac{1}{3}$ also matches the simulation in [12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md),
Practice 6.

## Simulate It

Build fathers and sons with the construction, then check Example 1 (with a
thin window around 189 cm) and Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_families = 10_000_000

# Father X and son Y: both mean 175 cm, SD 7 cm, correlation 0.5.
mu, sigma, rho = 175.0, 7.0, 0.5
Z1, Z2 = rng.standard_normal(n_families), rng.standard_normal(n_families)
X = mu + sigma * Z1
Y = mu + sigma * (rho * Z1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * Z2)   # the construction

# Sons of fathers who are about 189 cm tall.
sons = Y[np.abs(X - 189) < 0.5]
print(f"E[Y | X = 189]:  {sons.mean():.2f}   (exact 182)")
print(f"SD[Y | X = 189]: {sons.std():.3f}   (exact {7 * np.sqrt(0.75):.3f})")
print(f"P(son taller than 189): {np.mean(sons > 189):.4f}   (exact 0.1241)")

# Both above average: 1/4 + arcsin(rho) / (2 pi).
both = np.mean((X > mu) & (Y > mu))
print(f"P(both above mean): {both:.4f}   (exact {0.25 + np.arcsin(rho) / (2 * np.pi):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
E[Y | X = 189]:  182.04   (exact 182)
SD[Y | X = 189]: 6.062   (exact 6.062)
P(son taller than 189): 0.1247   (exact 0.1241)
P(both above mean): 0.3333   (exact 0.3333)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Predicting with slope $\sigma_Y/\sigma_X$ instead of $\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X$.**
  That ignores regression to the mean and over-predicts extremes. The long
  axis of the ellipse is **not** the prediction line.
- **Thinking the two regression lines are the same.** Predicting $Y$ from $X$
  uses slope $\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X$; predicting $X$ from $Y$ uses
  $\rho\sigma_X/\sigma_Y$. They are different lines (Practice 4).
- **Forgetting $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$ in the constant.** As $\rho \to \pm 1$ the
  peak gets infinitely high, because all the mass squeezes onto a line.
- **Reading regression to the mean as a cause.** Sons of tall fathers are not
  "pulled down" by anything. The father's height is partly luck that the son
  does not inherit ([22.6](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md)).
- **Using these formulas for any two normal variables.** They need $(X, Y)$ to
  be **jointly** normal.

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics and data science:** simple linear regression is the
  conditional mean of a bivariate normal; $\rho^2$ is the fraction of variance
  explained ("R squared").
- **Computer science:** locality-sensitive hashing with random hyperplanes
  (SimHash) uses the quadrant formula: two vectors at angle $\theta$ get the
  same hash bit with probability $1 - \theta/\pi$ (Practice 5).
- **AI and machine learning:** the angle formula also explains why the signs
  of random projections preserve similarity, a building block of fast
  nearest-neighbor search.
- **Finance:** the chance that two assets fall together, as in Example 2, is
  a first measure of joint risk.
- **Everyday life:** after an unusually good exam or match, expect the next
  one to be closer to your average; that is the bivariate normal at work
  ([22.6 Regression to the Mean](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ has SD 2, $Y$ has SD 3, and $\rho = -0.4$. Write the
covariance matrix.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The covariance is $\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y = -0.4 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = -2.4$. So
$\Sigma$ has rows $(4, -2.4)$ and $(-2.4, 9)$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For the pair of Practice 1, with $\mu_X = 10$ and $\mu_Y = 20$,
find $E[Y \mid X = 13]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 13)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[Y \mid X = 13] = 20 + (-0.4) \cdot \frac{3}{2} \cdot (13 - 10) = 20 - 1.8 = 18.2$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 13) = 9 \cdot (1 - 0.16) = 7.56$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the pair of Practice 1, find the density at the mean,
$f(\mu_X, \mu_Y)$. What happens to it as $\rho \to 1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

At the mean both z-scores are 0, so the exponential is 1:

$$f(\mu_X, \mu_Y) = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \sqrt{1 - 0.16}} = \frac{1}{12\pi \cdot 0.9165} \approx 0.0289$$

As $\rho \to 1$, $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \to 0$ and the peak goes to infinity: the
whole unit of probability squeezes onto the line $z_y = z_x$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that the slope of the line predicting $Y$ from $X$ times
the slope of the line predicting $X$ from $Y$ equals $\rho^2$. When are the
two lines the same?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X} \cdot \rho\frac{\sigma_X}{\sigma_Y} = \rho^2$$

Drawn in the same $(x, y)$ plane, the second line has slope
$\frac{\sigma_Y}{\rho\sigma_X}$ (solve $x - \mu_X = \rho\frac{\sigma_X}{\sigma_Y}(y - \mu_Y)$
for $y$). The two slopes are equal only when $\rho^2 = 1$. For
$\lvert \rho \rvert \lt 1$ the lines differ, and both are flatter (in z-units)
than the 45 degree line in their own direction.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $(X, Y)$ be standard bivariate normal with correlation
$\rho$. Prove that $P(X \text{ and } Y \text{ have the same sign}) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\operatorname{arcsin}(\rho)}{\pi}$.
Then explain the SimHash fact: for a random Gaussian vector $\mathbf{g}$ and
two unit vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ at angle $\theta$, the signs of
$\mathbf{g}^\top \mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{g}^\top \mathbf{v}$ agree with
probability $1 - \theta/\pi$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By symmetry ($(-X, -Y)$ has the same distribution as $(X, Y)$),
$P(X \lt 0, Y \lt 0) = P(X \gt 0, Y \gt 0)$. So

$$P(\text{same sign}) = 2\left( \frac{1}{4} + \frac{\operatorname{arcsin}\rho}{2\pi} \right) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\operatorname{arcsin}\rho}{\pi}$$

For SimHash: if $\mathbf{g} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$, the pair
$(\mathbf{g}^\top \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{g}^\top \mathbf{v})$ is jointly normal
([12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)) with variances
$\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{u} = 1$ and $\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v} = 1$, and
covariance $\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \cos\theta$. So $\rho = \cos\theta$ and
$\operatorname{arcsin}(\cos\theta) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$ for $\theta$ between 0 and $\pi$:

$$P(\text{same sign}) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\pi/2 - \theta}{\pi} = 1 - \frac{\theta}{\pi}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check the formula of Practice 5 for
$\rho = -0.5, 0, 0.5, 0.9$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
for rho in [-0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 0.9]:
    Z1, Z2 = rng.standard_normal(n), rng.standard_normal(n)
    X, Y = Z1, rho * Z1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * Z2
    same_sign = np.mean(np.sign(X) == np.sign(Y))
    exact = 0.5 + np.arcsin(rho) / np.pi
    print(f"rho = {rho:4.1f}: P(same sign) = {same_sign:.4f}   exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
rho = -0.5: P(same sign) = 0.3334   exact 0.3333
rho =  0.0: P(same sign) = 0.5000   exact 0.5000
rho =  0.5: P(same sign) = 0.6672   exact 0.6667
rho =  0.9: P(same sign) = 0.8563   exact 0.8564
```

</details>

## Summary

- Five numbers, $\mu_X, \mu_Y, \sigma_X, \sigma_Y, \rho$, describe a bivariate
  normal completely.
- The PDF depends on the z-scores through $z_x^2 - 2\rho z_x z_y + z_y^2$,
  divided by $1 - \rho^2$.
- Predicting $Y$ from $X$: move $\rho$ SDs for every SD of $X$; the spread
  left over is $\sigma_Y\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$. This is regression to the mean.
- Completing the square splits the joint density into marginal times
  conditional.
- Both above the mean: $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{\operatorname{arcsin}\rho}{2\pi}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{Y \mid X = x \ \sim \ \mathcal{N}\left( \mu_Y + \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X), \ \sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2) \right)}
$$
