# 12.7 Conditionals

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.7 Conditionals

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [12.6 Marginals](12.6-Marginals.md)

> **Big idea:** If you observe some entries of a Gaussian vector, the rest is still Gaussian: its mean moves along a straight line with what you saw, and its variance shrinks by an amount that does not depend on what you saw.

---

## Why It Matters

"Given what I measured, what do I now believe about what I did not
measure?" is the central question of prediction. For most distributions the
conditional distribution is hard to compute ([10.4 Conditional Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md)).
For Gaussians there is one exact formula. It is the engine inside Kalman
filters, Gaussian process regression, filling in missing data, and linear
regression.

## Intuition

A teacher has years of data: midterm marks have mean 65 and SD 8, final marks
have mean 70 and SD 10, and the correlation is $0.6$. A student just scored 81
on the midterm, 2 SDs above average. What will the final mark be?

Two things should happen. First, the guess should go **up**, but not all the
way: the student is 2 SDs above on the midterm, and a correlation of $0.6$
says to expect about $0.6 \times 2 = 1.2$ SDs above on the final, that is
$70 + 1.2 \times 10 = 82$. Second, we should be **more sure** than before: the
midterm explains part of the variation, so the spread of the final shrinks
from SD 10 to SD $10\sqrt{1 - 0.6^2} = 8$. And all of this would be the same
spread for a student who scored 50 or 90 on the midterm: only the center
moves.

Geometrically: slice the Gaussian hill along the line "midterm = 81". The
profile of the slice is again a bell curve. That bell curve, rescaled to
area 1, is the conditional distribution.

## Picture

```text
   x2 (midterm)
    |                          ...........
 81 +- - - - - - - - - - -[=====+=====]- - - -    slice x2 = 81
    |                 .....      :      ..
    |              ....          :    ...         conditional of X1:
    |           ....       μ     :  ....          center 82 (moved
    |         ...                ...              toward the slice)
    |        ..             .....:                SD 8 (narrower
    |       ..         ......    :                than the SD of 10)
    |        ...........         :
    +----------------------------+------------- x1 (final)
                          70     82
```

Fixing the midterm at 81 cuts a slice through the joint hill. The slice is a
narrower bell curve for the final, centered at 82 instead of 70.

## Definition

**Two dimensions first.** Let $(X_1, X_2)$ be bivariate normal with means
$\mu_1, \mu_2$, variances $\Sigma_{11}, \Sigma_{22}$ and covariance
$\Sigma_{12}$. Given $X_2 = x_2$:

$$
X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2 \ \sim \ \mathcal{N}\left( \mu_1 + \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{22}} (x_2 - \mu_2), \ \ \Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}} \right)
$$

**In words:** start from the mean of $X_1$; move it by the surprise
$x_2 - \mu_2$ times the slope $\Sigma_{12}/\Sigma_{22}$; the variance drops by
$\Sigma_{12}^2/\Sigma_{22}$, whatever $x_2$ is.

With SDs $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and correlation $\rho$ (so $\Sigma_{12} = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$)
this is $\mathcal{N}\left( \mu_1 + \rho \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(x_2 - \mu_2), \ \sigma_1^2(1 - \rho^2) \right)$.
Topic [12.10](12.10-Bivariate-Normal.md) studies this form.

**The general statement.** Split a Gaussian vector into blocks as in
[12.6 Marginals](12.6-Marginals.md):

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{X}_1 \cr \mathbf{X}_2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \cr \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \cr \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix} \right)
$$

Then, given that we observe $\mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2$:

$$
\mathbf{X}_1 \mid \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2 \ \sim \ \mathcal{N}\left( \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2), \ \ \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \right)
$$

where:

- $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$ is the
  **conditional mean**: a straight-line (affine) function of $\mathbf{x}_2$;
- $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$ is the **conditional
  covariance** (also called the **Schur complement**); it does not depend on
  $\mathbf{x}_2$ at all.

**In words:** the 2-D formula with matrices: divide by $\Sigma_{22}$ becomes
multiply by $\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$.

Three facts to remember:

1. The conditional distribution is **Gaussian**.
2. The conditional mean is **linear** in what you observed.
3. The conditional variance is **never bigger** than the unconditional one,
   and it is the **same for every observed value**.

## Why It Is True

**The 2-D case, using the Cholesky trick.** Let $Z_a, Z_b$ be independent
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and build a pair, putting $X_2$ first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_2 &= \mu_2 + \sqrt{\Sigma_{22}} \thinspace Z_b \\
X_1 &= \mu_1 + \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}}} Z_b + \sqrt{\Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}}} \thinspace Z_a
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the recipe of [12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md).
Check the covariance: $\operatorname{Var}(X_1) = \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}} + \Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}} = \Sigma_{11}$
and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}}} \sqrt{\Sigma_{22}} = \Sigma_{12}$.
So this pair has exactly our bivariate normal distribution.

Now observe $X_2 = x_2$. That fixes $Z_b = (x_2 - \mu_2)/\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}}$.
It tells us **nothing** about $Z_a$, because $Z_a$ is independent of $Z_b$.
Substitute:

$$
X_1 = \underbrace{\mu_1 + \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{22}}(x_2 - \mu_2)}_{\text{now a fixed number}} + \underbrace{\sqrt{\Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}}} \thinspace Z_a}_{\text{still random, } \mathcal{N}(0, \ \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}^2/\Sigma_{22})}
$$

A fixed number plus a normal variable is normal. That is the 2-D formula.

**The general case, the same way in blocks.** Let $L_{22}$ be the Cholesky
factor of $\Sigma_{22}$, and let $D$ be a matrix with
$DD^\top = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$ (this matrix is
positive definite when $\Sigma$ is). With independent standard normal vectors
$\mathbf{Z}_a, \mathbf{Z}_b$, set $C = \Sigma_{12}(L_{22}^\top)^{-1}$ and

$$
\mathbf{X}_2 = \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 + L_{22}\mathbf{Z}_b, \qquad \mathbf{X}_1 = \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + C\mathbf{Z}_b + D\mathbf{Z}_a
$$

Using $(L_{22}^\top)^{-1} L_{22}^{-1} = (L_{22}L_{22}^\top)^{-1} = \Sigma_{22}^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_1, \mathbf{X}_2) &= C L_{22}^\top = \Sigma_{12} && \text{(only } \mathbf{Z}_b \text{ is shared)} \\
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_1) &= CC^\top + DD^\top = \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} + \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} = \Sigma_{11} && \text{(} \mathbf{Z}_a, \mathbf{Z}_b \text{ independent)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So this vector has our distribution (it is Gaussian by
[12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)). Observing $\mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2$
fixes $\mathbf{Z}_b = L_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$ and
leaves $\mathbf{Z}_a$ untouched. Then
$C\mathbf{Z}_b = \Sigma_{12}(L_{22}^\top)^{-1}L_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) = \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$,
and $D\mathbf{Z}_a \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, DD^\top)$. That is the general
formula.

**Why the variance shrinks.** In 2-D the drop is
$\Sigma_{12}^2/\Sigma_{22} \ge 0$. In general,
$\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} = CC^\top$, and
$\mathbf{a}^\top CC^\top \mathbf{a} = \lVert C^\top \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 \ge 0$
for every $\mathbf{a}$. Information never makes a Gaussian more uncertain.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Predicting the Final Exam

**Problem.** Final mark $X_1$ and midterm mark $X_2$ are bivariate normal
with means 70 and 65, SDs 10 and 8, and correlation $0.6$. A student scored
81 on the midterm. Find the distribution of the final mark, and the chance
that it is at least 90.

**Solution.**

1. Covariance: $\Sigma_{12} = 0.6 \cdot 10 \cdot 8 = 48$. Also $\Sigma_{11} = 100$
   and $\Sigma_{22} = 64$.
2. Slope: $\frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{22}} = \frac{48}{64} = 0.75$.
3. Conditional mean: $70 + 0.75 \cdot (81 - 65) = 70 + 0.75 \cdot 16 = 70 + 12 = 82$.
4. Conditional variance: $100 - \frac{48^2}{64} = 100 - \frac{2304}{64} = 100 - 36 = 64$, so the SD is 8.
5. Probability:

$$
P(X_1 \ge 90 \mid X_2 = 81) = P\left( Z \ge \frac{90 - 82}{8} \right) = P(Z \ge 1) \approx 0.1587
$$

**Answer.** Final $\mid$ midterm $= 81$ is $\mathcal{N}(82, 64)$, and the
chance of at least 90 is about $0.159$.

**Check.** Without the midterm, $P(X_1 \ge 90) = P(Z \ge 2) \approx 0.023$.
The good midterm makes a high final about 7 times as likely. The variance
dropped from 100 to $64 = 100(1 - 0.6^2)$, as the $\rho$ form says.

### Example 2: Filling In a Broken Weather Station

**Problem.** Temperatures (Celsius) at three nearby stations A, B, C are
Gaussian with mean $(20, 21, 22)$ and the covariance matrix below. Station A
is broken. B reads 24 and C reads 22. Predict A, with its uncertainty.

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \cr 2 & 5 & 3 \cr 2 & 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. Blocks: $\mathbf{X}_1 = A$ (one entry) and $\mathbf{X}_2 = (B, C)$. So
   $\Sigma_{11} = 4$, $\Sigma_{12} = (2, 2)$ as a row, and $\Sigma_{22}$ has rows
   $(5, 3)$ and $(3, 6)$.
2. Invert $\Sigma_{22}$: $\det = 5 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 3 = 21$, so

$$
\Sigma_{22}^{-1} = \frac{1}{21} \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -3 \cr -3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

3. The weights: $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1} = \frac{1}{21}(2 \cdot 6 + 2 \cdot (-3), \ 2 \cdot (-3) + 2 \cdot 5) = \frac{1}{21}(6, 4)$.
4. The surprise: $\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = (24 - 21, 22 - 22) = (3, 0)$.
5. Conditional mean: $20 + \frac{1}{21}(6 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 0) = 20 + \frac{18}{21} \approx 20.857$.
6. Conditional variance: $4 - \frac{1}{21}(6 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 2) = 4 - \frac{20}{21} = \frac{64}{21} \approx 3.048$, so the SD is about $1.746$.

**Answer.** Station A is about $\mathcal{N}(20.86, 3.05)$: best guess 20.9
degrees, give or take 1.7.

**Check.** Using station B alone (the 2-D formula with $\Sigma_{12} = 2$,
$\Sigma_{22} = 5$) gives mean $20 + \frac{2}{5} \cdot 3 = 21.2$ and variance
$4 - \frac{4}{5} = 3.2$. Adding C lowers the variance a little more, as it
must. It also lowers the mean, although C read exactly its average: B and C
are correlated, so part of B's high reading is "explained" by a shared
effect that C does not show. Practice 6 checks this by simulation.

## Simulate It

Check Example 1 by brute force: simulate many students, keep only those whose
midterm is within 0.5 of 81, and look at their finals.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_students = 10_000_000

# (final, midterm): means 70 and 65, SDs 10 and 8, correlation 0.6.
mu = np.array([70.0, 65.0])
Sigma = np.array([[100.0, 48.0],
                  [48.0, 64.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=n_students)

# Condition on midterm = 81 by keeping students with midterm within 0.5 of 81.
near = np.abs(X[:, 1] - 81) < 0.5
final = X[near, 0]
print(f"Students kept: {near.sum()}")
print(f"Mean of final:   {final.mean():.3f}   (exact 82)")
print(f"SD of final:     {final.std():.3f}   (exact 8)")
print(f"P(final >= 90):  {np.mean(final >= 90):.4f}   (exact 0.1587)")
```

Output:

```text
Students kept: 67107
Mean of final:   81.989   (exact 82)
SD of final:     8.015   (exact 8)
P(final >= 90):  0.1577   (exact 0.1587)
```

Conditioning on a single value has probability 0, so the code uses a thin
window. Only about 0.7% of students land in it, which is why we need 10
million of them.

## Common Mistakes

- **Moving the mean all the way.** A midterm 2 SDs above average does **not**
  predict a final 2 SDs above average. The slope $\Sigma_{12}/\Sigma_{22}$
  (here 0.75 marks per mark, or $\rho = 0.6$ SDs per SD) pulls the guess back
  toward the mean. This is **regression to the mean**
  ([22.6](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.6-Regression-to-the-Mean.md)).
- **Making the conditional variance depend on $x_2$.** For a Gaussian it does
  not. A surprising observation moves the center, not the width.
- **Mixing up the blocks.** $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ multiplies the
  surprise of the **observed** block. Write down which block is observed before
  you start.
- **Using the formula for non-Gaussian data.** For other distributions the
  conditional mean need not be linear, and the conditional variance can change
  with $x_2$.
- **Combining observations one at a time as if independent.** In Example 2,
  B and C are correlated; the matrix inverse $\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ takes care of
  the overlap. Adding the two 2-D updates separately would double count.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Gaussian process regression predicts the
  function value at a new input by this exact formula, with $\mathbf{X}_2$ the
  observed training outputs. Missing-value imputation in Gaussian models works
  the same way.
- **Robotics and navigation:** the "update" step of a Kalman filter is this
  conditional formula: the state is $\mathbf{X}_1$, the sensor reading is
  $\mathbf{X}_2$.
- **Statistics:** linear regression is the conditional mean of a Gaussian;
  the slope $\Sigma_{12}/\Sigma_{22}$ is the regression coefficient
  ([14.5 Best Predictor](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)).
- **Weather and geostatistics:** "kriging" fills in values between weather
  stations or mining samples, as in Example 2
  ([21.11 Weather and Forecasting](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)).
- **Everyday life:** guessing a child's adult height from the parents'
  heights is a conditional Gaussian prediction, pulled toward the average.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In Example 1, a student scored exactly 65 on the midterm (the
average). What is the distribution of the final?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The surprise is $65 - 65 = 0$, so the mean stays at 70. The variance is still
$100 - 36 = 64$:

$$X_1 \mid X_2 = 65 \ \sim \ \mathcal{N}(70, 64)$$

Even an average midterm reduces the uncertainty, from SD 10 to SD 8.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $(X_1, X_2)$ have means 0, variances 1 and correlation
$0.8$. Find the distribution of $X_1$ given $X_2 = 1.5$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\Sigma_{12} = 0.8$ and $\Sigma_{22} = 1$:

$$X_1 \mid X_2 = 1.5 \ \sim \ \mathcal{N}(0.8 \cdot 1.5, \ 1 - 0.8^2) = \mathcal{N}(1.2, 0.36)$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Example 1, go the other way: a student scored 90 on the
final. What is the distribution of the midterm mark?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Now we observe $X_1 = 90$ and want $X_2$. Swap the roles of the indices:

$$E = 65 + \frac{48}{100}(90 - 70) = 65 + 9.6 = 74.6, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = 64 - \frac{48^2}{100} = 64 - 23.04 = 40.96$$

So the midterm is $\mathcal{N}(74.6, 40.96)$, with SD $6.4 = 8\sqrt{1 - 0.36}$.
The slopes in the two directions ($0.75$ and $0.48$) are different; they are
not inverses of each other.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In Example 2, predict station A using only station C (which
reads 22). Compare with the answer that uses B and C.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $\Sigma_{12} = 2$ and $\Sigma_{22} = 6$:

$$E = 20 + \frac{2}{6}(22 - 22) = 20, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = 4 - \frac{2^2}{6} = 4 - 0.667 \approx 3.333$$

Using C alone gives $\mathcal{N}(20, 3.33)$. Using B and C gives
$\mathcal{N}(20.86, 3.05)$: more information, smaller variance.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** In 2-D, let $m(x_2)$ and $v$ be the conditional mean and
variance of $X_1$ given $X_2 = x_2$. Show that
$\operatorname{Var}(m(X_2)) + v = \Sigma_{11}$. (This is the law of total
variance, [14.4](../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md).)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$m(X_2) = \mu_1 + \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{22}}(X_2 - \mu_2)$ is a constant
plus a constant times $X_2$, so

$$\operatorname{Var}(m(X_2)) = \left( \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{22}} \right)^2 \Sigma_{22} = \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}}$$

Since $v = \Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}}$ is the same for every $x_2$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(m(X_2)) + v = \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}} + \Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}} = \Sigma_{11}$$

The total variance splits into the part explained by $X_2$ and the part left
over.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 2 by simulation. Keep samples with B within
0.25 of 24 and C within 0.25 of 22, and compare the mean and variance of A
with $20.857$ and $3.048$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
mu = np.array([20.0, 21.0, 22.0])
Sigma = np.array([[4.0, 2.0, 2.0], [2.0, 5.0, 3.0], [2.0, 3.0, 6.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=4_000_000)
near = (np.abs(X[:, 1] - 24) < 0.25) & (np.abs(X[:, 2] - 22) < 0.25)
A = X[near, 0]                               # station A when B = 24 and C = 22
print(f"Kept {near.sum()} samples")
print(f"Mean: {A.mean():.3f}   (exact {20 + 18 / 21:.3f})")
print(f"Var:  {A.var():.3f}   (exact {64 / 21:.3f})")
```

Output:

```text
Kept 9660 samples
Mean: 20.854   (exact 20.857)
Var:  3.073   (exact 3.048)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Conditioning a Gaussian on some of its entries gives a Gaussian.
- In 2-D: $X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2$ has mean $\mu_1 + \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{22}}(x_2 - \mu_2)$
  and variance $\Sigma_{11} - \frac{\Sigma_{12}^2}{\Sigma_{22}}$.
- The conditional mean is linear in the observation; the conditional variance
  is smaller and the same for every observation.
- The proof: write the vector with the Cholesky trick, observed part first;
  observing fixes some of the standard normals and leaves the others alone.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{X}_1 \mid \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2 \ \sim \ \mathcal{N}\left( \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2), \ \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \right)}
$$
