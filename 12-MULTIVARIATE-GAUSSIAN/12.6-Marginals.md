# 12.6 Marginals

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.6 Marginals

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [12.5 Affine Transformations](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)

> **Big idea:** Any part of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian, and you get its mean and covariance by simply keeping the matching entries of the mean vector and the matching rows and columns of the covariance matrix.

---

## Why It Matters

A model often has many variables, but a question is usually about a few of
them. In general, the marginal distribution needs an integral over all the
other variables ([10.3 Marginal Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.3-Marginal-Distributions.md)),
and in high dimensions that integral can be impossible to do by hand. For a
Gaussian, the integral is never needed: you just **read off** the answer. This
is one reason Gaussian processes and Kalman filters can work with huge
numbers of variables.

## Intuition

A health survey models height (cm), weight (kg) and age (years) together as
one 3-D Gaussian. A clothing company only cares about height. What is the
distribution of height alone? The answer is just "normal, with the height
mean and the height variance from the model". Nothing about weight or age
changes it.

Picture the 2-D Gaussian hill again. Shine a light from the side and look at
its **shadow** on the $x_1$-axis: you add up the hill along each vertical line.
The shadow of a Gaussian hill is a 1-D bell curve, centered below the top of
the hill. Tilting the hill (correlation) changes the hill, but the shadow is
still a bell curve with variance $\Sigma_{11}$.

## Picture

The marginal of $X_1$ is the shadow of the joint hill on the $x_1$-axis: a
1-D normal with the same center $\mu_1$.

## Definition

**Two dimensions first.** If $(X_1, X_2)$ is bivariate normal with mean
$(\mu_1, \mu_2)$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$, then

$$
X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_{11}), \qquad X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \Sigma_{22})
$$

**In words:** each coordinate of a bivariate normal is normal, with its own
mean and its own variance. The covariance $\Sigma_{12}$ plays no role.

**The general statement.** Split a Gaussian vector into two parts, a top
block $\mathbf{X}_1$ (the first $k$ entries) and a bottom block $\mathbf{X}_2$
(the other $d - k$ entries). Split $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\Sigma$ the same
way:

$$
\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{X}_1 \cr \mathbf{X}_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \cr \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \cr \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $\Sigma_{11}$ is the $k \times k$ covariance matrix of $\mathbf{X}_1$,
$\Sigma_{22}$ that of $\mathbf{X}_2$, and $\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21}^\top$
holds the covariances between the two blocks. (In 2-D every block is a single
number, and this is the notation used above.) Then

$$
\mathbf{X}_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma_{11})
$$

**In words:** to get the marginal of some of the variables, keep their
entries of the mean, keep their rows and columns of the covariance matrix,
and throw away everything else.

The variables do not have to be the first ones. For any list of positions,
keep those entries and those rows and columns.

## Why It Is True

A marginal is an affine map that **selects** entries. In 2-D, take the
$1 \times 2$ matrix $A$ with the single row $(1, 0)$, so
$A\mathbf{X} = X_1$. By [12.5 Affine Transformations](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md),
$X_1$ is Gaussian, and:

$$
A\boldsymbol{\mu} = 1 \cdot \mu_1 + 0 \cdot \mu_2 = \mu_1, \qquad A\Sigma A^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \cr \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr 0 \end{bmatrix} = \Sigma_{11}
$$

In $d$ dimensions, use the block matrix $A$ made of the $k \times k$
identity $I_k$ next to a $k \times (d - k)$ block of zeros. Then
$A\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}_1$, and the same multiplication, block by block,
gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\boldsymbol{\mu} &= I_k \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + 0 \cdot \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 && \text{(block multiplication)} \\
A\Sigma A^\top &= \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I_k \cr 0 \end{bmatrix} = \Sigma_{11} && \text{(the zero block removes } \Sigma_{12} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\mathbf{X}_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma_{11})$. The integral
over $\mathbf{x}_2$ is done for us by the affine rule. (Practice 5 does the
integral by hand in 2-D, to see that it really works.)

### The Converse Is False

If $(X, Y)$ is jointly Gaussian, then $X$ and $Y$ are each normal. But two
normal variables are **not** always jointly Gaussian. Here is the standard
counterexample.

Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and let $S$ be a fair random sign, $+1$ or $-1$
with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, independent of $X$. Set $Y = SX$.

- $Y$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$: by symmetry, $-X$ has the same distribution as
  $X$, so flipping the sign at random changes nothing (Practice 4).
- $(X, Y)$ is **not** jointly Gaussian: $X + Y$ equals $0$ whenever $S = -1$,
  so $P(X + Y = 0) = \frac{1}{2}$. A jointly Gaussian pair would make $X + Y$
  normal ([12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)), and a normal variable has
  probability 0 of hitting one exact value (or, if its variance is 0,
  probability 1).

> **Warning:** "Each variable is normal" is weaker than "the variables are
> jointly normal". All the nice rules of this module need the stronger one.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Height, Weight and Age

**Problem.** (Height cm, weight kg, age years) is Gaussian with

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 170 \cr 65 \cr 40 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 100 & 50 & 0 \cr 50 & 100 & 20 \cr 0 & 20 & 144 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(a) What is the distribution of (height, age)? (b) What fraction of people
weigh more than 80 kg?

**Solution.**

1. (a) Keep positions 1 and 3. The mean is $(170, 40)$. Keep rows 1 and 3
   and columns 1 and 3 of $\Sigma$:

$$
(\text{height}, \text{age}) \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{bmatrix} 170 \cr 40 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 100 & 0 \cr 0 & 144 \end{bmatrix} \right)
$$

2. (b) Keep only position 2: weight $\sim \mathcal{N}(65, 100)$, so its SD is 10.
3. Standardize:

$$
P(\text{weight} \gt 80) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{80 - 65}{10} \right) = P(Z \gt 1.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.5) \approx 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668
$$

**Answer.** (a) Height and age are bivariate normal with means 170 and 40,
SDs 10 and 12, and covariance 0. (b) About $0.067$, or 6.7% of people.

**Check.** In (a) the covariance block is diagonal, so by
[12.8](12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md) height and age are even
independent in this model. Weight is correlated with both, but that does not
matter for the marginals.

### Example 2: Marginal, Then a Linear Combination

**Problem.** $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2, X_3)$ is Gaussian with mean $(1, 2, 3)$
and the covariance matrix of [12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md),
Example 3, with rows $(4, 2, 2)$, $(2, 5, 3)$ and $(2, 3, 6)$. Find
$P(X_1 \gt X_3)$.

**Solution.**

1. Only $X_1$ and $X_3$ matter. Their marginal: means 1 and 3, variances
   $\Sigma_{11} = 4$ and $\Sigma_{33} = 6$, covariance $\Sigma_{13} = 2$.
2. Let $W = X_1 - X_3$. By [12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md),
   $E[W] = 1 - 3 = -2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(W) = 4 - 2 \cdot 2 + 6 = 6$.
3. So $W \sim \mathcal{N}(-2, 6)$ and

$$
P(W \gt 0) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{0 - (-2)}{\sqrt{6}} \right) = P(Z \gt 0.8165) = 1 - \Phi(0.8165) \approx 0.2071
$$

**Answer.** $P(X_1 \gt X_3) \approx 0.207$.

**Check.** $X_2$ never entered the calculation, as expected. The answer is
below $\frac{1}{2}$ because $X_3$ has the larger mean.

## Simulate It

Sample the 3-D model of Example 1 and check that the (height, age) part has
exactly the sub-matrix as covariance, and that $P(\text{weight} \gt 80)$
matches.

```python
import numpy as np

np.set_printoptions(suppress=True)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 1_000_000

# (height cm, weight kg, age years) as a 3-D Gaussian.
mu = np.array([170.0, 65.0, 40.0])
Sigma = np.array([[100.0,  50.0,   0.0],
                  [ 50.0, 100.0,  20.0],
                  [  0.0,  20.0, 144.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=n_people)

# Marginal of (height, age): keep entries 0 and 2 of mu, rows/columns 0 and 2 of Sigma.
keep = [0, 2]
print("Simulated mean of (height, age):", X[:, keep].mean(axis=0).round(2))
print("Simulated cov of (height, age):\n", np.cov(X[:, keep].T).round(1))
print("Sub-matrix of Sigma:\n", Sigma[np.ix_(keep, keep)])

# Marginal of weight is N(65, 100): P(weight > 80) = P(Z > 1.5).
print(f"P(weight > 80): {np.mean(X[:, 1] > 80):.4f}   exact 0.0668")
```

Output:

```text
Simulated mean of (height, age): [170.    39.99]
Simulated cov of (height, age):
 [[100.   -0.1]
 [ -0.1 143.9]]
Sub-matrix of Sigma:
 [[100.   0.]
 [  0. 144.]]
P(weight > 80): 0.0668   exact 0.0668
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Integrating when you do not need to.** For a Gaussian, the marginal is
  read off from $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\Sigma$. No integral is needed.
- **Keeping the rows but not the columns.** The marginal covariance of
  variables 1 and 3 uses rows 1 and 3 **and** columns 1 and 3. Keeping only
  rows gives a matrix of the wrong shape.
- **Taking a variance as an SD.** The marginal of $X_i$ is
  $\mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \Sigma_{ii})$; its SD is $\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}}$.
- **Believing the converse.** Normal marginals do not make a vector jointly
  Gaussian. $Y = SX$ above is the classic trap.
- **Thinking correlation changes the marginal.** The marginal of $X_1$ does
  not depend on $\Sigma_{12}$. Correlation matters only for questions that
  involve both variables.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a Gaussian process is an infinite Gaussian
  vector (one entry per input). To predict at 10 points, you only ever use the
  10-dimensional marginal, which is why Gaussian processes are practical.
- **Robotics:** a robot's state may have position, speed and angle; to draw
  its position uncertainty on a map, you take the 2-D marginal of the position
  entries.
- **Finance:** the risk of one stock in a big multivariate model is read
  from its own diagonal entry; a sub-portfolio uses its own sub-matrix.
- **Statistics:** confidence intervals for one parameter of a model come from
  one diagonal entry of the estimated covariance matrix.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In Example 1, what is the distribution of age alone? What
fraction of people are older than 64?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Age $\sim \mathcal{N}(40, 144)$, so its SD is 12.

$$P(\text{age} \gt 64) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{64 - 40}{12} \right) = P(Z \gt 2) \approx 0.0228$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** In Example 1, write the mean and covariance matrix of
(weight, age).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Keep positions 2 and 3. The mean is $(65, 40)$. The covariance matrix has rows
$(100, 20)$ and $(20, 144)$, so the correlation is
$\frac{20}{10 \cdot 12} \approx 0.167$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Example 2, find $P(X_1 + X_3 \gt 6)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X_1 + X_3$ has mean $1 + 3 = 4$ and variance $4 + 2 \cdot 2 + 6 = 14$:

$$P(X_1 + X_3 \gt 6) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{6 - 4}{\sqrt{14}} \right) = P(Z \gt 0.5345) \approx 0.2965$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In the counterexample, prove that $Y = SX$ is
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and that $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Split on the sign, using that $S$ is independent of $X$, and that $-X$ has the
same distribution as $X$:

$$P(Y \le y) = \tfrac{1}{2} P(X \le y) + \tfrac{1}{2} P(-X \le y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Phi(y) + \tfrac{1}{2}\Phi(y) = \Phi(y)$$

For the covariance, both means are 0 and $S$ is independent of $X$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[X \cdot SX] = E[S] \thinspace E[X^2] = 0 \cdot 1 = 0$$

So $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated, normal, and still clearly dependent
($\lvert Y \rvert = \lvert X \rvert$ always).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $(X_1, X_2)$ be bivariate normal with means 0, variances 1
and correlation $\rho$, so that its PDF is
$f(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{x_1^2 - 2\rho x_1 x_2 + x_2^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \right)$.
Integrate out $x_2$ by hand and show that $X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Complete the square in $x_2$:

$$x_1^2 - 2\rho x_1 x_2 + x_2^2 = (x_2 - \rho x_1)^2 + (1 - \rho^2) x_1^2$$

So the exponent splits into a part with $x_2$ and a part without it:

$$f(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_2 - \rho x_1)^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \right) \exp\left( -\frac{x_1^2}{2} \right)$$

The first exponential, integrated over all $x_2$, is the integral of an
unnormalized $\mathcal{N}(\rho x_1, 1 - \rho^2)$ density, which equals
$\sqrt{2\pi(1 - \rho^2)}$. Therefore:

$$f_{X_1}(x_1) = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi(1 - \rho^2)}}{2\pi\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} e^{-x_1^2/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x_1^2/2}$$

This is the $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ density. (The leftover piece,
$\mathcal{N}(\rho x_1, 1 - \rho^2)$, is the conditional distribution of $X_2$
given $X_1 = x_1$: see [12.7 Conditionals](12.7-Conditionals.md).)

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the counterexample $Y = SX$. Check that
$P(Y \le 1) \approx \Phi(1) \approx 0.8413$, that the covariance is near 0,
and that $P(X + Y = 0) \approx 0.5$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
X = rng.standard_normal(n)
S = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=n)          # a fair random sign, independent of X
Y = S * X
print(f"P(Y <= 1):    {np.mean(Y <= 1):.4f}   (N(0,1) gives 0.8413)")
print(f"Cov(X, Y):    {np.mean(X * Y):.4f}   (exact 0)")
print(f"P(X + Y = 0): {np.mean(X + Y == 0):.4f}   (a normal would give 0)")
```

Output:

```text
P(Y <= 1):    0.8417   (N(0,1) gives 0.8413)
Cov(X, Y):    0.0020   (exact 0)
P(X + Y = 0): 0.4994   (a normal would give 0)
```

</details>

## Summary

- Every sub-vector of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian.
- Its mean is the matching part of $\boldsymbol{\mu}$; its covariance is the
  matching sub-matrix of $\Sigma$ (same rows and same columns).
- In 2-D: $X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_{11})$, whatever the correlation.
- The proof is the affine rule with a "selection" matrix; no integral is needed.
- The converse fails: normal marginals do not imply a jointly Gaussian vector.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{X}_1 \cr \mathbf{X}_2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \cr \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \cr \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix} \right) \implies \mathbf{X}_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma_{11})}
$$
