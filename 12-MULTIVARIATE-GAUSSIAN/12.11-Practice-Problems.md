# 12.11 Practice Problems

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.11 Practice Problems

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 3 hours · **Before this:** [12.10 Bivariate Normal](12.10-Bivariate-Normal.md)

> **Big idea:** A Gaussian vector stays Gaussian under linear maps, marginals and conditioning, so almost every problem reduces to finding one mean and one covariance and then using a 1-D normal table.

---

## How to Use This Set

- Try each problem on paper before you open the answer. Write down $\boldsymbol{\mu}$
  and $\Sigma$ first; most mistakes come from a wrong matrix.
- Each problem names the topic it uses. If you are stuck, reread that topic.
- Many problems share one 3-D model, called **Model M** below, so you can see
  how the tools fit together.
- Do the simulation projects last. They check several results at once.

**Model M.** $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2, X_3)$ is Gaussian with

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cr -1 \cr 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 3 & 0 \cr 3 & 4 & 1 \cr 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(Its eigenvalues are about $10.42$, $3$ and $0.58$, all positive, so it is a
valid covariance matrix.)

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy, 12.1)** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ in 2-D,
with $\Sigma$ diagonal with entries 1 and 9. Find the density at the origin.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\det \Sigma = 1 \cdot 9 = 9$ and the quadratic form at $\mathbf{0}$ is 0:

$$f(\mathbf{0}) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{9}} e^{0} = \frac{1}{6\pi} \approx 0.0531$$

</details>

**W2. (Easy, 12.6)** In Model M, what is the distribution of $X_2$? Find
$P(X_2 \gt 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Keep entry 2 of $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and entry $(2, 2)$ of $\Sigma$:
$X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(-1, 4)$, with SD 2.

$$P(X_2 \gt 1) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{1 - (-1)}{2} \right) = P(Z \gt 1) \approx 0.1587$$

</details>

**W3. (Easy, 12.9)** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(10, 4)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(5, 12)$
are independent. Find $P(X \lt Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$D = X - Y \sim \mathcal{N}(10 - 5, 4 + 12) = \mathcal{N}(5, 16)$, with SD 4.

$$P(X \lt Y) = P(D \lt 0) = \Phi\left( \frac{0 - 5}{4} \right) = \Phi(-1.25) \approx 0.1056$$

</details>

**W4. (Easy, 12.4)** Find the Cholesky factor of the matrix with rows
$(16, 8)$ and $(8, 13)$, and write a recipe that samples from
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\ell_{11} = \sqrt{16} = 4$, $\ell_{21} = 8/4 = 2$, $\ell_{22} = \sqrt{13 - 2^2} = 3$.
So $L$ has rows $(4, 0)$ and $(2, 3)$. Recipe: $X_1 = 4Z_1$ and
$X_2 = 2Z_1 + 3Z_2$ with independent standard normals. Check:
$\operatorname{Var}(X_2) = 4 + 9 = 13$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 4 \cdot 2 = 8$.

</details>

**W5. (Easy, 12.8)** In Model M, which pairs of variables are independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Model M is jointly Gaussian, so a zero covariance means independence.
$\Sigma_{13} = 0$, so $X_1 \perp X_3$. The pairs $(X_1, X_2)$ and $(X_2, X_3)$
have covariances 3 and 1, so they are dependent.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium, 12.2)** Let $\Sigma$ have rows $(6, 2)$ and $(2, 3)$. Find the
eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors, and describe the contour with $c = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\lambda^2 - 9\lambda + (18 - 4) = \lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 14 = (\lambda - 7)(\lambda - 2) = 0$$

For $\lambda_1 = 7$: $(6 - 7)q_1 + 2q_2 = 0$, so $q_2 = q_1/2$ and
$\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(2, 1)$. For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $\mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(1, -2)$.
The contour is an ellipse centered at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, with half-length
$\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$ along $(2, 1)$ (about 26.6 degrees above the
$x_1$-axis) and $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ along $(1, -2)$.

</details>

**C2. (Medium, 12.3)** With $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\Sigma$ from
C1, find the squared Mahalanobis distance of the point $(3, -1)$. Is the
point outside the 95% contour? How rare is a point at least this far out?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\det \Sigma = 18 - 4 = 14$, and $\Sigma^{-1}$ is $\frac{1}{14}$ times the
matrix with rows $(3, -2)$ and $(-2, 6)$:

$$D^2 = \frac{3 \cdot 3^2 - 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot (-1) + 6 \cdot (-1)^2}{14} = \frac{27 + 12 + 6}{14} = \frac{45}{14} \approx 3.214$$

The 95% cut-off in 2-D is $5.99$, so the point is inside. In 2-D,
$P(D^2 \gt t) = e^{-t/2}$, so a point at least this far out has probability
$e^{-1.607} \approx 0.200$: not rare.

</details>

**C3. (Medium, 12.5)** In Model M, let $Y_1 = X_1 + X_2$ and $Y_2 = X_2 - X_3$.
Find the joint distribution of $(Y_1, Y_2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$A$ has rows $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, -1)$. Mean: $A\boldsymbol{\mu} = (2 - 1, -1 - 0) = (1, -1)$.
The entries of $A\Sigma A^\top$, using bilinearity:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y_1) = 9 + 2 \cdot 3 + 4 = 19, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_2) = 4 - 2 \cdot 1 + 1 = 3$$

$$\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1, Y_2) = \Sigma_{12} - \Sigma_{13} + \Sigma_{22} - \Sigma_{23} = 3 - 0 + 4 - 1 = 6$$

So $(Y_1, Y_2)$ is bivariate normal with mean $(1, -1)$, variances 19 and 3,
and covariance 6.

</details>

**C4. (Medium, 12.7)** In Model M, find the distribution of $X_1$ given
$X_2 = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the 2-D formula with $\Sigma_{11} = 9$, $\Sigma_{12} = 3$, $\Sigma_{22} = 4$:

$$E[X_1 \mid X_2 = 1] = 2 + \frac{3}{4}(1 - (-1)) = 2 + 1.5 = 3.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_1 \mid X_2 = 1) = 9 - \frac{3^2}{4} = 6.75$$

So $X_1 \mid X_2 = 1 \sim \mathcal{N}(3.5, 6.75)$.

</details>

**C5. (Medium, 12.7)** In Model M, find the distribution of $X_1$ given
$X_2 = 1$ **and** $X_3 = 0$. Compare with C4. Why does observing $X_3$ change
the answer, even though $X_1 \perp X_3$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\Sigma_{12} = (3, 0)$ and $\Sigma_{22}$ has rows $(4, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$,
with determinant 3, so $\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ is $\frac{1}{3}$ times the matrix with
rows $(1, -1)$ and $(-1, 4)$. The weights are
$\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}(3, -3) = (1, -1)$. The surprise is
$(1 - (-1), 0 - 0) = (2, 0)$.

$$E = 2 + (1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 0) = 4, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = 9 - (1 \cdot 3 + (-1) \cdot 0) = 6$$

So $X_1 \mid (X_2 = 1, X_3 = 0) \sim \mathcal{N}(4, 6)$. The variance is smaller
than in C4 (6 versus 6.75). $X_3$ is independent of $X_1$, but it is linked to
$X_2$: knowing $X_3$ tells us which part of $X_2$ is noise coming from $X_3$
and which part is shared with $X_1$. Independence of a pair does not make an
observation useless once other variables are observed too.

</details>

**C6. (Medium, 12.10)** Today's and tomorrow's maximum temperatures are
bivariate normal, both with mean 20 and SD 5, and $\rho = 0.7$. Today it was
30 degrees. Find the chance that tomorrow is above 30 degrees.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E = 20 + 0.7 \cdot \frac{5}{5}(30 - 20) = 27, \qquad \text{SD} = 5\sqrt{1 - 0.49} = 5\sqrt{0.51} \approx 3.571$$

$$P(\text{tomorrow} \gt 30 \mid \text{today} = 30) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{30 - 27}{3.571} \right) = P(Z \gt 0.840) \approx 0.200$$

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, 12.8 and 12.10, proof)** Let $(X, Y)$ be bivariate normal with
means $\mu_X, \mu_Y$, SDs $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y$ and correlation $\rho$. Find $b$
such that $R = Y - bX$ is independent of $X$, and use it to prove
$E[Y \mid X = x] = \mu_Y + \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X)$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x) = \sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$(X, R)$ is a linear map of $(X, Y)$, so it is jointly Gaussian
([12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)). It is independent exactly when the
covariance is 0 ([12.8](12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md)):

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y - bX) = \rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y - b\sigma_X^2 = 0 \implies b = \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}$$

Now $Y = bX + R$ with $R$ independent of $X$. Given $X = x$, the distribution
of $R$ does not change, so $Y \mid X = x$ is $bx + R$:

$$E[Y \mid X = x] = bx + E[R] = bx + \mu_Y - b\mu_X = \mu_Y + \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X)$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x) = \operatorname{Var}(R) = \sigma_Y^2 - 2b\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y + b^2\sigma_X^2 = \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho^2\sigma_Y^2 + \rho^2\sigma_Y^2 = \sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$$

Since $R$ is normal, $Y \mid X = x$ is normal too.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, 12.3 and 12.5, proof)** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$,
let $A$ be an invertible $d \times d$ matrix, and let $\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}$.
Prove that the Mahalanobis distance does not change: the distance of
$A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ under the distribution of $\mathbf{Y}$ equals the
distance of $\mathbf{x}$ under the distribution of $\mathbf{X}$. What does this
mean for changing units?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\mathbf{Y}$ has mean $A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$ and covariance $A\Sigma A^\top$.
For $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, the difference is
$\mathbf{y} - (A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}) = A(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$.
Using $(A\Sigma A^\top)^{-1} = (A^\top)^{-1}\Sigma^{-1}A^{-1}$ and $(A\mathbf{v})^\top = \mathbf{v}^\top A^\top$:

$$D_{\mathbf{Y}}^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top A^\top (A^\top)^{-1} \Sigma^{-1} A^{-1} A (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = D_{\mathbf{X}}^2$$

So measuring height in inches instead of cm, or temperature in Fahrenheit
instead of Celsius, does not change how unusual a point is. Euclidean distance
does not have this property.

</details>

**H3. (Hard, 12.7)** A sensor measures a true temperature $\theta$. Before the
reading you believe $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(20, 4)$. The sensor reads
$Y = \theta + \varepsilon$, with noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$
independent of $\theta$. The reading is $y = 25$. Find the distribution of
$\theta$ given $Y = 25$, and show that its variance is
$\left( \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{1} \right)^{-1}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$(\theta, Y)$ is a linear map of the independent normals $(\theta, \varepsilon)$,
so it is jointly Gaussian with

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 4 + 1 = 5, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\theta, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(\theta, \theta + \varepsilon) = 4$$

By the 2-D conditional formula of [12.7](12.7-Conditionals.md):

$$E[\theta \mid Y = 25] = 20 + \frac{4}{5}(25 - 20) = 24, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\theta \mid Y = 25) = 4 - \frac{4^2}{5} = 0.8$$

And $\left( \frac{1}{4} + 1 \right)^{-1} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$. So the precisions
(one over the variance) add: prior precision $\frac{1}{4}$ plus sensor
precision 1. The new guess, 24, sits between the prior mean 20 and the reading
25, closer to the more precise source. This is one step of a Kalman filter.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (12.4 and 12.2)** Sample one million points from $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$
with $\Sigma$ from C1, using the Cholesky trick. Check the sample covariance,
and check that the contours with $c = 1, 2, 3$ hold the fractions
$1 - e^{-c^2/2}$. (Optional: plot the cloud and the three ellipses with
matplotlib.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 1_000_000

Sigma = np.array([[6.0, 2.0],
                  [2.0, 3.0]])
L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)
X = rng.standard_normal((n_samples, 2)) @ L.T     # samples of N(0, Sigma)
print("Sample covariance:\n", np.cov(X.T).round(3))

# Squared Mahalanobis distance of each point; contour c holds 1 - exp(-c^2/2).
D2 = np.sum(X @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma) * X, axis=1)
for c in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(f"c = {c}: inside {np.mean(D2 <= c**2):.4f}   exact {1 - np.exp(-c**2 / 2):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Sample covariance:
 [[5.985 1.994]
 [1.994 3.002]]
c = 1: inside 0.3937   exact 0.3935
c = 2: inside 0.8651   exact 0.8647
c = 3: inside 0.9889   exact 0.9889
```

</details>

**S2. (12.3)** Show by simulation that the squared Mahalanobis distance of
$\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ in $d$ dimensions
follows $\chi^2(d)$. Use random $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\Sigma$ for $d = 3$
and $d = 10$, and compare the quartiles.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 500_000

for d in [3, 10]:
    A = rng.normal(size=(d, d))
    Sigma = A @ A.T + np.eye(d)                  # a random valid covariance
    mu = rng.normal(size=d)
    X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=n_samples)
    diff = X - mu
    D2 = np.sum(diff @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma) * diff, axis=1)
    # Compare the quartiles of D^2 with those of chi-square(d).
    print(f"d = {d}")
    print("  simulated quartiles:", np.quantile(D2, [0.25, 0.5, 0.75]).round(3))
    print("  chi2(d) quartiles:  ", stats.chi2.ppf([0.25, 0.5, 0.75], d).round(3))
```

Output:

```text
d = 3
  simulated quartiles: [1.211 2.363 4.104]
  chi2(d) quartiles:   [1.213 2.366 4.108]
d = 10
  simulated quartiles: [ 6.737  9.338 12.536]
  chi2(d) quartiles:   [ 6.737  9.342 12.549]
```

</details>
