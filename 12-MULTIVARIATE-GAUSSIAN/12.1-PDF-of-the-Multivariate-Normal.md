# 12.1 PDF of the Multivariate Normal

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.1 PDF of the Multivariate Normal

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 11: Covariance and Correlation](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/README.md)

> **Big idea:** The multivariate normal is a bell-shaped hill in many dimensions, fully described by a mean vector (where the top is) and a covariance matrix (how the hill is stretched and tilted).

---

## Why It Matters

Real data has many numbers per item: height and weight, the pixels of an
image, the position and speed of a robot. We need one distribution for the
whole vector, not one per number. The **multivariate normal** is that
distribution. It is the most used multi-dimensional distribution in
probability, statistics and machine learning, and the rest of this module
shows why: almost every question about it has an exact answer.

## Intuition

Throw darts at a board. Say the left-right error $X_1$ and the up-down error
$X_2$ are each standard normal, $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (mean 0, variance 1), and
independent. The chance of landing near the point $(x_1, x_2)$ is the product
of the two 1-D bell curves. Both curves are biggest at 0, so the darts pile up
in the center. The product depends only on $x_1^2 + x_2^2$, the squared
distance from the center. So the density is a **round hill**.

Now measure the height and weight of 1,000 adults. Taller people tend to be
heavier, so the cloud of points is not round. It is an oval, stretched along
the diagonal "tall and heavy / short and light". The hill is still
bell-shaped, but it is **stretched and tilted**.

The multivariate normal describes every such hill with two things:

- the **mean vector**: where the top of the hill is, for example 170 cm and 65 kg;
- the **covariance matrix**: how wide the hill is in each direction and how it
  tilts (the variances and covariances from
  [11.5 Random Vectors and Covariance Matrix](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.5-Random-Vectors-and-Covariance-Matrix.md)).

## Definition

### Matrix Tools You Need

We write a vector as a column. For $d = 2$:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \cr x_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The **transpose** $\mathbf{x}^\top$ turns the column into a row. Four tools are
enough for this whole topic. Here they are for a $2 \times 2$ matrix $M$ with
entries $a, b, c, d$:

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \cr c & d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det M = ad - bc, \qquad M^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \cr -c & a \end{bmatrix}
$$

- The **determinant** $\det M$ is one number. It tells how much the map
  $\mathbf{x} \mapsto M\mathbf{x}$ stretches area. If $\det M = 3$, every small
  square becomes a shape with 3 times the area.
- The **inverse** $M^{-1}$ undoes $M$: $M^{-1} M = I$, where $I$ is the
  **identity matrix** (1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere). It exists only when
  $\det M \ne 0$.
- The **quadratic form** $\mathbf{v}^\top M \mathbf{v}$ is one number. For a
  symmetric $M$ (so $b = c$) it is $a v_1^2 + 2b v_1 v_2 + d v_2^2$.

For example, take $\Sigma$ below. Its determinant is $2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 3$:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \cr 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \cr -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Check one entry of $\Sigma^{-1} \Sigma$: the top-left is
$\frac{1}{3}(2 \cdot 2 + (-1) \cdot 1) = \frac{3}{3} = 1$. The other entries
work the same way.

### The Formula in Two Dimensions

A random vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2)^\top$ has the **bivariate normal**
distribution with mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance matrix
$\Sigma$ if its joint PDF is

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{\det \Sigma}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right)
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (\mu_1, \mu_2)^\top$ holds the two means and
$\Sigma$ holds the variances $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ on the diagonal and the
covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)$ off the diagonal. Topic
[12.10 Bivariate Normal](12.10-Bivariate-Normal.md) writes this out with
$\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and $\rho$.

### The Formula in d Dimensions

A random vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_d)^\top$ has the **multivariate
normal** (or **multivariate Gaussian**) distribution, written
$\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$, if its joint PDF is

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \sqrt{\det \Sigma}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right), \qquad \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d
$$

where:

- $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the **mean vector**, $\mu_i = E[X_i]$;
- $\Sigma$ is the $d \times d$ **covariance matrix**, $\Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$;
- $\Sigma$ must be symmetric and **positive definite**, which means
  $\mathbf{a}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{a} \gt 0$ for every nonzero vector $\mathbf{a}$.
  Then $\det \Sigma \gt 0$ and $\Sigma^{-1}$ exists. (Every covariance matrix
  has $\mathbf{a}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{a} \ge 0$, see
  [11.6 Covariance Matrix is PSD](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.6-Covariance-Matrix-is-PSD.md).)

**In words:** the density is a constant times $e$ to the power "minus one half
of the squared, shape-adjusted distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to the center". The
constant makes the total volume under the hill equal to 1.

The number $(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$
is the squared **Mahalanobis distance**. It gets its own topic,
[12.3 Mahalanobis Distance](12.3-Mahalanobis-Distance.md).

## Why It Is True

**Step 1: independent standard normals.** Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_d$ be independent
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. By independence, the joint PDF is the product of the 1-D
PDFs $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}$ from
[9.2 Normal Gaussian](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z}) &= \prod_{i=1}^{d} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z_i^2/2} && \text{(independence)} \\
&= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{d} z_i^2 \right) && \text{(multiply the exponentials)} \\
&= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{z} \right) && \text{(since } \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{z} = z_1^2 + \dots + z_d^2 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the formula with $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\Sigma = I$
(then $\det I = 1$ and $I^{-1} = I$).

**Step 2: stretch, tilt and shift.** Take an invertible matrix $A$ with
$A A^\top = \Sigma$ (topic [12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)
shows how to find one) and set $\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + A\mathbf{Z}$.
Then $\mathbf{z} = A^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$. The map stretches
area by $\lvert \det A \rvert$, so the density must shrink by the same factor
(the change-of-variables rule, see
[13.3 Jacobian](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac{1}{\lvert \det A \rvert} f_{\mathbf{Z}}\big( A^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \big) && \text{(change of variables)} \\
\mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{z} &= (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top (A^{-1})^\top A^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(since } (M\mathbf{v})^\top = \mathbf{v}^\top M^\top \text{)} \\
&= (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top (A A^\top)^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(inverse of a product)} \\
&= (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(since } AA^\top = \Sigma \text{)} \\
\det \Sigma &= \det A \cdot \det A^\top = (\det A)^2 && \text{(so } \lvert \det A \rvert = \sqrt{\det \Sigma} \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Put these into Step 1 and you get exactly the formula in the Definition.

**Step 3: the parameters are the mean and covariance.** By linearity,
$E[\mathbf{X}] = \boldsymbol{\mu} + A E[\mathbf{Z}] = \boldsymbol{\mu}$. By
[11.7 Linear Transformations](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md),
$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}) = A I A^\top = \Sigma$. So the letters
$\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\Sigma$ really are the mean vector and the covariance
matrix.

**Check with d = 1.** Then $\Sigma$ is the single number $\sigma^2$, so
$\det \Sigma = \sigma^2$ and $\Sigma^{-1} = 1/\sigma^2$:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2} \sqrt{\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu) \frac{1}{\sigma^2} (x - \mu) \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)
$$

This is the familiar 1-D normal PDF. The multivariate formula is a true
generalization.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Independent Coordinates

**Problem.** $X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$
are independent (the second parameter is the variance, so $X_2$ has SD 2).
Find the joint density at $(1, 2)$ in two ways: as a product, and with the
matrix formula.

**Solution.**

1. Product of 1-D densities:

$$
f(1, 2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-1/2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 4}} e^{-4/8} = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 2} e^{-1/2 - 1/2} = \frac{e^{-1}}{4\pi}
$$

2. Matrix formula. Independence means covariance 0, so $\Sigma$ is diagonal:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \cr 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det \Sigma = 4, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \cr 0 & 1/4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

3. The quadratic form is $1 \cdot 1^2 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2^2 = 1 + 1 = 2$, so:

$$
f(1, 2) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{4}} e^{-2/2} = \frac{e^{-1}}{4\pi} \approx \frac{0.3679}{12.566} \approx 0.0293
$$

**Answer.** $f(1, 2) = \frac{e^{-1}}{4\pi} \approx 0.0293$ both ways.

**Check.** With a diagonal $\Sigma$ the formula must split into a product of
1-D normals. It does, which is a first sign that "uncorrelated" and
"independent" are the same for Gaussians (topic
[12.8](12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md)).

### Example 2: Correlation Changes Which Points Are Likely

**Problem.** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$, where
$\Sigma$ is the example matrix from the Definition (variances 2, covariance 1).
Compare the density at $(1, 1)$ and at $(1, -1)$. Both points are the same
distance $\sqrt{2}$ from the center.

**Solution.**

1. From the Definition, $\det \Sigma = 3$ and
   $\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}$ times the matrix with rows $(2, -1)$ and $(-1, 2)$.
2. The constant is $\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{3}} \approx \frac{1}{10.883} \approx 0.09189$.
3. At $(1, 1)$ the quadratic form is
   $\frac{1}{3}(2 \cdot 1^2 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1^2) = \frac{2}{3}$.
4. At $(1, -1)$ it is
   $\frac{1}{3}(2 \cdot 1^2 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot (-1)^2) = \frac{6}{3} = 2$.
5. So:

$$
f(1, 1) = 0.09189 \cdot e^{-1/3} \approx 0.09189 \times 0.7165 \approx 0.0658, \qquad f(1, -1) = 0.09189 \cdot e^{-1} \approx 0.0338
$$

**Answer.** The density at $(1, 1)$ is about $0.0658$, nearly twice the
density at $(1, -1)$, which is about $0.0338$.

**Check.** The covariance is positive, so $X_1$ and $X_2$ tend to have the
same sign. A point like $(1, 1)$, where both are positive, should be more
likely than $(1, -1)$. It is, by a factor $e^{2/3} \approx 1.95$.

### Example 3: Height and Weight

**Problem.** Adult height (cm) and weight (kg) follow a bivariate normal with
mean vector $(170, 65)^\top$, both SDs equal to 10, and correlation $0.5$. So
the covariance is $0.5 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 50$. About what fraction of adults
have height within 1 cm of 180 and weight within 1 kg of 75?

**Solution.**

1. Write the parameters:

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 170 \cr 65 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 100 & 50 \cr 50 & 100 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det \Sigma = 100 \cdot 100 - 50 \cdot 50 = 7500
$$

2. The inverse is $\frac{1}{7500}$ times the matrix with rows $(100, -50)$ and $(-50, 100)$.
3. The difference is $\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} = (10, 10)^\top$. The quadratic form is
   $\frac{1}{7500}(100 \cdot 10^2 - 2 \cdot 50 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 + 100 \cdot 10^2) = \frac{10000}{7500} = \frac{4}{3}$.
4. The density:

$$
f(180, 75) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{7500}} e^{-2/3} \approx \frac{0.5134}{544.14} \approx 0.000944
$$

5. The region is a 2 cm by 2 kg box, with area 4. For a small region,
   probability is about density times area: $0.000944 \times 4 \approx 0.00377$.

**Answer.** About $0.0038$, or roughly 4 adults in 1,000.

**Check.** The density is a "probability per square unit" (here per cm times
kg). It is small because the hill is spread over a wide area. The simulation
below counts real samples in the box and agrees.

## Simulate It

Draw 4 million (height, weight) pairs and count how many land in the small
box of Example 3. Compare with density times area.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 4_000_000

# Height (cm) and weight (kg): means 170 and 65, SDs 10 and 10, correlation 0.5.
mu = np.array([170.0, 65.0])
Sigma = np.array([[100.0, 50.0],
                  [50.0, 100.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=n_people)

# The PDF formula with d = 2.
def mvn_pdf(x, mu, Sigma):
    diff = x - mu
    q = diff @ np.linalg.inv(Sigma) @ diff          # (x - mu)^T Sigma^{-1} (x - mu)
    d = len(mu)
    return np.exp(-q / 2) / ((2 * np.pi) ** (d / 2) * np.sqrt(np.linalg.det(Sigma)))

# Probability of a small 2 cm x 2 kg box (area 4) around (180, 75) is about f x area.
point = np.array([180.0, 75.0])
in_box = np.all(np.abs(X - point) <= 1.0, axis=1)
print(f"Fraction in the box: {in_box.mean():.5f}")
print(f"f(180, 75) x 4:      {4 * mvn_pdf(point, mu, Sigma):.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Fraction in the box: 0.00374
f(180, 75) x 4:      0.00377
```

The count from real samples matches the formula to within about 1%.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using $\Sigma$ instead of $\Sigma^{-1}$ in the exponent.** A big variance
  must make the hill **wider**, so it must make the exponent **smaller**. That
  only happens with the inverse.
- **Wrong constant.** It is $(2\pi)^{d/2}$, not $(2\pi)^d$, and
  $\sqrt{\det \Sigma}$, not $\det \Sigma$. Check your formula with $d = 1$: it
  must give $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}$.
- **Putting SDs on the diagonal.** The diagonal of $\Sigma$ holds variances
  $\sigma_i^2$. Off the diagonal are covariances $\rho \sigma_i \sigma_j$, not
  correlations.
- **Reading the density as a probability.** $f(\mathbf{x})$ can be larger
  than 1. With $\Sigma = 0.01 I$ in two dimensions, $f(\mathbf{0}) \approx 15.9$.
  Only density times a small area is a probability.
- **Using a matrix that is not a valid covariance.** The matrix with rows
  $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ has determinant $-3$, so it would need a correlation
  of 2. It cannot be a covariance matrix, and the formula makes no sense.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Gaussian discriminant analysis and Gaussian
  mixture models ([10.8 Mixture Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md))
  fit one multivariate normal per class or cluster. Variational autoencoders
  and diffusion models start from noise $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$
  ([21.1.4 Generative Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
- **Computer science and robotics:** a robot's belief about its position and
  speed is a multivariate normal, updated by the Kalman filter.
- **Finance:** daily returns of many stocks are often modelled as a
  multivariate normal to measure portfolio risk
  ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Weather:** errors of temperature, pressure and wind forecasts at many
  places are modelled together as one big Gaussian vector.
- **Everyday life:** body measurements (height, arm length, shoe size) are
  roughly jointly normal, which is why a small set of clothing sizes fits most
  people.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$ in two dimensions.
What is the density at the origin?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\det I = 1$ and the quadratic form at $\mathbf{0}$ is $0$:

$$f(\mathbf{0}) = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 1} e^{0} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \approx 0.159$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Find the determinant and the inverse of this matrix:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \cr 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\det \Sigma = 4 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 2 = 16$$

Swap the diagonal entries, change the sign of the off-diagonal entries, and
divide by 16. So $\Sigma^{-1}$ has rows $(\frac{5}{16}, -\frac{2}{16})$ and
$(-\frac{2}{16}, \frac{4}{16})$. Check the top-left entry of $\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma$:
$\frac{1}{16}(5 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2) = 1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ with
$\boldsymbol{\mu} = (1, 0)^\top$ and $\Sigma$ from Problem 2. Find $f(2, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The difference is $(1, 1)^\top$. With $\Sigma^{-1}$ from Problem 2:

$$(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \frac{1}{16}(5 \cdot 1^2 - 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 1^2) = \frac{5}{16}$$

$$f(2, 1) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{16}} e^{-5/32} = \frac{1}{8\pi} e^{-0.15625} \approx 0.03979 \times 0.8553 \approx 0.0340$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Which of these can be the covariance matrix of a bivariate
normal with a PDF? (a) rows $(3, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$; (b) rows $(1, 2)$ and
$(2, 1)$; (c) rows $(4, -2)$ and $(-2, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Yes. Variances are positive and $\det = 9 - 1 = 8 \gt 0$.

(b) No. $\det = 1 - 4 = -3 \lt 0$. The implied correlation is $2 / (1 \cdot 1) = 2$, which is impossible.

(c) Not with a PDF. $\det = 4 - 4 = 0$, so $\Sigma^{-1}$ does not exist. The
correlation is exactly $-1$: $X_2$ is a straight-line function of $X_1$, and
all the probability sits on a line. This is a **degenerate** Gaussian.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that when $\Sigma$ is diagonal, with entries
$\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_d^2$, the multivariate normal PDF is the product of
the 1-D normal PDFs of $\mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a diagonal matrix, the determinant is the product of the diagonal
entries, and the inverse is diagonal with entries $1/\sigma_i^2$. So:

$$\det \Sigma = \prod_{i=1}^{d} \sigma_i^2, \qquad (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{(x_i - \mu_i)^2}{\sigma_i^2}$$

The exponential of a sum is a product of exponentials, and
$(2\pi)^{d/2} \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 \cdots \sigma_d^2} = \sqrt{2\pi\sigma_1^2} \cdots \sqrt{2\pi\sigma_d^2}$. So:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^{d} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_i^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_i - \mu_i)^2}{2\sigma_i^2} \right)$$

A joint PDF that factors like this means the $X_i$ are independent.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** For $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with the
$\Sigma$ of Example 2, estimate the probability of the small box
$[0.9, 1.1] \times [-1.1, -0.9]$ and compare it with $f(1, -1) \times 0.04$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
Sigma = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal([0.0, 0.0], Sigma, size=4_000_000)
in_box = (np.abs(X[:, 0] - 1) <= 0.1) & (np.abs(X[:, 1] + 1) <= 0.1)
exact = np.exp(-1) / (2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(3)) * 0.04
print(f"Simulation: {in_box.mean():.5f}")
print(f"f x area:   {exact:.5f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.00135
f x area:   0.00135
```

</details>

## Summary

- A multivariate normal is described by a mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ (the
  center) and a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ (the spread and tilt).
- The PDF is a constant times $e$ to the power minus one half of the squared
  Mahalanobis distance. Points with the same distance have the same density.
- $\Sigma$ must be symmetric and positive definite; then $\det \Sigma \gt 0$
  and $\Sigma^{-1}$ exists.
- The formula comes from $d$ independent standard normals, stretched by a
  matrix $A$ with $AA^\top = \Sigma$ and shifted by $\boldsymbol{\mu}$.
- With $d = 1$ it gives back the usual normal PDF.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \sqrt{\det \Sigma}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right)}
$$
