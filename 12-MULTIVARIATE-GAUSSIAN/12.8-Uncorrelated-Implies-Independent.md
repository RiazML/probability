# 12.8 Uncorrelated Implies Independent

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.8 Uncorrelated Implies Independent

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [12.7 Conditionals](12.7-Conditionals.md)

> **Big idea:** For jointly Gaussian variables, zero covariance is the same as independence, so checking one number in $\Sigma$ settles a question that is usually very hard.

---

## Why It Matters

In general, uncorrelated variables can still be strongly dependent: $X$ and
$X^2$ are the classic example
([11.4 Uncorrelated vs Independent](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md)).
Proving independence normally means checking that the joint PDF factors
everywhere. For jointly Gaussian variables, you only need to check that a
covariance is 0. This shortcut is used all the time: in PCA, in regression,
and in the proof that the sample mean and sample variance of normal data are
independent, which is the basis of the t-test.

## Intuition

Think of the Gaussian hill again. With correlation, the ellipses are tilted.
Slice the hill at a big value of $x_1$, and the slice for $x_2$ is centered
high; slice it at a small $x_1$, and the slice is centered low. Knowing $x_1$
tells you something about $x_2$: dependence.

Now set the covariance to 0. The ellipses line up with the axes. Every
vertical slice now has the **same** center and the **same** width; only its
height changes. Knowing $x_1$ tells you nothing about $x_2$. That is
independence.

Why can't a Gaussian hide dependence the way $X$ and $X^2$ do? A Gaussian is
completely described by its means and covariances. There is no other place
where dependence could hide. So if the covariances say "no linear link",
there is no link at all.

## Picture

```text
   Correlated: dependent                Uncorrelated: independent

   x2       slice at x1 = a  b          x2      slice at x1 = a  b
    |                  :  :              |                   :  :
    |             .-'':''-:.             |      .-''''''''''':''':-.
    |          .-'    :  .:'             |    .'             :   :  '.
    |       .-'    μ  :.' :              |   :        μ      :   :   :
    |     .'      .'  :   :              |    '.             :   :  .'
    |    :     .-'    :   :              |      '-...........:...:-'
    |     '--''       :   :              |                   :   :
    +-------------------------- x1       +--------------------------- x1

   the slice at b sits higher than      every slice has the same center
   the slice at a: x1 tells about x2    and width: x1 says nothing of x2
```

With covariance 0 the ellipses are lined up with the axes, so every slice
through the hill looks the same.

## Definition

**Two dimensions first.** Let $(X_1, X_2)$ be **jointly** Gaussian
(bivariate normal). Then

$$
X_1 \perp X_2 \iff \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 0
$$

**In words:** for a bivariate normal, "uncorrelated" and "independent" mean
the same thing.

**The general statement.** Let $\mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{X}_1, \mathbf{X}_2)$ be
jointly Gaussian, split into blocks as in [12.6 Marginals](12.6-Marginals.md).
Then the block $\mathbf{X}_1$ is independent of the block $\mathbf{X}_2$ if and
only if every covariance between them is 0:

$$
\mathbf{X}_1 \perp \mathbf{X}_2 \iff \Sigma_{12} = \mathbf{0}
$$

In particular, $X_1, \dots, X_d$ are **mutually independent** if and only if
$\Sigma$ is diagonal.

**In words:** inside one Gaussian vector, a zero block in $\Sigma$ means two
groups of variables have nothing to do with each other.

> **Warning:** The word **jointly** is essential. Two variables that are each
> normal, but not jointly normal, can be uncorrelated and dependent (see the
> example $Y = SX$ in [12.6](12.6-Marginals.md) and Practice 2 below).

## Why It Is True

**Independent implies uncorrelated.** This is true for all random variables:
if $X_1 \perp X_2$, then $E[X_1 X_2] = E[X_1]E[X_2]$, so the covariance is 0
([11.1 Covariance](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.1-Covariance.md)).

**Uncorrelated implies independent, in 2-D.** Let $\Sigma_{12} = 0$. Then
$\Sigma$ is diagonal, with entries $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$, and the PDF of
[12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md) splits:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_1, x_2) &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{(x_1 - \mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(x_2 - \mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2} \right) \right) && \text{(diagonal } \Sigma \text{)} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_1^2}} e^{-\frac{(x_1 - \mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_2^2}} e^{-\frac{(x_2 - \mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}} && \text{(split the constant and the exponential)} \\
&= f_{X_1}(x_1) \thinspace f_{X_2}(x_2) && \text{(the marginals, by 12.6)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A joint PDF that factors into the marginals for all $(x_1, x_2)$ means
independence ([10.5 Independence of Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)).

**Another view, from 12.7.** With $\Sigma_{12} = 0$, the conditional
distribution of $X_1$ given $X_2 = x_2$ is
$\mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + 0, \Sigma_{11} - 0) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_{11})$:
exactly the marginal, whatever $x_2$ is. Learning $X_2$ changes nothing.

**The general block case.** If $\Sigma_{12} = \mathbf{0}$ (and so
$\Sigma_{21} = \mathbf{0}$), then $\Sigma$ is **block diagonal**:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & 0 \cr 0 & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11}^{-1} & 0 \cr 0 & \Sigma_{22}^{-1} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det \Sigma = \det \Sigma_{11} \cdot \det \Sigma_{22}
$$

So the quadratic form splits into a part with only $\mathbf{x}_1$ plus a part
with only $\mathbf{x}_2$, and the constant splits too:

$$
(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = (\mathbf{x}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)^\top \Sigma_{11}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1) + (\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^\top \Sigma_{22}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)
$$

The exponential of a sum is a product, so $f(\mathbf{x}) = f_{\mathbf{X}_1}(\mathbf{x}_1) f_{\mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_2)$.
The blocks are independent.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Splitting Off the Independent Part

**Problem.** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$
having rows $(4, 2)$ and $(2, 5)$. Find a number $c$ such that
$R = X_2 - cX_1$ is independent of $X_1$. What is the distribution of $R$?

**Solution.**

1. $(X_1, R)$ is a linear map of $\mathbf{X}$, so it is jointly Gaussian
   ([12.5](12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)). So we only need $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, R) = 0$.
2. By bilinearity: $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2 - cX_1) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) - c\operatorname{Var}(X_1) = 2 - 4c$.
3. Set it to 0: $c = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$.
4. Variance of $R$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left( X_2 - \tfrac{1}{2}X_1 \right) = 5 - 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 4 = 5 - 2 + 1 = 4
$$

**Answer.** $c = \frac{1}{2}$, and $R \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$ is independent
of $X_1$. So $X_2 = \frac{1}{2}X_1 + R$: "a part explained by $X_1$ plus
independent noise".

**Check.** The slope $c = \Sigma_{12}/\Sigma_{11}$ is the regression slope of
[12.7](12.7-Conditionals.md), and $\operatorname{Var}(R) = 5 - \frac{2^2}{4} = 4$
is the conditional variance of $X_2$ given $X_1$. Both agree.

### Example 2: Sum and Difference, Gaussians Versus Dice

**Problem.** Let $X, Y \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.
Show that $U = X + Y$ and $W = X - Y$ are independent. Then do the same
calculation for two fair dice and see what goes wrong.

**Solution.**

1. $(U, W)$ is a linear map of the Gaussian vector $(X, Y)$, so it is jointly
   Gaussian.
2. $\operatorname{Cov}(U, W) = \operatorname{Var}(X) - \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) + \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X) - \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = 0$.
3. Jointly Gaussian and uncorrelated, so $U \perp W$.
4. Dice: the same algebra gives $\operatorname{Cov}(U, W) = 0$. But if
   $U = 2$, both dice show 1, so $W = 0$ for sure. In general
   $P(W = 0) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$, while $P(W = 0 \mid U = 2) = 1$.
   So $U$ and $W$ are dependent.

**Answer.** For normal $X, Y$: $U$ and $W$ are independent. For dice: they
are uncorrelated but dependent.

**Check.** The dice are not Gaussian, so the theorem does not apply. The
simulation below shows both cases side by side.

## Simulate It

For Gaussian $X, Y$, compare $P(U \gt 1, W \gt 1)$ with $P(U \gt 1)P(W \gt 1)$.
For dice, compare $P(W = 0)$ with $P(W = 0 \mid U = 2)$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Gaussian case: X, Y iid N(0, 1), U = X + Y, W = X - Y.
X, Y = rng.standard_normal(n), rng.standard_normal(n)
U, W = X + Y, X - Y
print(f"Gaussian: corr(U, W) = {np.corrcoef(U, W)[0, 1]:.4f}")
print(f"  P(U > 1, W > 1)    = {np.mean((U > 1) & (W > 1)):.4f}")
print(f"  P(U > 1) P(W > 1)  = {np.mean(U > 1) * np.mean(W > 1):.4f}")

# Dice case: two fair dice, same U and W. Uncorrelated, but NOT independent.
D1, D2 = rng.integers(1, 7, n), rng.integers(1, 7, n)
U, W = D1 + D2, D1 - D2
print(f"Dice:     corr(U, W) = {np.corrcoef(U, W)[0, 1]:.4f}")
print(f"  P(W = 0)           = {np.mean(W == 0):.4f}   (exact {1/6:.4f})")
print(f"  P(W = 0 | U = 2)   = {np.mean(W[U == 2] == 0):.4f}   (exact 1)")
```

Output:

```text
Gaussian: corr(U, W) = 0.0012
  P(U > 1, W > 1)    = 0.0576
  P(U > 1) P(W > 1)  = 0.0574
Dice:     corr(U, W) = -0.0002
  P(W = 0)           = 0.1665   (exact 0.1667)
  P(W = 0 | U = 2)   = 1.0000   (exact 1)
```

For the Gaussian pair the joint probability equals the product (exact value
$(1 - \Phi(1/\sqrt{2}))^2 \approx 0.0575$). For the dice the correlation is
also 0, yet one value of $U$ completely fixes $W$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting "jointly".** Two normal variables with zero correlation are
  independent only if they are jointly normal. $Y = SX$ from
  [12.6](12.6-Marginals.md) is normal and uncorrelated with $X$, yet
  $\lvert Y \rvert = \lvert X \rvert$ always.
- **Using it outside the Gaussian world.** For dice, exponential times, or
  real data with curved patterns, zero correlation proves nothing about
  independence.
- **Checking only some of the covariances.** For two blocks to be
  independent, **every** entry of $\Sigma_{12}$ must be 0. One zero covariance
  makes only that one pair independent.
- **Reading a small sample correlation as exactly 0.** A sample correlation
  of $0.01$ is evidence, not proof, that the true covariance is 0.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** PCA rotates Gaussian data onto the eigenvectors
  of $\Sigma$ ([12.2](12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md)); the new coordinates are
  uncorrelated, and for Gaussian data they are fully independent. "Whitening"
  inputs before training uses the same idea.
- **Statistics:** for normal samples, the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ is
  independent of the sample variance $S^2$ (Practice 5). This makes the
  Student-t distribution of the t-test exact
  ([9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)).
- **Signal processing:** independent component analysis (ICA) works only
  because real signals are **not** Gaussian; for Gaussians, removing
  correlation already removes all dependence, so there is nothing left to find.
- **Finance:** factor models split a return into a part driven by the market
  plus an independent, stock-specific part, as in Example 1.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** In [12.6](12.6-Marginals.md), Example 1, (height, weight, age)
is Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}(\text{height}, \text{age}) = 0$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(\text{height}, \text{weight}) = 50$ and
$\operatorname{Cov}(\text{weight}, \text{age}) = 20$. Which pairs are
independent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The vector is jointly Gaussian, so a zero covariance means independence.
Height and age are independent. Height and weight are dependent (covariance
50). Weight and age are dependent (covariance 20).

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = SX$, with $S$ a fair random
sign independent of $X$. Both are normal and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$.
Why are they not independent, and why does this not contradict the theorem?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

They are dependent because $\lvert Y \rvert = \lvert X \rvert$: knowing $X = 2$
tells you $Y$ is $2$ or $-2$. There is no contradiction, because $(X, Y)$ is
not jointly Gaussian: $X + Y = 0$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, which no
normal variable can do.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X, Y \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Are
$X + 2Y$ and $2X - Y$ independent? What about $X + Y$ and $X + 2Y$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Both pairs are jointly Gaussian (linear maps of $(X, Y)$), so check the covariance:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X + 2Y, 2X - Y) = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot 1 = 2 - 2 = 0$$

So $X + 2Y$ and $2X - Y$ are independent.

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X + Y, X + 2Y) = 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 3 \ne 0$$

So $X + Y$ and $X + 2Y$ are dependent.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In the weather model of [12.7](12.7-Conditionals.md),
Example 2 (covariance rows $(4, 2, 2)$, $(2, 5, 3)$, $(2, 3, 6)$), find $c$
so that $X_3 - cX_2$ is independent of $X_2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_2, X_3 - cX_2) = \Sigma_{23} - c\Sigma_{22} = 3 - 5c = 0 \implies c = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.
Prove that $\bar{X}_n$ is independent of the vector of deviations
$(X_1 - \bar{X}_n, \dots, X_n - \bar{X}_n)$, and so of the sample variance
$S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1}\sum_i (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The vector $(\bar{X}_n, X_1 - \bar{X}_n, \dots, X_n - \bar{X}_n)$ is a linear
map of the Gaussian vector $(X_1, \dots, X_n)$, so it is jointly Gaussian.
Only the term $j = i$ of $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_j X_j$ is correlated
with $X_i$, so:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(\bar{X}_n, X_i) = \frac{1}{n}\sigma^2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

$$\operatorname{Cov}(\bar{X}_n, X_i - \bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} - \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = 0$$

So the block $\bar{X}_n$ has zero covariance with every entry of the
deviation block. By the theorem, $\bar{X}_n$ is independent of the whole
deviation vector, and therefore of any function of it, such as $S^2$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Draw 200,000 samples of size 5, from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$
and from $\text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1, mean 1). For each, compute the correlation
between $\bar{X}$ and $S^2$ across the samples. What do you see?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_repeats, n = 200_000, 5

# 200,000 samples of size 5: sample mean and sample variance of each.
normal = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_repeats, n))
expo = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_repeats, n))
for name, data in [("Normal", normal), ("Exponential", expo)]:
    xbar = data.mean(axis=1)
    s2 = data.var(axis=1, ddof=1)
    print(f"{name:12s} corr(Xbar, S^2) = {np.corrcoef(xbar, s2)[0, 1]:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Normal       corr(Xbar, S^2) = 0.0041
Exponential  corr(Xbar, S^2) = 0.6821
```

For normal data the correlation is near 0, as Practice 5 predicts. For
exponential data a large mean usually comes with a large spread, so they are
strongly dependent.

</details>

## Summary

- Independence always implies zero covariance. The converse is false in
  general.
- For **jointly** Gaussian variables the converse is true: zero covariance
  implies independence.
- Blocks of a Gaussian vector are independent exactly when the cross-covariance
  block $\Sigma_{12}$ is zero; all entries are independent exactly when
  $\Sigma$ is diagonal.
- The proof: a block-diagonal $\Sigma$ makes the PDF factor.
- A useful trick: subtract the right multiple, $X_2 - \frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\Sigma_{11}}X_1$,
  to get a part that is independent of $X_1$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{(X_1, X_2) \text{ jointly Gaussian}: \quad X_1 \perp X_2 \iff \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 0}
$$
