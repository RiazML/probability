# 12.4 Standard Normal Vectors and Cholesky

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.4 Standard Normal Vectors and Cholesky

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [12.3 Mahalanobis Distance](12.3-Mahalanobis-Distance.md)

> **Big idea:** Every Gaussian vector is a stretched, tilted and shifted copy of independent standard normals, $\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$, and the Cholesky factor $L$ of $\Sigma$ is the cheapest way to build it.

---

## Why It Matters

A computer can easily make independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ numbers (for
example with the Box-Muller trick of
[13.8](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md)).
But simulations of stock prices, weather or robots need **correlated**
Gaussians with a given covariance matrix $\Sigma$. The Cholesky trick turns
independent noise into correlated noise with one matrix multiplication. Run
it backwards and it removes correlation ("whitening"), which is how the
Mahalanobis distance of [12.3](12.3-Mahalanobis-Distance.md) is computed in
practice.

## Intuition

How do you fake the math and physics marks of a class so that they are
correlated? Give each student two independent random numbers: $Z_1$, a
"general ability", and $Z_2$, a "physics-only luck". Then set

- math mark $= 2 Z_1$ (only general ability), and
- physics mark $= 1 \cdot Z_1 + 2 Z_2$ (some general ability, plus its own luck).

Because both marks contain the shared $Z_1$, they move together. The math
mark has variance $2^2 = 4$, the physics mark has variance $1^2 + 2^2 = 5$,
and the covariance comes only from the shared part: $2 \cdot 1 = 2$. We built
the covariance matrix with rows $(4, 2)$ and $(2, 5)$ from scratch.

The recipe is a matrix with zeros above the diagonal: the first variable uses
only $Z_1$, the second uses $Z_1$ and $Z_2$, the third would use $Z_1, Z_2, Z_3$,
and so on. That triangular matrix is the **Cholesky factor**.

## Picture

```text
   Z: standard normal vector          X = μ + L Z: a correlated Gaussian
   (round cloud, no correlation)      (tilted, stretched, shifted cloud)

   z2                                  x2
    |       . . .                       |                . .:.
    |    . : .:. : .                    |             . .:::'
    |   . :.:::::.: .   --- L Z --->    |          . .:::':
    |    . : ':': : .   stretch, tilt   |        .:::::' .
    |       . . .                       |      .:::'. .
    |                   --- + μ --->    |     ': .
    +----------------- z1               +------------------ x1
                         shift
```

Multiplying by $L$ turns the round cloud into a tilted ellipse with
covariance $LL^\top$; adding $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ moves its center.

## Definition

A **standard normal vector** is $\mathbf{Z} = (Z_1, \dots, Z_d)^\top$ with
$Z_1, \dots, Z_d \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Its mean is
$\mathbf{0}$ and its covariance is the identity, so
$\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$.

### Matrix Tool: The Cholesky Decomposition

A matrix is **lower triangular** if every entry above the diagonal is 0.
For every symmetric positive definite matrix $\Sigma$ there is exactly one
lower triangular $L$ with positive diagonal entries such that

$$
\Sigma = L L^\top
$$

This $L$ is the **Cholesky factor** of $\Sigma$. Think of it as the "square
root" of a matrix: as $\sigma$ is to $\sigma^2$, so $L$ is to $\Sigma$.

In two dimensions, with variances $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ and correlation
$\rho$:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \cr \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \cr \rho\sigma_2 & \sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \end{bmatrix}
$$

### The Sampling Recipe

$$
\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}, \quad \mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I) \implies \mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, LL^\top) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)
$$

**In words:** to sample from any Gaussian, draw $d$ independent standard
normals, multiply by the Cholesky factor, and add the mean.

In two dimensions the recipe reads:

$$
X_1 = \mu_1 + \sigma_1 Z_1, \qquad X_2 = \mu_2 + \sigma_2 \left( \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \thinspace Z_2 \right)
$$

**In words:** $X_2$ borrows a share $\rho$ of the noise that drives $X_1$, and
fills up the rest of its variance with its own fresh noise.

**Whitening** is the recipe run backwards: $\mathbf{Z} = L^{-1}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$
turns a Gaussian vector back into independent standard normals.

## Why It Is True

**The recipe has the right mean and covariance.** By linearity and by the
rule $\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{Z}) = A \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}) A^\top$
from [11.7 Linear Transformations](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[\mathbf{X}] &= \boldsymbol{\mu} + L E[\mathbf{Z}] = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{0} = \boldsymbol{\mu} && \text{(linearity)} \\
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}) &= L \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}) L^\top = L I L^\top = LL^\top = \Sigma && \text{(adding } \boldsymbol{\mu} \text{ does not change covariance)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The recipe gives a Gaussian.** Step 2 of "Why It Is True" in
[12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md) showed that
$\boldsymbol{\mu} + A\mathbf{Z}$ has exactly the multivariate normal PDF with
covariance $AA^\top$, for any invertible $A$. The Cholesky $L$ is invertible
(its diagonal entries are positive, and the determinant of a triangular
matrix is the product of its diagonal).

**Finding L in two dimensions.** Multiply out $LL^\top$ for an unknown
lower triangular $L$ and match the entries with $\Sigma$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \ell_{11} & 0 \cr \ell_{21} & \ell_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ell_{11} & \ell_{21} \cr 0 & \ell_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \ell_{11}^2 & \ell_{11}\ell_{21} \cr \ell_{11}\ell_{21} & \ell_{21}^2 + \ell_{22}^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \cr \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

1. Top-left: $\ell_{11}^2 = \sigma_1^2$, so $\ell_{11} = \sigma_1$.
2. Off-diagonal: $\ell_{11}\ell_{21} = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$, so $\ell_{21} = \rho\sigma_2$.
3. Bottom-right: $\ell_{22}^2 = \sigma_2^2 - \rho^2\sigma_2^2$, so $\ell_{22} = \sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$.

Step 3 needs $1 - \rho^2 \gt 0$, which is exactly the condition that $\Sigma$
is positive definite.

**Finding L in d dimensions.** The same matching works column by column,
from left to right. For column $j$:

$$
\ell_{jj} = \sqrt{\Sigma_{jj} - \sum_{k \lt j} \ell_{jk}^2}, \qquad \ell_{ij} = \frac{1}{\ell_{jj}} \left( \Sigma_{ij} - \sum_{k \lt j} \ell_{ik}\ell_{jk} \right) \quad \text{for } i \gt j
$$

**In words:** each diagonal entry is the variance that is still "unexplained"
by earlier columns; each entry below it is the covariance that is still
unexplained, divided by that diagonal entry. If $\Sigma$ is positive definite,
the number under every square root is positive. If it is not, the algorithm
fails, which is a handy test for a valid covariance matrix. The work grows
like $d^3/3$ operations, cheaper than finding eigenvectors.

> **Note:** $L$ is not the only "square root". Any $A$ with $AA^\top = \Sigma$
> works in the recipe, for example $A = Q\Lambda^{1/2}$ from the eigenvectors
> of [12.2](12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md). All give the same distribution,
> because a Gaussian is fixed by its mean and covariance. Cholesky is just the
> cheapest.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Correlated Variables, Forward and Backward

**Problem.** Let $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (1, 2)^\top$ and let $\Sigma$ have rows
$(4, 2)$ and $(2, 5)$. (a) Find $L$. (b) Turn the standard normal draw
$\mathbf{z} = (0.5, -1)$ into a sample $\mathbf{x}$. (c) Whiten $\mathbf{x}$
back to $\mathbf{z}$.

**Solution.**

1. $\ell_{11} = \sqrt{4} = 2$; $\ell_{21} = 2 / 2 = 1$; $\ell_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 1^2} = 2$. So:

$$
L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \cr 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad LL^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot 2 & 2 \cdot 1 \cr 1 \cdot 2 & 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \cr 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

2. Forward: $x_1 = 1 + 2(0.5) = 2$ and $x_2 = 2 + 1(0.5) + 2(-1) = 0.5$.
3. Backward (**forward substitution**, top row first): from row 1,
   $z_1 = (x_1 - \mu_1)/2 = (2 - 1)/2 = 0.5$. From row 2,
   $z_2 = (x_2 - \mu_2 - 1 \cdot z_1)/2 = (0.5 - 2 - 0.5)/2 = -1$.

**Answer.** $L$ has rows $(2, 0)$ and $(1, 2)$; the sample is
$\mathbf{x} = (2, 0.5)$; whitening gives back $\mathbf{z} = (0.5, -1)$.

**Check.** This is the class-marks story from the Intuition. A triangular $L$
makes whitening easy: you never need the full inverse, only one row at a time.

### Example 2: Correlated Stock Returns

**Problem.** Two stocks have daily returns with mean 0, SDs of 1% and 2%, and
correlation $0.5$. Working in percent, give a recipe that simulates one day.

**Solution.**

1. Here $\sigma_1 = 1$, $\sigma_2 = 2$, $\rho = 0.5$. The covariance is
   $0.5 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 1$, so $\Sigma$ has rows $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 4)$.
2. Use the 2-D formula: $\ell_{11} = 1$, $\ell_{21} = 0.5 \cdot 2 = 1$,
   $\ell_{22} = 2\sqrt{1 - 0.25} = 2\sqrt{0.75} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$.
3. The recipe: draw independent $Z_1, Z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and set

$$
R_1 = Z_1, \qquad R_2 = Z_1 + \sqrt{3} \thinspace Z_2
$$

**Answer.** $R_1 = Z_1$ and $R_2 = Z_1 + 1.732 Z_2$ (in percent).

**Check.** $\operatorname{Var}(R_2) = 1 + 3 = 4$ (SD 2) and
$\operatorname{Cov}(R_1, R_2) = \operatorname{Cov}(Z_1, Z_1) = 1$, so the
correlation is $\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} = 0.5$. Correct.

### Example 3: A 3 x 3 Cholesky by Hand

**Problem.** Find the Cholesky factor of

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \cr 2 & 5 & 3 \cr 2 & 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. Column 1: $\ell_{11} = \sqrt{4} = 2$, $\ell_{21} = 2/2 = 1$, $\ell_{31} = 2/2 = 1$.
2. Column 2: $\ell_{22} = \sqrt{5 - \ell_{21}^2} = \sqrt{5 - 1} = 2$, and
   $\ell_{32} = (3 - \ell_{31}\ell_{21}) / \ell_{22} = (3 - 1)/2 = 1$.
3. Column 3: $\ell_{33} = \sqrt{6 - \ell_{31}^2 - \ell_{32}^2} = \sqrt{6 - 1 - 1} = 2$.

**Answer.**

$$
L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \cr 1 & 2 & 0 \cr 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Check.** Entry $(3, 3)$ of $LL^\top$ is $1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6$, and entry
$(3, 2)$ is $1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 0 = 3$. Both match $\Sigma$. The
code below gets the same $L$.

## Simulate It

Run the column-by-column algorithm on Example 3, compare it with numpy, and
check that $\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$ has the right mean and covariance.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 1_000_000

Sigma = np.array([[4.0, 2.0, 2.0],
                  [2.0, 5.0, 3.0],
                  [2.0, 3.0, 6.0]])
mu = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])

# Cholesky by hand, column by column (the algorithm in the text).
d = len(Sigma)
L = np.zeros((d, d))
for j in range(d):
    L[j, j] = np.sqrt(Sigma[j, j] - np.sum(L[j, :j] ** 2))
    for i in range(j + 1, d):
        L[i, j] = (Sigma[i, j] - np.sum(L[i, :j] * L[j, :j])) / L[j, j]
print("L by hand:\n", L)
print("Same as numpy?", np.allclose(L, np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)))

# Sampling recipe: X = mu + L Z, with Z a standard normal vector.
Z = rng.standard_normal((n_samples, d))    # each row is one Z
X = mu + Z @ L.T                           # each row is mu + L z
print("Sample mean:", X.mean(axis=0).round(3))
print("Sample covariance:\n", np.cov(X.T).round(3))
```

Output:

```text
L by hand:
 [[2. 0. 0.]
 [1. 2. 0.]
 [1. 1. 2.]]
Same as numpy? True
Sample mean: [1.    2.003 3.003]
Sample covariance:
 [[3.994 1.996 1.995]
 [1.996 4.997 2.993]
 [1.995 2.993 5.995]]
```

Note the line `X = mu + Z @ L.T`: when samples are stored as **rows**, the
recipe $L\mathbf{z}$ becomes `z @ L.T`.

## Common Mistakes

- **Multiplying by $\Sigma$ instead of $L$.** $\boldsymbol{\mu} + \Sigma\mathbf{Z}$
  has covariance $\Sigma\Sigma^\top = \Sigma^2$, not $\Sigma$. In one dimension
  this is the classic slip of using $\sigma^2 Z$ instead of $\sigma Z$.
- **Using the upper factor by accident.** Some libraries (for example
  `scipy.linalg.cholesky` by default) return the upper triangular $U = L^\top$.
  Then $\boldsymbol{\mu} + U\mathbf{Z}$ has covariance $U U^\top = L^\top L$,
  which is usually **not** $\Sigma$. Check which one you have.
- **Taking square roots entry by entry.** The matrix of $\sqrt{\Sigma_{ij}}$
  is not a square root of $\Sigma$. You need $LL^\top = \Sigma$.
- **Making up correlations that cannot happen together.** Three variables
  cannot all have correlation $-0.9$ with each other. Such a matrix is not
  positive definite, and the Cholesky algorithm stops with a negative number
  under a square root (see Practice 4).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the **reparameterization trick** in
  variational autoencoders writes a Gaussian sample as
  $\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{z}$, so that gradients can flow through
  $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $L$ ([21.1.4 Generative Models](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)).
  Gaussian processes draw random functions this way.
- **Finance:** Monte Carlo risk models simulate thousands of correlated asset
  returns per day with one Cholesky factor
  ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Computer science:** Cholesky is the standard fast, stable way to solve
  the linear systems $\Sigma \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{b}$ that appear in
  least squares and Kalman filters.
- **Engineering:** simulating correlated wind loads, sensor noise or
  manufacturing errors for reliability studies.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Find the Cholesky factor of the matrix with rows $(9, 3)$ and
$(3, 5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\ell_{11} = \sqrt{9} = 3$, $\ell_{21} = 3/3 = 1$, $\ell_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 1} = 2$.
So $L$ has rows $(3, 0)$ and $(1, 2)$. Check: $\ell_{21}^2 + \ell_{22}^2 = 1 + 4 = 5$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You sample with $X_1 = 2Z_1$ and $X_2 = Z_1 + Z_2$, where
$Z_1, Z_2$ are independent standard normals. What is the covariance matrix
of $(X_1, X_2)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$L$ has rows $(2, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, so $\Sigma = LL^\top$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X_1) = 4, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_2) = 1 + 1 = 2, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 2 \cdot 1 = 2$$

So $\Sigma$ has rows $(4, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$, and the correlation is
$\frac{2}{2\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Using $X_1 = \sigma_1 Z_1$ and
$X_2 = \sigma_2(\rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} Z_2)$, show directly that
$\operatorname{Var}(X_2) = \sigma_2^2$ and the correlation of $X_1$ and $X_2$
is $\rho$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent with variance 1:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X_2) = \sigma_2^2 \left( \rho^2 + (1 - \rho^2) \right) = \sigma_2^2$$

Only the shared $Z_1$ contributes to the covariance:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \rho \operatorname{Var}(Z_1) = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2, \qquad \text{correlation} = \frac{\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} = \rho$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Someone proposes three variables, each with variance 1, with
correlations $0.9$ between $X_1$ and $X_2$, $0.9$ between $X_1$ and $X_3$, and
$-0.9$ between $X_2$ and $X_3$. Run the Cholesky algorithm. What happens, and
why does it make sense?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Column 1: $\ell_{11} = 1$, $\ell_{21} = 0.9$, $\ell_{31} = 0.9$. Column 2:
$\ell_{22} = \sqrt{1 - 0.81} = \sqrt{0.19} \approx 0.436$ and
$\ell_{32} = (-0.9 - 0.9 \cdot 0.9)/0.436 \approx -3.923$. Column 3 needs

$$\ell_{33}^2 = 1 - 0.9^2 - 3.923^2 \approx 1 - 0.81 - 15.39 = -15.2 \lt 0$$

The algorithm fails, so the matrix is not positive definite (its determinant
is about $-2.89$). It makes sense: if $X_1$ is close to both $X_2$ and $X_3$,
then $X_2$ and $X_3$ must be close to each other too. They cannot also be
strongly opposite.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $A = Q\Lambda^{1/2}$, where $\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ is
the eigen-decomposition of [12.2](12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md) and
$\Lambda^{1/2}$ is diagonal with entries $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Show that
$AA^\top = \Sigma$, and explain why $\boldsymbol{\mu} + A\mathbf{Z}$ and
$\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$ have the same distribution even though
$A \ne L$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A diagonal matrix equals its own transpose, so:

$$AA^\top = Q\Lambda^{1/2} (\Lambda^{1/2})^\top Q^\top = Q \Lambda^{1/2} \Lambda^{1/2} Q^\top = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \Sigma$$

By Step 2 of "Why It Is True" in [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md),
$\boldsymbol{\mu} + A\mathbf{Z}$ has the PDF of $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, AA^\top)$,
and $\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$ has the PDF of $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, LL^\top)$.
Both covariances equal $\Sigma$, so both PDFs are the same function. The
individual samples differ (for the same $\mathbf{z}$), but the distributions
are identical.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate one million days of the two stocks in Example 2.
Estimate the correlation and the probability that both stocks fall on the
same day. (The exact answer, $\frac{1}{3}$, comes from
[12.10](12.10-Bivariate-Normal.md).)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_days = 1_000_000
L = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [1.0, np.sqrt(3)]])   # Cholesky factor of [[1, 1], [1, 4]]
Z = rng.standard_normal((n_days, 2))
R = Z @ L.T                                      # daily returns in percent, mean 0
print(f"Correlation: {np.corrcoef(R.T)[0, 1]:.4f}   (exact 0.5)")
print(f"P(both fall): {np.mean((R[:, 0] < 0) & (R[:, 1] < 0)):.4f}   (exact {1/3:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Correlation: 0.4989   (exact 0.5)
P(both fall): 0.3334   (exact 0.3333)
```

</details>

## Summary

- A standard normal vector $\mathbf{Z}$ has independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$
  entries: $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$.
- Every Gaussian is $\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$.
- The Cholesky factor $L$ is lower triangular; find it column by column. In
  2-D it has rows $(\sigma_1, 0)$ and $(\rho\sigma_2, \sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2})$.
- Whitening, $\mathbf{Z} = L^{-1}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$, removes
  correlation and is solved one row at a time.
- If the algorithm meets a negative number under a square root, the matrix
  is not a valid covariance matrix.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}, \quad \Sigma = LL^\top, \quad \mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I) \implies \mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)}
$$
