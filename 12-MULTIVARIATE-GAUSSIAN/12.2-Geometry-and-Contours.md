# 12.2 Geometry and Contours

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.2 Geometry and Contours

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [12.1 PDF of the Multivariate Normal](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md)

> **Big idea:** The points of equal density of a Gaussian form ellipses centered at the mean, and the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the covariance matrix give the directions and lengths of their axes.

---

## Why It Matters

A formula with $\Sigma^{-1}$ in it is hard to "see". The contour picture
turns the covariance matrix into a shape you can draw: an ellipse with a
center, a direction and two widths. This picture explains correlation at a
glance, and it is the idea behind **principal component analysis** (PCA),
confidence ellipses in GPS and robotics, and outlier detection.

## Intuition

A hiking map draws a hill with **contour lines**: each line joins points at
the same height. For a Gaussian hill, the height is the density $f(\mathbf{x})$.

Start with two independent standard normals. The density depends only on
$x_1^2 + x_2^2$, so the contour lines are **circles**. Now make $X_1$ three
times as spread out as $X_2$, still independent. The circles get pulled
sideways into **ellipses** whose long side lies along the $x_1$-axis.

Finally add correlation. Think of the math and physics marks of 500 students.
Good students tend to score well in both, so the cloud of points leans along
the diagonal. The ellipses turn so that their long axis points "up and to the
right". The question of this topic is: exactly which direction, and exactly
how long? The answer comes from the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$.

One contour of a 2-D Gaussian with positive correlation: an ellipse centered
at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ whose axes point along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$.

## Definition

### Matrix Tool: Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

Most vectors change direction when you multiply them by a matrix. An
**eigenvector** of $\Sigma$ is a special nonzero vector $\mathbf{q}$ that
keeps its direction: $\Sigma$ only stretches it by a number $\lambda$, called
its **eigenvalue**:

$$
\Sigma \mathbf{q} = \lambda \mathbf{q}
$$

**In words:** along an eigenvector, the matrix acts like plain multiplication
by a number.

Take the matrix from [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md):

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \cr 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cr 3 \end{bmatrix} = 3 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr -1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cr -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

So $(1, 1)$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3, and $(1, -1)$ is an
eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.

**How to find them for a 2 x 2 matrix.** The eigenvalues solve
$\det(\Sigma - \lambda I) = 0$. For a symmetric matrix with entries $a, b, b, d$:

$$
(a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - b^2 = 0 \iff \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - b^2) = 0
$$

For each root $\lambda$, solve $(a - \lambda) q_1 + b q_2 = 0$ for the direction.

Three facts hold for every covariance matrix (they are symmetric and
positive definite). We use them without proof; any linear algebra book has
them under the name **spectral theorem**:

- All eigenvalues are real and positive: $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \gt 0$.
- The eigenvectors can be chosen of length 1 and at right angles to each other.
- Put them as the columns of a matrix $Q$. Then $Q^\top Q = I$ ($Q$ is a
  rotation, maybe with a flip) and

$$
\Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^\top, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = Q \Lambda^{-1} Q^\top
$$

where $\Lambda$ is the diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues.

### Contours Are Ellipses

A **contour** of the density is a set of points with the same density. For
the multivariate normal $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$, the density
depends on $\mathbf{x}$ only through the quadratic form, so each contour is

$$
\left\lbrace \mathbf{x} : (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = c^2 \right\rbrace
$$

for some number $c \gt 0$. This set is an **ellipse** (in $d$ dimensions, an
**ellipsoid**) with:

- center $\boldsymbol{\mu}$;
- axes along the eigenvectors $\mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_d$ of $\Sigma$;
- half-length $c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ along $\mathbf{q}_i$.

**In words:** the ellipse sits on the mean, points its long axis along the
eigenvector with the biggest eigenvalue, and each axis is as long as $c$
standard deviations in that direction.

## Why It Is True

Rotate the coordinates so that the axes line up with the eigenvectors. Let
$\mathbf{y} = Q^\top (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$, so $y_i$ is the
coordinate of $\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}$ along $\mathbf{q}_i$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) &= (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top Q \Lambda^{-1} Q^\top (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) && \text{(spectral theorem)} \\
&= \mathbf{y}^\top \Lambda^{-1} \mathbf{y} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{y} \text{)} \\
&= \frac{y_1^2}{\lambda_1} + \frac{y_2^2}{\lambda_2} + \dots + \frac{y_d^2}{\lambda_d} && \text{(} \Lambda^{-1} \text{ is diagonal)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting this equal to $c^2$ gives

$$
\frac{y_1^2}{(c\sqrt{\lambda_1})^2} + \dots + \frac{y_d^2}{(c\sqrt{\lambda_d})^2} = 1
$$

which is the school equation of an ellipse (ellipsoid) with half-axes
$c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, in the rotated coordinates. Rotating back by $Q$ puts the
axes along the $\mathbf{q}_i$ and the center at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$.

**A bonus fact: the axes are the uncorrelated directions.** The random vector
$\mathbf{Y} = Q^\top (\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$ has covariance (by
[11.7 Linear Transformations](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md))

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}) = Q^\top \Sigma Q = Q^\top Q \Lambda Q^\top Q = \Lambda
$$

So along the axes the coordinates are uncorrelated, and the variance along
$\mathbf{q}_i$ is exactly $\lambda_i$. This is why the half-length is
$c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$: $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ is the standard deviation in that
direction. Finding these directions from data is what PCA does.

### What the Shape Tells You

| Covariance matrix | Contours |
|---|---|
| $\Sigma = \sigma^2 I$ | circles of radius $c\sigma$ |
| diagonal, $\sigma_1 \gt \sigma_2$ | ellipses lined up with the axes, long along $x_1$ |
| equal variances, $\rho \gt 0$ | ellipses tilted at 45 degrees, long along $(1, 1)$ |
| equal variances, $\rho \lt 0$ | ellipses tilted at -45 degrees, long along $(1, -1)$ |
| $\rho \to \pm 1$ | the short axis shrinks to 0; the ellipse becomes a line |

In two dimensions, the fraction of probability inside the contour with value
$c$ is $1 - e^{-c^2/2}$. For $c = 1$ that is only about 39%, not 68% as in
one dimension. Topic [12.3 Mahalanobis Distance](12.3-Mahalanobis-Distance.md)
proves this.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Equal Variances, Positive Correlation

**Problem.** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with
$\Sigma_{11} = \Sigma_{22} = 2$ and $\Sigma_{12} = 1$. Describe the contour
with $c = 1$.

**Solution.**

1. Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 - (2 + 2)\lambda + (2 \cdot 2 - 1^2) = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0$,
   so $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.
2. Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 3$: $(2 - 3) q_1 + 1 \cdot q_2 = 0$, so
   $q_2 = q_1$. Unit length gives $\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)$.
3. Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 1$: $(2 - 1) q_1 + q_2 = 0$, so
   $q_2 = -q_1$, giving $\mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1)$.
4. Check $\Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^\top$:

$$
\frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \cr 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \cr 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \cr 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \cr 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

5. Half-lengths: $1 \cdot \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$ along $(1, 1)$, and
   $1 \cdot \sqrt{1} = 1$ along $(1, -1)$.

**Answer.** An ellipse centered at the origin, tilted at 45 degrees, with
half-length about $1.73$ along the line $x_2 = x_1$ and $1$ along the line
$x_2 = -x_1$.

**Check.** This agrees with Example 2 of
[12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md): the point $(1, 1)$ lies on the
long axis, inside the $c = 1$ ellipse (its quadratic form was $\frac{2}{3} \lt 1$),
while $(1, -1)$ lies on the short axis, outside it (quadratic form $2 \gt 1$).

### Example 2: Unequal Variances, a 30-Degree Tilt

**Problem.** A robot's position error (in cm) is
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with the matrix below. In which direction is
the robot most uncertain, and how big is its "2-sigma" ellipse ($c = 2$)?

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2\sqrt{3} \cr 2\sqrt{3} & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Solution.**

1. Trace and determinant: $a + d = 10$ and $ad - b^2 = 21 - 12 = 9$.
2. Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 - 10\lambda + 9 = (\lambda - 9)(\lambda - 1) = 0$, so
   $\lambda_1 = 9$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.
3. Eigenvector for 9: $(7 - 9) q_1 + 2\sqrt{3} q_2 = 0$, so $q_2 = q_1 / \sqrt{3}$.
   The unit vector is $\mathbf{q}_1 = (\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, which
   points at 30 degrees above the $x_1$-axis.
4. Eigenvector for 1: at right angles, $\mathbf{q}_2 = (-\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})$,
   at 120 degrees.
5. Check: $\Sigma \mathbf{q}_1 = (7 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 2\sqrt{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2},\ 2\sqrt{3} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{2}) = (\frac{9\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{9}{2}) = 9 \mathbf{q}_1$.
6. Half-lengths for $c = 2$: $2\sqrt{9} = 6$ cm along $\mathbf{q}_1$ and
   $2\sqrt{1} = 2$ cm along $\mathbf{q}_2$.

**Answer.** The robot is most uncertain along the direction 30 degrees above
the $x_1$-axis. Its 2-sigma ellipse is 12 cm long and 4 cm wide (half-lengths
6 cm and 2 cm), and it contains a fraction $1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865$ of the
probability.

**Check.** The SD along $x_1$ alone is $\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$, less than
the SD of 3 along $\mathbf{q}_1$. That must be so: no direction can have more
variance than the top eigenvector. The simulation finds the 30-degree angle
from the data.

## Simulate It

Sample the robot error of Example 2, measure the variance along each axis,
and find the long-axis direction from the data alone.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 1_000_000

Sigma = np.array([[7.0, 2 * np.sqrt(3)],
                  [2 * np.sqrt(3), 3.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal([0.0, 0.0], Sigma, size=n_samples)

# Exact eigenvectors: q1 at 30 degrees (eigenvalue 9), q2 at 120 degrees (eigenvalue 1).
q1 = np.array([np.cos(np.pi / 6), np.sin(np.pi / 6)])
q2 = np.array([-np.sin(np.pi / 6), np.cos(np.pi / 6)])
y1, y2 = X @ q1, X @ q2          # coordinates along the ellipse axes

print(f"Var along q1:  {y1.var():.3f}   (exact 9)")
print(f"Var along q2:  {y2.var():.3f}   (exact 1)")
print(f"Cov(y1, y2):   {np.mean(y1 * y2):.3f}   (exact 0)")

# Direction of the long axis, found from the data alone.
eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(np.cov(X.T))
v = eigvecs[:, 1]                # eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue
angle = np.degrees(np.arctan(v[1] / v[0]))
print(f"Long-axis angle from data: {angle:.2f} degrees   (exact 30)")

# Fraction of points inside the c = 1 ellipse: y1^2/9 + y2^2/1 <= 1.
inside = (y1**2 / 9 + y2**2 / 1) <= 1
print(f"Inside c = 1 ellipse: {inside.mean():.4f}   (exact {1 - np.exp(-0.5):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Var along q1:  8.978   (exact 9)
Var along q2:  1.002   (exact 1)
Cov(y1, y2):   0.001   (exact 0)
Long-axis angle from data: 30.01 degrees   (exact 30)
Inside c = 1 ellipse: 0.3937   (exact 0.3935)
```

The data "knows" its own ellipse: the eigenvectors of the sample covariance
point the same way as the true ones.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using SDs of $X_1$ and $X_2$ as the axis lengths.** That is right only
  when $\Sigma$ is diagonal. With correlation, the axes are the eigenvectors,
  and their lengths come from the eigenvalues.
- **Forgetting the square root.** Half-lengths are $c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, not
  $c\lambda_i$. Eigenvalues are variances; axis lengths are like SDs.
- **Thinking the 1-sigma ellipse holds 68%.** In two dimensions it holds
  only about 39%. You need $c \approx 2.45$ for 95% (see
  [12.3](12.3-Mahalanobis-Distance.md)).
- **Reading the tilt as the correlation.** The angle depends on the variances
  too. In Example 2 the correlation is $\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{7}\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.76$,
  but the tilt is 30 degrees, not 45. Only for equal variances is the tilt
  always 45 degrees.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** PCA finds the eigenvectors of the data's
  covariance matrix and keeps the directions with the biggest eigenvalues, to
  compress data with little loss.
- **Robotics and GPS:** position uncertainty is drawn as an error ellipse;
  the Kalman filter updates its center and shape at every step.
- **Computer science:** Gaussian mixture clustering draws each cluster as an
  ellipse, so it can find long, tilted clusters that k-means misses.
- **Statistics:** confidence ellipses for two estimated numbers at once.
- **Everyday life:** the scatter of shots on a target, or of arrows, is
  usually an ellipse, not a circle; its long axis shows the main source of
  error.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with
$\Sigma$ diagonal, $\Sigma_{11} = 9$ and $\Sigma_{22} = 1$. Describe the
contour with $c = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A diagonal $\Sigma$ has eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ with eigenvalues 9
and 1. The contour is the ellipse $\frac{x_1^2}{9} + x_2^2 = 1$: lined up with
the axes, half-length $\sqrt{9} = 3$ along $x_1$ and $1$ along $x_2$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** What do the contours look like when $\Sigma = 4I$ in two
dimensions?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 4. The contours are circles
$x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 4c^2$ with radius $c\sqrt{4} = 2c$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For $\Sigma_{11} = \Sigma_{22} = 5$ and $\Sigma_{12} = 3$, find
the eigenvalues, the eigenvectors, and the half-lengths of the contour with
$c = 2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\lambda^2 - 10\lambda + (25 - 9) = \lambda^2 - 10\lambda + 16 = (\lambda - 8)(\lambda - 2) = 0$$

So $\lambda_1 = 8$ with eigenvector $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)$, and
$\lambda_2 = 2$ with eigenvector $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1)$. Half-lengths:
$2\sqrt{8} \approx 5.66$ along $(1, 1)$ and $2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ along $(1, -1)$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that for equal variances $\sigma^2$ and correlation
$\rho$, the eigenvalues are $\sigma^2(1 + \rho)$ and $\sigma^2(1 - \rho)$.
What happens to the ellipse when $\rho \to 1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $a = d = \sigma^2$ and $b = \rho\sigma^2$. Check the vector $(1, 1)$:
$\Sigma (1, 1)^\top = (\sigma^2 + \rho\sigma^2, \rho\sigma^2 + \sigma^2)^\top = \sigma^2(1 + \rho)(1, 1)^\top$.
In the same way $\Sigma(1, -1)^\top = \sigma^2(1 - \rho)(1, -1)^\top$. As
$\rho \to 1$ the small eigenvalue $\sigma^2(1 - \rho) \to 0$, so the short
half-axis $c\sigma\sqrt{1 - \rho}$ shrinks to 0 and the ellipse flattens onto
the line $x_2 = x_1$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $\mathbf{u}$ be any unit vector. Prove that the variance of
$\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{X}$ is at most $\lambda_1$, with equality when
$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{q}_1$. (So the long axis is the direction of largest
spread.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $\mathbf{u}$ in the eigenvector basis: $\mathbf{u} = a_1 \mathbf{q}_1 + \dots + a_d \mathbf{q}_d$
with $a_1^2 + \dots + a_d^2 = 1$ (the $\mathbf{q}_i$ are unit length and at
right angles). Using $\Sigma \mathbf{q}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{q}_i$ and
$\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{q}_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{u}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{u} = \lambda_1 a_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_d a_d^2 \le \lambda_1 (a_1^2 + \dots + a_d^2) = \lambda_1$$

Equality holds when $a_1 = 1$ and the other $a_i = 0$, that is, $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{q}_1$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Using the $\Sigma$ of Problem 3, check by simulation that
the $c = 2$ ellipse holds a fraction $1 - e^{-2}$ of the probability.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
Sigma = np.array([[5.0, 3.0], [3.0, 5.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal([0.0, 0.0], Sigma, size=1_000_000)
y1 = X @ np.array([1, 1]) / np.sqrt(2)     # along q1, eigenvalue 8
y2 = X @ np.array([1, -1]) / np.sqrt(2)    # along q2, eigenvalue 2
inside = y1**2 / 8 + y2**2 / 2 <= 2**2     # the c = 2 ellipse
print(f"Simulation: {inside.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact:      {1 - np.exp(-2):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Simulation: 0.8651
Exact:      0.8647
```

</details>

## Summary

- Contours of a Gaussian density are ellipses (ellipsoids) centered at
  $\boldsymbol{\mu}$.
- Their axes point along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$; the half-length along
  $\mathbf{q}_i$ is $c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
- Along the eigenvector directions the coordinates are uncorrelated, with
  variances equal to the eigenvalues. This is the idea of PCA.
- No correlation and equal variances give circles; correlation tilts the
  ellipse; correlation near $\pm 1$ flattens it into a line.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^\top \implies \text{contour axes along } \mathbf{q}_i \text{ with half-lengths } c\sqrt{\lambda_i}}
$$
