# 12.5 Affine Transformations

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](README.md) / 12.5 Affine Transformations

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [12.4 Standard Normal Vectors and Cholesky](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)

> **Big idea:** If $\mathbf{X}$ is Gaussian, then any linear map plus a shift, $A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}$, is again Gaussian, with mean $A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$ and covariance $A\Sigma A^\top$.

---

## Why It Matters

Sums, differences, averages, portfolios, unit changes and rotations are all
linear maps. This topic says they never leave the Gaussian family, and it
gives the new mean and covariance by a simple formula. So questions like
"what is the chance my portfolio loses more than 2% today?" or "what is the
chance $X_1 \gt X_2$?" get exact answers with one normal table lookup. The
next topics (marginals, conditionals, sums) are all special cases of this
one fact.

## Intuition

In [11.7 Linear Transformations](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md)
you saw that, for **any** random vector, $A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}$ has mean
$A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$ and covariance $A\Sigma A^\top$. That part
is not special. What is special is the **shape**.

Add two independent uniform numbers on $[0, 1]$: the sum has a triangle
shape, not a flat one. The uniform family is not closed under adding. Add two
Gaussians that are jointly Gaussian: the sum is again a bell curve. Stretch,
rotate, shift or project a Gaussian cloud and you still get a Gaussian cloud,
just with a different ellipse. So once you know the new mean and covariance,
you know **everything** about the result.

## Picture

```text
      X ~ N(μ, Σ)                        Y = A X + b ~ N(A μ + b, A Σ A^T)

   x2                                 y2
    |       .-''-.                     |     .-'''''-.
    |     .'      '.                   |   .'         '.
    |    (    μ     )   ---- A ---->   |  (   A μ + b   )
    |     '.      .'     then + b      |   '.         .'
    |       '-..-'                     |     '-.....-'
    +--------------- x1                +------------------ y1

        Gaussian in   ---- linear map + shift ---->   Gaussian out
```

A linear map turns one ellipse-shaped Gaussian into another; only the center
and the ellipse change.

## Definition

**Two dimensions first.** Let $(X_1, X_2)$ be bivariate normal with means
$\mu_1, \mu_2$, variances $\Sigma_{11}, \Sigma_{22}$ and covariance
$\Sigma_{12}$. For numbers $a_1, a_2, b$:

$$
a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2 + b \sim \mathcal{N}\left( a_1\mu_1 + a_2\mu_2 + b, \ a_1^2 \Sigma_{11} + 2 a_1 a_2 \Sigma_{12} + a_2^2 \Sigma_{22} \right)
$$

**In words:** any weighted sum of two jointly normal variables is normal; its
variance includes the covariance term $2a_1 a_2 \Sigma_{12}$.

**The general statement.** An **affine transformation** is a linear map
followed by a shift: $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$. Let
$\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ in $d$ dimensions, let
$A$ be a $k \times d$ matrix and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^k$. Then

$$
\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, \ A\Sigma A^\top \right)
$$

where:

- $\mathbf{Y}$ has $k$ entries, one for each row of $A$; $k$ may be smaller
  than, equal to, or larger than $d$;
- $A\Sigma A^\top$ is the $k \times k$ covariance matrix of $\mathbf{Y}$.

**In words:** a linear map of a Gaussian is Gaussian; push the mean through
the map, and sandwich the covariance between $A$ and $A^\top$.

The case $k = 1$ is worth stating on its own. For a fixed vector $\mathbf{a}$:

$$
\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{a}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}, \ \mathbf{a}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{a} \right)
$$

Every linear combination of a Gaussian vector is a 1-D normal. In fact, this
property **characterizes** Gaussian vectors: many books define "jointly
Gaussian" as "every linear combination is normal".

> **Note:** If the rows of $A$ are linearly dependent (for example
> $Y_1 = X_1 + X_2$ and $Y_2 = 2X_1 + 2X_2$), then $A\Sigma A^\top$ has
> determinant 0. $\mathbf{Y}$ is still Gaussian, but all its probability
> lies on a line or plane, so it has no PDF. This is a **degenerate**
> Gaussian.

## Why It Is True

**Mean and covariance.** These hold for every distribution, by linearity
of expectation and [11.7](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md).
The work is to show that $\mathbf{Y}$ is **Gaussian**.

**Proof when A is square and invertible.** Write
$\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$ with $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$
([12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)). Then

$$
\mathbf{Y} = A(\boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}) + \mathbf{b} = (A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}) + (AL)\mathbf{Z}
$$

This is a fixed vector plus an invertible matrix times $\mathbf{Z}$. By
Step 2 of "Why It Is True" in [12.1](12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md),
it has the Gaussian PDF with covariance $(AL)(AL)^\top = A LL^\top A^\top = A\Sigma A^\top$.

**Proof for any A, using the MGF.** The **moment generating function**
(MGF) of a random vector is $M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t}) = E[e^{\mathbf{t}^\top \mathbf{X}}]$.
Like in one dimension, two random vectors with the same MGF have the same
distribution ([15.1](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md),
[15.4](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.4-Uniqueness-Theorem.md)). We use one
1-D fact: for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, $E[e^{cZ}] = e^{c^2/2}$.

First the MGF of $\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{Z}$. Let
$\mathbf{c} = L^\top \mathbf{t}$, so $\mathbf{t}^\top L\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{Z} = c_1 Z_1 + \dots + c_d Z_d$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t}) &= e^{\mathbf{t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}} E\left[ e^{c_1 Z_1} \cdots e^{c_d Z_d} \right] && \text{(split the exponent)} \\
&= e^{\mathbf{t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}} e^{c_1^2/2} \cdots e^{c_d^2/2} && \text{(independence, then the 1-D fact)} \\
&= \exp\left( \mathbf{t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu} + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{c} \right) = \exp\left( \mathbf{t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu} + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{t}^\top LL^\top \mathbf{t} \right) && \text{(since } \mathbf{c} = L^\top \mathbf{t} \text{)} \\
&= \exp\left( \mathbf{t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu} + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{t}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{t} \right) && \text{(since } LL^\top = \Sigma \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now the MGF of $\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}$, at a vector $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^k$.
Use $\mathbf{s}^\top A\mathbf{X} = (A^\top \mathbf{s})^\top \mathbf{X}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{s}) &= e^{\mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{b}} \thinspace M_{\mathbf{X}}(A^\top \mathbf{s}) && \text{(pull out the constant)} \\
&= \exp\left( \mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{s}^\top A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{s}^\top A\Sigma A^\top \mathbf{s} \right) && \text{(MGF of } \mathbf{X} \text{ at } A^\top \mathbf{s} \text{)} \\
&= \exp\left( \mathbf{s}^\top (A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}) + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{s}^\top (A\Sigma A^\top) \mathbf{s} \right) && \text{(collect terms)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the MGF of $\mathcal{N}(A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, A\Sigma A^\top)$.
By uniqueness, that is the distribution of $\mathbf{Y}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Sum, Difference, and Who Is Bigger

**Problem.** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ with
$\boldsymbol{\mu} = (1, 2)^\top$ and $\Sigma$ with rows $(4, 2)$ and $(2, 5)$.
Find the joint distribution of $S = X_1 + X_2$ and $W = X_1 - X_2$, and find
$P(X_1 \gt X_2)$.

**Solution.**

1. Write the map as a matrix: $(S, W)^\top = A\mathbf{X}$ with

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \cr 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 2 \cr 1 - 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cr -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

2. Multiply $A\Sigma$ first, then multiply by $A^\top$:

$$
A\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 + 2 & 2 + 5 \cr 4 - 2 & 2 - 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 7 \cr 2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\Sigma A^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 6 + 7 & 6 - 7 \cr 2 - 3 & 2 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 & -1 \cr -1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

3. So $W \sim \mathcal{N}(-1, 5)$. The event $X_1 \gt X_2$ is $W \gt 0$.
   Standardize with $\sigma_W = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$:

$$
P(W \gt 0) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{0 - (-1)}{\sqrt{5}} \right) = P(Z \gt 0.4472) = 1 - \Phi(0.4472) \approx 1 - 0.6726 = 0.3274
$$

**Answer.** $(S, W)$ is bivariate normal with mean $(3, -1)$, variances 13
and 5, and covariance $-1$. $P(X_1 \gt X_2) \approx 0.327$.

**Check.** By the 2-D formula, $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 4 + 2 \cdot 2 + 5 = 13$
and $\operatorname{Var}(W) = 4 - 2 \cdot 2 + 5 = 5$. Also
$\operatorname{Cov}(S, W) = \operatorname{Var}(X_1) - \operatorname{Var}(X_2) = 4 - 5 = -1$.
All three agree with the matrix. The answer is below $\frac{1}{2}$ because
$X_2$ has the bigger mean.

### Example 2: The Risk of a Portfolio

**Problem.** Two stocks have daily returns (in percent) that are bivariate
normal with means $0.05$ and $0.10$, SDs 1 and 2, and correlation $0.5$ (the
stocks of [12.4](12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md), Example 2). You
put 60% of your money in stock 1 and 40% in stock 2. What is the chance of
losing more than 2% in one day?

**Solution.**

1. The portfolio return is $P = 0.6R_1 + 0.4R_2 = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{R}$
   with $\mathbf{w} = (0.6, 0.4)$. The covariance is $0.5 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 1$,
   so $\Sigma$ has rows $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 4)$.
2. Mean: $\mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu} = 0.6 \cdot 0.05 + 0.4 \cdot 0.10 = 0.03 + 0.04 = 0.07$.
3. Variance:

$$
\mathbf{w}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{w} = 0.6^2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0.6 \cdot 0.4 \cdot 1 + 0.4^2 \cdot 4 = 0.36 + 0.48 + 0.64 = 1.48
$$

4. So $P \sim \mathcal{N}(0.07, 1.48)$, with SD $\sqrt{1.48} \approx 1.2166$. Then:

$$
P(P \lt -2) = \Phi\left( \frac{-2 - 0.07}{1.2166} \right) = \Phi(-1.7015) \approx 0.0444
$$

**Answer.** About $0.044$: a loss of more than 2% happens on about 1 trading
day in 23.

**Check.** If the stocks were uncorrelated, the variance would be
$0.36 + 0.64 = 1.00$, and the risk would be
$\Phi(-2.07) \approx 0.019$. Positive correlation adds the $0.48$ term and
more than doubles the risk. This is why diversification works best with
uncorrelated assets. The simulation in Practice 6 agrees.

## Simulate It

Check Example 1: the mean and covariance of $(S, W)$, the probability
$P(X_1 \gt X_2)$, and that $W$ really has a normal shape.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 1_000_000

mu = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
Sigma = np.array([[4.0, 2.0],
                  [2.0, 5.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=n_samples)

# Y = A X: first row is the sum, second row is the difference.
A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, -1.0]])
Y = X @ A.T
print("Mean of Y, simulated:", Y.mean(axis=0).round(3), " exact:", A @ mu)
print("Cov of Y, simulated:\n", np.cov(Y.T).round(3))
print("Cov of Y, exact A Sigma A^T:\n", A @ Sigma @ A.T)

# The difference W = X1 - X2 should be exactly N(-1, 5).
W = Y[:, 1]
print(f"P(X1 > X2): {np.mean(W > 0):.4f}   exact {1 - stats.norm.cdf(1 / np.sqrt(5)):.4f}")
# A normal shape check: about 68.27% within one SD of the mean.
print(f"P(|W + 1| < sqrt(5)): {np.mean(np.abs(W + 1) < np.sqrt(5)):.4f}   exact 0.6827")
```

Output:

```text
Mean of Y, simulated: [ 2.999 -1.002]  exact: [ 3. -1.]
Cov of Y, simulated:
 [[12.968 -0.998]
 [-0.998  5.009]]
Cov of Y, exact A Sigma A^T:
 [[13. -1.]
 [-1.  5.]]
P(X1 > X2): 0.3272   exact 0.3274
P(|W + 1| < sqrt(5)): 0.6823   exact 0.6827
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Dropping the covariance term.** $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2)$ is
  $\Sigma_{11} + \Sigma_{22}$ only when the covariance is 0. In general it is
  $\Sigma_{11} + 2\Sigma_{12} + \Sigma_{22}$, which is what $A\Sigma A^\top$
  computes for you.
- **Writing the sandwich in the wrong order.** It is $A\Sigma A^\top$, with
  $A$ on the left. $A^\top \Sigma A$ usually does not even have the right size.
- **Applying it to variables that are only marginally normal.** The rule
  needs $\mathbf{X}$ to be **jointly** Gaussian. Two normal variables that are
  not jointly normal can have a sum that is not normal at all (see
  [12.6 Marginals](12.6-Marginals.md) and [12.9 Sums of Gaussians](12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md)).
- **Applying it to non-linear maps.** $X^2$, $e^X$ or $\max(X_1, X_2)$ are not
  Gaussian. Their distributions need the methods of
  [Module 13](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/README.md).
- **Expecting a PDF in the degenerate case.** If $A\Sigma A^\top$ has
  determinant 0, $\mathbf{Y}$ lives on a line or plane and has no density.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance:** portfolio return and Value at Risk are $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{R}$
  with $\mathbf{w}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{w}$ as the variance, exactly as in
  Example 2 ([21.5 Finance and Insurance](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** a linear layer applied to Gaussian input gives
  Gaussian output; this is used to analyze weight initialization and in
  Bayesian linear regression, where the prediction $\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{w}$
  of a Gaussian weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ is Gaussian.
- **Robotics:** the "predict" step of a Kalman filter moves the state by a
  linear motion model $F$, and the uncertainty becomes $F\Sigma F^\top$ plus noise.
- **Signal processing:** filtering, averaging and rotating Gaussian noise
  leave it Gaussian, with a covariance you can compute in advance.
- **Everyday life:** changing units (Celsius to Fahrenheit, taka to dollars)
  of normally distributed data keeps it normal.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** With $\mathbf{X}$ as in Example 1, find the distribution of
$3X_1 - X_2 + 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $a_1 = 3$, $a_2 = -1$, $b = 1$:

$$E = 3 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 2 + 1 = 2, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = 9 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 \cdot (-1) \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 5 = 36 - 12 + 5 = 29$$

So $3X_1 - X_2 + 1 \sim \mathcal{N}(2, 29)$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Daily temperatures in two cities (in Celsius) are bivariate
normal with means 20 and 25, SDs 3 and 4, and correlation $0.5$. Convert
both to Fahrenheit with $F = 1.8C + 32$. Find the new means, SDs and
correlation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $A = 1.8 I$ and $\mathbf{b} = (32, 32)$. Means: $1.8 \cdot 20 + 32 = 68$ and
$1.8 \cdot 25 + 32 = 77$. SDs: $1.8 \cdot 3 = 5.4$ and $1.8 \cdot 4 = 7.2$.
Every entry of $\Sigma$ is multiplied by $1.8^2 = 3.24$, so the correlation
stays $0.5$: changing units never changes correlation.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with
variances 2 and covariance 1. Let $Y_1 = (X_1 + X_2)/\sqrt{2}$ and
$Y_2 = (X_1 - X_2)/\sqrt{2}$. Find the distribution of $(Y_1, Y_2)$. What
does it mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y_1) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2 + 2 \cdot 1 + 2) = 3, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2 - 2 \cdot 1 + 2) = 1, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(Y_1, Y_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2 - 2) = 0$$

So $(Y_1, Y_2) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \text{diag}(3, 1))$. This map is a
rotation onto the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ ([12.2](12.2-Geometry-and-Contours.md)):
in the rotated coordinates the ellipse is lined up with the axes and the
correlation is gone.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.
Use this topic to find the exact distribution of the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The vector $\mathbf{X}$ is $\mathcal{N}(\mu \mathbf{1}, \sigma^2 I)$, where
$\mathbf{1}$ is the vector of ones. The sample mean is $\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{X}$
with every entry of $\mathbf{a}$ equal to $\frac{1}{n}$:

$$E[\bar{X}_n] = n \cdot \tfrac{1}{n} \mu = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \mathbf{a}^\top (\sigma^2 I) \mathbf{a} = \sigma^2 \cdot n \cdot \tfrac{1}{n^2} = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

So $\bar{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ exactly, for every $n$, not
just approximately as in the CLT.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$ in $d$
dimensions and let $Q$ be any **orthogonal** matrix ($Q^\top Q = QQ^\top = I$,
a rotation or reflection). Prove that $Q\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$.
Why does this match the picture of a "round hill"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By this topic, $Q\mathbf{Z}$ is Gaussian with mean $Q\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$
and covariance

$$Q I Q^\top = QQ^\top = I$$

So $Q\mathbf{Z}$ has the same distribution as $\mathbf{Z}$. The density of
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$ depends only on the length $\lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert$,
and rotations do not change lengths. A round hill looks the same after any
rotation.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate one million days of the portfolio in Example 2
and estimate the chance of a loss of more than 2%.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
mu = np.array([0.05, 0.10])                    # mean daily returns, percent
Sigma = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 4.0]])
R = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=1_000_000)
w = np.array([0.6, 0.4])                       # portfolio weights
P = R @ w
exact = stats.norm.cdf((-2 - w @ mu) / np.sqrt(w @ Sigma @ w))
print(f"P(loss > 2%): simulation {np.mean(P < -2):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(loss > 2%): simulation 0.0441, exact 0.0444
```

</details>

## Summary

- Affine maps keep Gaussians Gaussian. Only the mean and the covariance
  change, and both are easy to compute.
- In 2-D: $a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2 + b$ is normal with variance
  $a_1^2\Sigma_{11} + 2a_1a_2\Sigma_{12} + a_2^2\Sigma_{22}$.
- Every linear combination $\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{X}$ of a Gaussian vector
  is a 1-D normal; this can serve as the definition of "jointly Gaussian".
- The MGF of $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ is
  $\exp(\mathbf{t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{t})$;
  it gives the cleanest proof.
- If $A\Sigma A^\top$ is singular, the result is a degenerate Gaussian with no
  PDF.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) \implies A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b} \sim \mathcal{N}(A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, \ A\Sigma A^\top)}
$$
