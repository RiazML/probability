# 14.1 Conditional Expectation Given a Value

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 14: Conditional Expectation](README.md) / 14.1 Conditional Expectation Given a Value

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/README.md)

> **Big idea:** $E[Y \mid X = x]$ is the ordinary average of $Y$, computed only over the cases where $X = x$.

---

## Why It Matters

You often know one thing and want to predict another. Given that a laptop is 2
years old, how many repairs will it need on average? Given that a user clicked
3 ads, how much will they spend on average? The answer is a **conditional
expectation**. It is the average you get after you filter the data. Every topic
in this module, and much of machine learning, is built on this one number.

## Intuition

Roll a fair die. The average roll is $3.5$. Now a friend peeks and tells you:
"the roll is even". The possible rolls are now only 2, 4 and 6, and they are
still equally likely. Their average is $(2 + 4 + 6) / 3 = 4$. If the friend says
"the roll is odd" instead, the average is $(1 + 3 + 5) / 3 = 3$.

That is all a conditional expectation is: **keep only the cases that match what
you know, then average**. Programmers already do this every day. The SQL query

```text
SELECT age, AVG(repairs) FROM laptops GROUP BY age;
```

returns, for each age, the average number of repairs among laptops of that age.
Each output row is one conditional expectation: the average of "repairs" given
"age = a".

## Picture

```text
   Joint table p(x, y)                   Keep row X = 2, rescale, average

            Y = 0   Y = 1   Y = 2  | p_X
          +-------+-------+-------+              Y = 0   Y = 1   Y = 2
   X = 1  | 0.30  | 0.15  | 0.05  | 0.50        +-------+-------+-------+
          +-------+-------+-------+     ---->   | 0.20  | 0.40  | 0.40  |
   X = 2  | 0.10  | 0.20  | 0.20  | 0.50        +-------+-------+-------+
          +-------+-------+-------+
                                     E[Y | X = 2] = 0(0.2) + 1(0.4) + 2(0.4)
                                                  = 1.2
```

To find $E[Y \mid X = 2]$, take the conditional distribution of $Y$ in row 2
(divide by the row sum $0.50$), then take its mean.

## Definition

Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables, and let $x$ be a value with $p_X(x) \gt 0$.
The **conditional expectation of $Y$ given $X = x$** is the mean of the
conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$
([10.4 Conditional Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md)).

**Discrete case:**

$$
E[Y \mid X = x] = \sum_{y} y \thinspace P(Y = y \mid X = x) = \sum_{y} y \thinspace \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_X(x)}
$$

**Continuous case** (for $f_X(x) \gt 0$):

$$
E[Y \mid X = x] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y \thinspace f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace dy
$$

where:

- $p_{X,Y}(x, y)$ is the joint PMF and $p_X(x)$ is the marginal PMF of $X$,
- $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = f_{X,Y}(x, y) / f_X(x)$ is the conditional density.

**In words:** take the slice of the joint distribution where $X = x$, rescale it
so it adds to 1, and compute its mean.

The same idea works for any event $A$ with $P(A) \gt 0$. The **conditional
expectation of $Y$ given $A$** is

$$
E[Y \mid A] = \frac{E[Y \thinspace I_A]}{P(A)}
$$

where $I_A$ is the indicator of $A$ (1 if $A$ happens, 0 if not).

**In words:** add up $Y$ only over the outcomes in $A$ (weighted by probability),
then divide by the probability of $A$.

$E[Y \mid X = x]$ is the special case $A = \lbrace X = x \rbrace$. For a function
$g$, the conditional version of LOTUS
([6.3 LOTUS](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.3-LOTUS.md)) also holds:

$$
E[g(Y) \mid X = x] = \sum_{y} g(y) \thinspace P(Y = y \mid X = x)
$$

**In words:** to average any function of $Y$ given $X = x$, weight its values by
the conditional PMF.

## Why It Is True

There is nothing deep to prove. Once $x$ is fixed, $P(Y = y \mid X = x)$ is a
genuine PMF in $y$: it is never negative and it adds to 1. So
$E[Y \mid X = x]$ is just the ordinary expectation of
[6.1 Expected Value](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.1-Expected-Value.md),
taken under a new distribution. This has a useful consequence: **every rule for
expectations also holds for conditional expectations**. For example, linearity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[aY + bZ \mid X = x] &= \sum_{y, z} (ay + bz) \thinspace P(Y = y, Z = z \mid X = x) && \text{(conditional LOTUS)} \\
&= a \sum_{y} y \thinspace P(Y = y \mid X = x) + b \sum_{z} z \thinspace P(Z = z \mid X = x) && \text{(split, sum out the other variable)} \\
&= a E[Y \mid X = x] + b E[Z \mid X = x] && \text{(definition)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The event formula comes from the same kind of calculation, for a discrete $Y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[Y \mid A] &= \sum_{y} y \thinspace P(Y = y \mid A) && \text{(mean of the conditional PMF)} \\
&= \frac{1}{P(A)} \sum_{y} y \thinspace P(\lbrace Y = y \rbrace \cap A) && \text{(conditional probability)} \\
&= \frac{E[Y \thinspace I_A]}{P(A)} && \text{(} Y I_A = Y \text{ on } A \text{, and } 0 \text{ off } A \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Laptop Repairs

**Problem.** A company has laptops that are 1 or 2 years old ($X$). The number of
repairs a laptop needs this year is $Y \in \lbrace 0, 1, 2 \rbrace$. The joint PMF
is the table in the Picture. Find $E[Y \mid X = 1]$, $E[Y \mid X = 2]$ and $E[Y]$.

**Solution.**

1. Row sums: $p_X(1) = 0.30 + 0.15 + 0.05 = 0.50$ and $p_X(2) = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.20 = 0.50$.
2. Conditional PMF in row 1: divide by $0.50$ to get $0.60, 0.30, 0.10$. Its mean:

$$
E[Y \mid X = 1] = 0(0.60) + 1(0.30) + 2(0.10) = 0 + 0.30 + 0.20 = 0.50
$$

3. Conditional PMF in row 2: $0.20, 0.40, 0.40$. Its mean:

$$
E[Y \mid X = 2] = 0(0.20) + 1(0.40) + 2(0.40) = 0 + 0.40 + 0.80 = 1.20
$$

4. Without conditioning, the column sums are $p_Y(0) = 0.40$, $p_Y(1) = 0.35$,
   $p_Y(2) = 0.25$, so:

$$
E[Y] = 0(0.40) + 1(0.35) + 2(0.25) = 0.35 + 0.50 = 0.85
$$

**Answer.** A 1-year-old laptop needs $0.5$ repairs on average, a 2-year-old one
needs $1.2$, and a random laptop needs $0.85$.

**Check.** $0.85$ lies between $0.5$ and $1.2$. In fact it is exactly their
average weighted by $p_X$: $0.5(0.5) + 0.5(1.2) = 0.85$. This is no accident. It is
the law of total expectation of [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).

### Example 2: The First Die Given the Sum

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice, $X_1$ and $X_2$, and let $S = X_1 + X_2$. Find
$E[X_1 \mid S = 8]$. Then find a formula for $E[X_1 \mid S = s]$.

**Solution.**

1. The event $S = 8$ contains 5 equally likely pairs:
   $(2, 6), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 3), (6, 2)$.
2. Given $S = 8$, each pair has conditional probability $\frac{1}{5}$. The first
   die shows $2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ in these pairs:

$$
E[X_1 \mid S = 8] = \frac{2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{5} = \frac{20}{5} = 4
$$

3. For a general $s$, use symmetry. Given $S = s$, the pairs $(a, b)$ and
   $(b, a)$ are both possible and equally likely, so $X_1$ and $X_2$ have the same
   conditional distribution. By linearity:

$$
E[X_1 \mid S = s] + E[X_2 \mid S = s] = E[X_1 + X_2 \mid S = s] = s
$$

4. The two terms on the left are equal, so each one is $\frac{s}{2}$.

**Answer.** $E[X_1 \mid S = 8] = 4$, and in general $E[X_1 \mid S = s] = \frac{s}{2}$.

**Check.** For $s = 2$ the only pair is $(1, 1)$, so $X_1 = 1 = \frac{2}{2}$. For
$s = 12$ it is $(6, 6)$, so $X_1 = 6 = \frac{12}{2}$. The simulation below checks
every $s$.

### Example 3: A Random Position in a Buffer

**Problem.** A program picks a buffer size $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ (in MB,
uniform on $[0, 1]$), then a position $Y$ inside it: given $X = x$,
$Y \sim \text{Unif}(0, x)$. This is Example 3 of
[10.4](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.4-Conditional-Distributions.md). Find
$E[Y \mid X = x]$ and $E[Y^2 \mid X = x]$.

**Solution.**

1. Given $X = x$, the conditional density is $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{x}$ for $0 \lt y \lt x$.
2. Integrate:

$$
E[Y \mid X = x] = \int_0^x y \cdot \frac{1}{x} \thinspace dy = \frac{1}{x} \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} = \frac{x}{2}
$$

3. For the second moment, use conditional LOTUS with $g(y) = y^2$:

$$
E[Y^2 \mid X = x] = \int_0^x y^2 \cdot \frac{1}{x} \thinspace dy = \frac{1}{x} \cdot \frac{x^3}{3} = \frac{x^2}{3}
$$

**Answer.** $E[Y \mid X = x] = \frac{x}{2}$ and $E[Y^2 \mid X = x] = \frac{x^2}{3}$.

**Check.** The mean of a uniform interval is its midpoint, and the midpoint of
$(0, x)$ is $\frac{x}{2}$. For $x = 1$ we get $E[Y^2 \mid X = 1] = \frac{1}{3}$, the
known second moment of $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$.

### Example 4: Conditioning on an Event

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. Find $E[\text{roll} \mid \text{roll} \ge 4]$ with the
formula $E[Y \mid A] = E[Y I_A] / P(A)$.

**Solution.**

1. $P(A) = P(\text{roll} \ge 4) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. $Y I_A$ is the roll when it is 4, 5 or 6, and 0 otherwise:

$$
E[Y I_A] = \frac{0 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = \frac{15}{6} = 2.5
$$

3. Divide:

$$
E[Y \mid A] = \frac{2.5}{0.5} = 5
$$

**Answer.** $E[\text{roll} \mid \text{roll} \ge 4] = 5$.

**Check.** Directly, the rolls 4, 5, 6 are equally likely and their average is 5.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2 with a "group by" average: for each sum $s$, it averages
the first die over the rolls where $S = s$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Two fair dice and their sum.
x1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
x2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
s = x1 + x2

# E[X1 | S = s]: keep only the rolls with S = s, then average X1.
for value in range(2, 13):
    estimate = x1[s == value].mean()
    print(f"E[X1 | S = {value:2d}]: simulation {estimate:.4f}, exact {value / 2:.4f}")
```

Output:

## Common Mistakes

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** $E[Y \mid X = x]$ uses the conditional PMF,
  so you must divide by $p_X(x)$. The sum $\sum_y y \thinspace p_{X,Y}(x, y)$ alone
  is too small: it is $E[Y I_A]$ with $A = \lbrace X = x \rbrace$.
- **Swapping the roles.** $E[Y \mid X = x]$ averages $Y$ over a row. $E[X \mid Y = y]$
  averages $X$ over a column. They are different numbers in general.
- **Thinking $E[Y \mid X = x]$ must be a possible value of $Y$.** In Example 1 it is
  $0.5$ repairs. Like any mean, it can fall between the possible values.
- **Forgetting that the support depends on $x$.** In Example 3 the integral runs
  from 0 to $x$, not from 0 to 1.
- **Using the event formula for a zero-probability event.** For a continuous $X$,
  $P(X = x) = 0$, so use the conditional density instead.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a regression model tries to learn
  $E[Y \mid X = x]$, the average target for input $x$
  ([14.5](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)).
- **Computer science:** "average response time given the server load" and
  "average query cost given the table size" are conditional expectations.
  Any `GROUP BY ... AVG` query estimates them from data.
- **Insurance:** the expected claim given the driver's age sets the price for that
  age group.
- **Medicine:** the expected survival time given the stage of a disease.
- **Everyday life:** "given that the bus is already 10 minutes late, how much
  longer should I expect to wait?"

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** For the laptop table in the Picture, find $E[Y^2 \mid X = 2]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The conditional PMF in row 2 is $0.20, 0.40, 0.40$:

$$E[Y^2 \mid X = 2] = 0^2(0.20) + 1^2(0.40) + 2^2(0.40) = 0.40 + 1.60 = 2.00$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Draw one card from a standard deck. Count an ace as 1 and J, Q, K as
11, 12, 13. Find the expected value given that the card is a face card (J, Q or
K), and given that it is not a face card.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Given a face card, the values 11, 12, 13 are equally likely, so the mean is 12. Given not a face card, the values 1 to 10 are equally likely (4 cards each):

$$E[\text{value} \mid \text{not face}] = \frac{1 + 2 + \dots + 10}{10} = \frac{55}{10} = 5.5$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Roll two fair dice. Let $M$ be the larger of the two. Find
$E[M \mid X_1 = 2]$, where $X_1$ is the first die.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Given $X_1 = 2$, the second die is still uniform on 1 to 6. The maximum is 2 when the second die is 1 or 2, and it equals the second die otherwise:

$$E[M \mid X_1 = 2] = \frac{2 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = \frac{22}{6} = \frac{11}{3} \approx 3.667$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Let $f(x, y) = x + y$ on the unit square. Its marginal is
$f_X(x) = x + \frac{1}{2}$. Find $E[Y \mid X = x]$, and evaluate it at $x = 0$ and
at $x = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[Y \mid X = x] = \int_0^1 y \thinspace \frac{x + y}{x + \frac{1}{2}} \thinspace dy = \frac{\frac{x}{2} + \frac{1}{3}}{x + \frac{1}{2}} = \frac{3x + 2}{6x + 3}$$

At $x = 0$ this is $\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$. At $x = 1$ it is $\frac{5}{9} \approx 0.556$. A larger $x$ makes the slice flatter, so its mean moves toward the middle, $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the rate (mean
$\frac{1}{\lambda}$). Show that $E[T \mid T \gt a] = a + \frac{1}{\lambda}$ for
every $a \ge 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the memoryless property ([9.3 Exponential](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)), given $T \gt a$ the extra time $T - a$ is again $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. So, by linearity of conditional expectation:

$$E[T \mid T \gt a] = a + E[T - a \mid T \gt a] = a + \frac{1}{\lambda}$$

You can also use the event formula. Here $P(T \gt a) = e^{-\lambda a}$, and integration by parts gives $E[T \thinspace I_A] = \int_a^{\infty} t \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \thinspace dt = (a + \frac{1}{\lambda}) e^{-\lambda a}$ for $A = \lbrace T \gt a \rbrace$. Dividing gives the same answer.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Example 1 by simulation: draw pairs $(X, Y)$ from the

## Summary

- $E[Y \mid X = x]$ is the mean of the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$.
- To compute it: keep the row (or slice) for $X = x$, rescale it to total 1, average.
- It is an ordinary expectation under a new distribution, so linearity and LOTUS
  still work.
- For an event, $E[Y \mid A] = E[Y I_A] / P(A)$.
- In data, it is a "group by, then average" query.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[Y \mid X = x] = \sum_{y} y \thinspace P(Y = y \mid X = x)}
$$
