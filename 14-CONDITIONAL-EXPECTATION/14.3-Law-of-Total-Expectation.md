# 14.3 Law of Total Expectation

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 14: Conditional Expectation](README.md) / 14.3 Law of Total Expectation

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [14.2 Conditional Expectation as a Random Variable](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md)

> **Big idea:** The overall average equals the average of the group averages, each weighted by how likely its group is: $E[E[Y \mid X]] = E[Y]$.

---

## Why It Matters

Many expectations are hard to compute directly but easy once you split into
cases. How many flips until you see two heads in a row? What is the average
response time of a server that sometimes hits its cache and sometimes misses?
The **law of total expectation** says: condition on something that makes the
problem easy, solve each case, then average the answers. It is the expectation
version of the law of total probability
([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)),
and it powers random sums ([14.7](14.7-Random-Sums.md)), Markov chains and
reinforcement learning.

## Intuition

A course has two sections. Section A has 30 students with an average exam score
of 70. Section B has 20 students with an average of 80. What is the average over
all 50 students?

It is **not** $\frac{70 + 80}{2} = 75$, because section A is bigger. Add up all the
points and divide by 50:

$$
\frac{30 \times 70 + 20 \times 80}{50} = \frac{2100 + 1600}{50} = \frac{3700}{50} = 74
$$

Look at it another way: a random student is in A with probability $\frac{30}{50} = 0.6$
and in B with probability $0.4$. The overall average is $0.6 \times 70 + 0.4 \times 80 = 42 + 32 = 74$.
That is the whole law: **the average of the group averages, weighted by the group
probabilities, is the overall average**.

In the language of [14.2](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md):
$E[\text{score} \mid \text{section}]$ is a random variable that is 70 with
probability $0.6$ and 80 with probability $0.4$. Its mean is 74, the mean score.

## Picture

```text
                      E[Y | X = x]       weight p_X(x)     product
                    +---------------+
          +- 0.6 -->| Section A: 70 |    0.6           0.6 x 70 = 42
  random -+         +---------------+
  student |         +---------------+
          +- 0.4 -->| Section B: 80 |    0.4           0.4 x 80 = 32
                    +---------------+
                                                       ------------
                                                E[Y] =  42 + 32 = 74
```

Go down each branch to its group average, multiply by the branch probability,
and add.

## Definition

The **law of total expectation** (also called the **tower property**, or
**Adam's law**) says that for any random variables $X$ and $Y$ with $E[\lvert Y \rvert]$ finite:

$$
E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] = E[Y]
$$

**In words:** the average of the conditional average is the plain average.

Written out, for a discrete $X$ and for a continuous $X$:

$$
E[Y] = \sum_{x} E[Y \mid X = x] \thinspace p_X(x), \qquad E[Y] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} E[Y \mid X = x] \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx
$$

**In words:** find the average of $Y$ in each case, then take a weighted average of
these answers, with the probabilities of the cases as weights.

The same holds for a **partition** $A_1, \dots, A_n$ of the sample space (events
that do not overlap and together cover everything,
[1.7 Partitions](../01-SETS-SAMPLE-SPACES-AND-EVENTS/1.7-Partitions.md)), each
with $P(A_i) \gt 0$:

$$
E[Y] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[Y \mid A_i] \thinspace P(A_i)
$$

**In words:** split the world into cases, average inside each case, and weight by
the chance of each case.

> **Note:** A more general form says $E\big[E[Y \mid X, Z] \mid X\big] = E[Y \mid X]$:
> averaging first with more information and then with less is the same as averaging
> once with less. The name "tower" comes from this stacking.

## Why It Is True

Here is the proof for discrete $X$ and $Y$. Write $g(x) = E[Y \mid X = x]$, so that
$E[Y \mid X] = g(X)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] &= E[g(X)] = \sum_{x} g(x) \thinspace p_X(x) && \text{(LOTUS for } g(X) \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{x} \Big( \sum_{y} y \thinspace \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_X(x)} \Big) p_X(x) && \text{(definition of } g(x) \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{x} \sum_{y} y \thinspace p_{X,Y}(x, y) && \text{(} p_X(x) \text{ cancels)} \\
&= \sum_{y} y \sum_{x} p_{X,Y}(x, y) && \text{(swap the order of the sums)} \\
&= \sum_{y} y \thinspace p_Y(y) = E[Y] && \text{(the inner sum is the marginal of } Y \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The continuous proof is the same, with integrals in place of sums and
$f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) f_X(x) = f_{X,Y}(x, y)$ in place of the cancellation. The
partition form is the same proof with $X$ = "which $A_i$ happened".

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Die, Two Ways

**Problem.** Roll a fair die; $Y$ is the roll. Check the law with
$X$ = "even or odd" and with $W$ = "high (4 to 6) or low (1 to 3)".

**Solution.**

1. From [14.2](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md), $E[Y \mid X]$ is 3 (odd) or 4 (even), each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$
E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 3 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 = 1.5 + 2 = 3.5
$$

2. $E[Y \mid W]$ is 2 (low) or 5 (high), each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$
E\big[E[Y \mid W]\big] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 5 = 1 + 2.5 = 3.5
$$

**Answer.** Both give $3.5 = E[Y]$.

**Check.** The two conditional expectations are different random variables, but
both have the same mean. Whatever you condition on, the average of the averages
is always $E[Y]$.

### Example 2: Rolling Until a Six (First-Step Analysis)

**Problem.** You roll a fair die until the first 6. Let $N$ be the number of rolls.
Find $E[N]$ without summing a series.

**Solution.**

1. Condition on the first roll. Let $A$ = "the first roll is 6", so $P(A) = \frac{1}{6}$ and $P(A^c) = \frac{5}{6}$.
2. If the first roll is 6, we stop: $E[N \mid A] = 1$.
3. If not, one roll is used up and the game **starts over**: the future rolls do not
   remember the past. So $E[N \mid A^c] = 1 + E[N]$.
4. Use the law with the partition $A, A^c$. Write $m = E[N]$:

$$
m = \frac{1}{6} \cdot 1 + \frac{5}{6} (1 + m) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{5}{6} + \frac{5}{6} m = 1 + \frac{5}{6} m
$$

5. Solve: $m - \frac{5}{6} m = 1$, so $\frac{1}{6} m = 1$ and $m = 6$.

**Answer.** $E[N] = 6$ rolls.

**Check.** $N \sim \text{Geom}(\frac{1}{6})$ (number of trials until the first
success), whose mean is $\frac{1}{p} = 6$
([7.3 Geometric](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md)). Conditioning on
the first step and solving an equation is called **first-step analysis**.

### Example 3: Waiting for HH Versus HT

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin again and again. On average, how many flips do you
need until you first see two heads in a row (HH)? How many until you first see a
head followed by a tail (HT)?

**Solution.** Both patterns have probability $\frac{1}{4}$ at any given pair of flips,
so many people guess the same answer. Use first-step analysis with **states**
that remember the useful part of the past.

**Waiting for HT.**

1. State $S$: no progress (start, or the last flip was T). State $H$: the last
   flip was H. Let $a$ and $b$ be the expected number of **further** flips to
   finish from $S$ and from $H$.
2. From $S$, flip once. Heads (probability $\frac{1}{2}$) moves us to $H$; tails keeps us
   in $S$:

$$
a = 1 + \frac{1}{2} b + \frac{1}{2} a
$$

3. From $H$, flip once. Tails finishes HT. Heads keeps us in $H$, because the newest
   flip is again an H that can start HT:

$$
b = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \frac{1}{2} b
$$

4. The second equation gives $\frac{1}{2} b = 1$, so $b = 2$. Then the first gives
   $\frac{1}{2} a = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 2$, so $a = 4$.

**Waiting for HH.**

1. Same states, new letters $a$ and $b$. From $S$, it is the same as before:

$$
a = 1 + \frac{1}{2} b + \frac{1}{2} a
$$

2. From $H$, heads finishes HH. But tails sends us **all the way back** to $S$:

$$
b = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \frac{1}{2} a
$$

3. The first equation gives $\frac{1}{2} a = 1 + \frac{1}{2} b$, so $a = 2 + b$. Put
   $b = 1 + \frac{1}{2} a$ into it: $a = 2 + 1 + \frac{1}{2} a$, so $\frac{1}{2} a = 3$
   and $a = 6$.

**Answer.** On average you need **6 flips for HH** but only **4 flips for HT**.

**Check.** The difference is what a failure costs. While waiting for HT, once you
have an H, a "wrong" flip (another H) keeps your progress. While waiting for HH,
a wrong flip (a T) destroys it. HT also has a simple direct check: wait for the
first H (mean 2 flips), then for the first T after it (mean 2 more), total 4. The
simulation below confirms both numbers.

### Example 4: A Coin With an Unknown Bias

**Problem.** A machine makes coins whose chance of heads $P$ is random, uniform on
$[0, 1]$. You take one coin and flip it 10 times. Let $Y$ be the number of heads.
Find $E[Y]$.

**Solution.**

1. Given $P = p$, the flips are independent with success chance $p$, so
   $Y \sim \text{Bin}(10, p)$ and $E[Y \mid P = p] = 10p$. So $E[Y \mid P] = 10P$.
2. Apply the law:

$$
E[Y] = E\big[E[Y \mid P]\big] = E[10P] = 10 \cdot E[P] = 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 5
$$

**Answer.** $E[Y] = 5$.

**Check.** The distribution of $Y$ is not $\text{Bin}(10, \frac{1}{2})$: it is much
more spread out ([14.4](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md) computes how much). But
its mean is still 5, by symmetry between heads and tails.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 3. Each row is one long run of flips; it finds where HH and
HT first finish.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# 100 flips per run is plenty: missing HH in 100 flips is extremely unlikely.
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(n_trials, 100))   # 1 = heads, 0 = tails
first, second = flips[:, :-1], flips[:, 1:]

hh = (first == 1) & (second == 1)
ht = (first == 1) & (second == 0)

# argmax gives the first pair position (0-based); the pattern ends at flip index + 2.
n_hh = hh.argmax(axis=1) + 2
n_ht = ht.argmax(axis=1) + 2

print(f"Runs that never saw HH: {np.sum(~hh.any(axis=1))}")
print(f"E[flips until HH]: simulation {n_hh.mean():.4f}, exact 6")
print(f"E[flips until HT]: simulation {n_ht.mean():.4f}, exact 4")
```

Output:

```text
Runs that never saw HH: 0
E[flips until HH]: simulation 6.0025, exact 6
E[flips until HT]: simulation 3.9994, exact 4
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Averaging the group averages without weights.** In the Intuition, $\frac{70 + 80}{2} = 75$
  is wrong. Each group average must be weighted by the probability of its group.
- **Using a partition that is not a partition.** The cases must not overlap and must
  cover every outcome. Otherwise some outcomes are counted twice or not at all.
- **Forgetting the step you just used.** In first-step analysis, $E[N \mid A^c] = 1 + E[N]$,
  not $E[N]$. The 1 counts the flip you already made.
- **Assuming the game "starts over" when it does not.** After HT fails with a second H,
  you are not back at the start. Choose states that remember exactly what matters.
- **Thinking equal pattern probabilities mean equal waiting times.** HH and HT both
  have probability $\frac{1}{4}$ per pair, yet their mean waiting times are 6 and 4,
  because HH can overlap with itself.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the **Bellman equation** of reinforcement learning,
  $V(s) = E[R + \gamma V(S') \mid S = s]$, is the law of total expectation applied to
  the first step, exactly like Example 2.
- **Computer science:** the average running time of randomized quicksort is found by
  conditioning on the pivot. The average cost of a cache is
  $P(\text{hit}) \cdot \text{hit time} + P(\text{miss}) \cdot \text{miss time}$.
- **Markov chains:** expected hitting times and absorption times are found by
  first-step analysis
  ([18.3.7 Absorbing Chains](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.7-Absorbing-Chains.md)).
- **Statistics:** the overall mean of stratified data is the weighted mean of the
  stratum means.
- **Everyday life:** "average delivery time" across couriers is the share of each
  courier times its average time, summed.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A factory has three machines. They make 50%, 30% and 20% of all
items. The average number of defects per batch is 1, 2 and 5 for the three machines.
What is the average number of defects per batch overall?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[Y] = 0.5 \times 1 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 5 = 0.5 + 0.6 + 1.0 = 2.1$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You are told that $E[Y \mid X] = 2X + 1$ and $E[X] = 3$. Find $E[Y]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the law and linearity:

$$E[Y] = E[2X + 1] = 2E[X] + 1 = 2 \times 3 + 1 = 7$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** You roll a fair die until you get two sixes in a row. Find the
expected number of rolls.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $a$ be the expected number of further rolls from the start (or after a non-six), and $b$ from "the last roll was a six". A non-six always sends you back to the start:

$$a = 1 + \tfrac{5}{6} a + \tfrac{1}{6} b, \qquad b = 1 + \tfrac{5}{6} a + \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot 0$$

Put $b = 1 + \frac{5}{6} a$ into the first equation: $a = 1 + \frac{5}{6} a + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{5}{36} a$. So $a (1 - \frac{35}{36}) = \frac{7}{6}$, which gives

$$a = \tfrac{7}{6} \times 36 = 42$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A program reads from a cache with probability $0.9$ (time 2 ms on
average) and from disk otherwise (time 50 ms on average). Find the average time per
read. What hit rate would make the average 5 ms?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[T] = 0.9 \times 2 + 0.1 \times 50 = 1.8 + 5 = 6.8 \text{ ms}$$

For hit rate $h$: $2h + 50(1 - h) = 5$, so $50 - 48h = 5$ and $h = \frac{45}{48} = 0.9375$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove the law of total expectation for continuous $X$ and $Y$ with a
joint density.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $g(x) = \int y \thinspace f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace dy$. Using LOTUS for $g(X)$, then $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) f_X(x) = f_{X,Y}(x, y)$, then swapping the order of integration:

$$E[g(X)] = \int \Big( \int y \thinspace f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \thinspace dy \Big) f_X(x) \thinspace dx = \int \int y \thinspace f_{X,Y}(x, y) \thinspace dx \thinspace dy = \int y \thinspace f_Y(y) \thinspace dy = E[Y]$$

The inner integral over $x$ gives the marginal density $f_Y(y)$. The swap is allowed because $E[\lvert Y \rvert]$ is finite.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 20_000
counts = np.empty(n_trials)
for t in range(n_trials):
    rolls, last_six = 0, False
    while True:
        rolls += 1
        six = rng.integers(1, 7) == 6
        if six and last_six:
            break
        last_six = six
    counts[t] = rolls
print(f"E[rolls until two sixes in a row]: simulation {counts.mean():.2f}, exact 42")
```

Output:

```text
E[rolls until two sixes in a row]: simulation 42.16, exact 42
```

</details>

## Summary

- The overall average is the weighted average of the conditional averages.
- In symbols: $E[E[Y \mid X]] = E[Y]$, or $E[Y] = \sum_x E[Y \mid X = x] \thinspace p_X(x)$.
- Pick something to condition on that makes each case easy.
- **First-step analysis:** condition on the first step, write an equation for the
  unknown mean, and solve it.
- Waiting for HH takes 6 flips on average, but HT only 4, because a failure costs
  more for HH.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] = E[Y]}
$$
