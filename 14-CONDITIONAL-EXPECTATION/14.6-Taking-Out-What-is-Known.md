# 14.6 Taking Out What is Known

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 14: Conditional Expectation](README.md) / 14.6 Taking Out What is Known

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [14.5 Best Predictor Under Squared Error](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)

> **Big idea:** Once you condition on $X$, any function of $X$ is a known constant, so it can be pulled out of the conditional expectation: $E[g(X) Y \mid X] = g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]$.

---

## Why It Matters

Products such as $XY$ appear everywhere: price times quantity, rate times time, a
feature times an error. Their expectations are hard when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent,
because $E[XY] \ne E[X] E[Y]$ in general. **Taking out what is known** fixes this:
condition on $X$, pull $X$ out, and average at the end. This rule finishes the
proof that residuals of the best predictor are uncorrelated with the inputs
([14.5](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)), and it is used in every
martingale argument of [Module 20](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/README.md).

## Intuition

A customer buys $X$ items, all at the same price $Y$ (in taka). The bill is $XY$.
Here are four equally likely customers:

| Customer | $X$ (items) | $Y$ (price) | $XY$ (bill) | $E[Y \mid X]$ | $X \cdot E[Y \mid X]$ | $E[XY \mid X]$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| a | 1 | 10 | 10 | 20 | 20 | 20 |
| b | 1 | 30 | 30 | 20 | 20 | 20 |
| c | 2 | 20 | 40 | 30 | 60 | 60 |
| d | 2 | 40 | 80 | 30 | 60 | 60 |

Look at the group $X = 2$ (customers c and d). Their bills are 40 and 80, with
average 60. But inside this group everyone bought exactly 2 items, so the bill is
just "2 times the price", and the average bill is 2 times the average price:
$2 \times 30 = 60$. Inside a group, $X$ is not random any more. It is a known number,
and a known number comes out of an average.

The last two columns are equal in every row. That is the rule:
$E[XY \mid X] = X \thinspace E[Y \mid X]$.

## Picture

```text
         group X = 1                        group X = 2
   +---------------------------+     +---------------------------+
   |  X is known: X = 1        |     |  X is known: X = 2        |
   |  bills: 1 x 10, 1 x 30    |     |  bills: 2 x 20, 2 x 40    |
   |  average = 1 x avg(Y)     |     |  average = 2 x avg(Y)     |
   |          = 1 x 20 = 20    |     |          = 2 x 30 = 60    |
   +---------------------------+     +---------------------------+

            E[XY | X]  =  X  x  E[Y | X]
```

Inside each group the known factor $X$ is a constant, so it moves outside the average.

## Definition

**Taking out what is known.** For any function $g$ (with all expectations finite):

$$
E\big[g(X) \thinspace Y \mid X\big] = g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]
$$

where $g(X)$ is any quantity that is fixed once you know $X$, such as $X$, $X^2$ or
$e^X$.

**In words:** when you condition on $X$, anything built from $X$ behaves like a
constant, so you can pull it out of the conditional expectation.

Combined with linearity and the law of total expectation
([14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)), it gives three useful facts:

$$
E\big[g(X) \thinspace Y\big] = E\big[g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]\big]
$$

**In words:** to average a product, first replace $Y$ by its conditional mean,
then average over $X$.

$$
E\Big[\big(Y - E[Y \mid X]\big) \thinspace g(X)\Big] = 0
$$

**In words:** the error of the best predictor is uncorrelated with every function of
$X$ (this is called **orthogonality**).

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}\big(X, E[Y \mid X]\big)
$$

**In words:** all of the covariance between $X$ and $Y$ goes through the conditional
mean of $Y$.

## Why It Is True

Take discrete $X$ and $Y$ and fix a value $x$. On the event $X = x$, $g(X) = g(x)$, a
plain number. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big[g(X) \thinspace Y \mid X = x\big] &= \sum_{y} g(x) \thinspace y \thinspace P(Y = y \mid X = x) && \text{(conditional LOTUS; } X = x \text{ is given)} \\
&= g(x) \sum_{y} y \thinspace P(Y = y \mid X = x) && \text{(}g(x)\text{ is a constant)} \\
&= g(x) \thinspace E[Y \mid X = x] && \text{(definition)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This holds for every $x$, so as random variables $E[g(X) Y \mid X] = g(X) E[Y \mid X]$.
The continuous proof is the same with integrals.

**The three facts.** The first comes from taking the expectation of both sides:
$E[g(X) Y] = E[E[g(X) Y \mid X]] = E[g(X) E[Y \mid X]]$. For the second, use the
first:

$$
E\big[(Y - E[Y \mid X]) \thinspace g(X)\big] = E\big[g(X) \thinspace Y\big] - E\big[g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]\big] = 0
$$

For the third, take $g(X) = X$ in the first fact, and use $E[Y] = E[E[Y \mid X]]$:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y] = E\big[X \thinspace E[Y \mid X]\big] - E[X] \thinspace E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] = \operatorname{Cov}\big(X, E[Y \mid X]\big)
$$

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Average Bill

**Problem.** For the four customers in the Intuition, find $E[XY]$ in two ways.

**Solution.**

1. Directly: the bills are 10, 30, 40, 80, each with probability $\frac{1}{4}$:

$$
E[XY] = \frac{10 + 30 + 40 + 80}{4} = \frac{160}{4} = 40
$$

2. By taking out what is known: $E[XY \mid X] = X \thinspace E[Y \mid X]$ is 20 (when $X = 1$)
   or 60 (when $X = 2$), each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$
E[XY] = E\big[X \thinspace E[Y \mid X]\big] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 20 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 60 = 10 + 30 = 40
$$

**Answer.** The average bill is 40 taka.

**Check.** The naive product $E[X] E[Y] = 1.5 \times 25 = 37.5$ is wrong, because
customers who buy more items also pay higher prices here. The difference
$40 - 37.5 = 2.5$ is $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

### Example 2: A Die and Some Coins

**Problem.** Roll a fair die to get $X$. Then flip $X$ fair coins, and let $Y$ be the
number of heads. Find $E[XY]$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

**Solution.**

1. Given $X = x$, $Y \sim \text{Bin}(x, \frac{1}{2})$, so $E[Y \mid X] = \frac{X}{2}$.
2. Take out what is known:

$$
E[XY \mid X] = X \thinspace E[Y \mid X] = X \cdot \frac{X}{2} = \frac{X^2}{2}
$$

3. Average, using $E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = \frac{91}{6}$:

$$
E[XY] = \frac{1}{2} E[X^2] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{91}{6} = \frac{91}{12} \approx 7.583
$$

4. $E[X] = 3.5$ and $E[Y] = E[X / 2] = 1.75$, so:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{91}{12} - 3.5 \times 1.75 = 7.5833 - 6.125 = 1.4583 = \frac{35}{24}
$$

**Answer.** $E[XY] = \frac{91}{12} \approx 7.583$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{35}{24} \approx 1.458$.

**Check.** By the third fact, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, \frac{X}{2}) = \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{35}{12} = \frac{35}{24}$.
Same answer, with almost no work.

### Example 3: Residuals Are Orthogonal

**Problem.** Roll a fair die; $Y$ is the roll and $X = 1$ if the roll is even, 0 if odd.
The best predictor is $E[Y \mid X] = 3 + X$
([14.2](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md)). Check that the
residual $R = Y - (3 + X)$ satisfies $E[R \thinspace g(X)] = 0$ for any function $g$.

**Solution.**

1. The residuals: odd rolls 1, 3, 5 give $R = -2, 0, 2$. Even rolls 2, 4, 6 give
   $R = 2 - 4, 4 - 4, 6 - 4 = -2, 0, 2$.
2. Inside the odd group, $g(X) = g(0)$ is one fixed number:

$$
E[R \thinspace g(X) \mid X = 0] = g(0) \cdot \frac{-2 + 0 + 2}{3} = g(0) \cdot 0 = 0
$$

3. In the same way, $E[R \thinspace g(X) \mid X = 1] = g(1) \cdot 0 = 0$.
4. Average the two groups: $E[R \thinspace g(X)] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0 = 0$.

**Answer.** $E[R \thinspace g(X)] = 0$ for every $g$, so the residual is uncorrelated
with every function of $X$.

**Check.** The residual averages to 0 **inside each group**, and that is why no
function of $X$ can find any pattern left in it. If a pattern were left, the
predictor could be improved, which [14.5](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)
says is impossible.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2: it compares $E[XY]$ with $E[X \thinspace E[Y \mid X]] = E[X^2] / 2$,
and the two ways to get the covariance.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Roll a die to get X, then flip X fair coins; Y is the number of heads.
x = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
y = rng.binomial(x, 0.5)

e_xy = np.mean(x * y)
e_x_cond = np.mean(x * (x / 2))                 # E[X E[Y | X]] with E[Y | X] = X/2
cov_xy = np.cov(x, y)[0, 1]
cov_x_cond = np.cov(x, x / 2)[0, 1]             # Cov(X, E[Y | X])

print(f"E[XY]:            {e_xy:.4f}   exact {91 / 12:.4f}")
print(f"E[X E[Y | X]]:    {e_x_cond:.4f}   exact {91 / 12:.4f}")
print(f"Cov(X, Y):        {cov_xy:.4f}   exact {35 / 24:.4f}")
print(f"Cov(X, E[Y | X]): {cov_x_cond:.4f}   exact {35 / 24:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[XY]:            7.5829   exact 7.5833
E[X E[Y | X]]:    7.5761   exact 7.5833
Cov(X, Y):        1.4608   exact 1.4583
Cov(X, E[Y | X]): 1.4576   exact 1.4583
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Pulling out a function of $Y$.** Only functions of what you condition on are known.
  $E[Y^2 \mid X]$ is **not** $Y \thinspace E[Y \mid X]$.
- **Pulling out something that is not a function of $X$.** If $Z$ is another random
  variable, $E[ZY \mid X] = Z \thinspace E[Y \mid X]$ is false in general. $Z$ is not
  known just because $X$ is.
- **Pulling out without conditioning.** $E[XY] = X \thinspace E[Y]$ makes no sense: the
  left side is a number and the right side is random. The rule works only inside
  $E[\thinspace \cdot \mid X]$.
- **Writing $E[XY] = E[X] E[Y]$ for dependent variables.** Example 1 shows the error.
  Condition on $X$ first instead.
- **Thinking that uncorrelated means a constant conditional mean.** A constant
  $E[Y \mid X]$ implies zero covariance, but not the reverse (Practice 5).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the orthogonality of residuals is why the error of a
  well-trained regression model shows no pattern against any feature. Checking
  residual plots is a test of this property.
- **Reinforcement learning:** policy gradient methods use
  $E[\nabla \log \pi(A \mid S) \thinspace b(S)] = 0$ for a baseline $b(S)$; the baseline
  is "known given the state" and comes out, leaving an expectation of 0.
- **Martingales:** proving that a betting strategy cannot beat a fair game uses
  $E[H_n (M_{n+1} - M_n) \mid \text{past}] = H_n \cdot 0$, where the bet size $H_n$ is
  known from the past ([20.8 Martingales](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.8-Martingales.md)).
- **Business:** expected revenue = E[quantity times price], computed by conditioning
  on the quantity when price depends on it.
- **Random sums:** $E[N \thinspace \mu \mid N] = N \mu$ is the key step of
  [14.7 Random Sums](14.7-Random-Sums.md).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You know that $E[Y \mid X] = 2X$. Simplify $E[X^2 Y + 3X \mid X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By linearity, taking out $X^2$, and $E[3X \mid X] = 3X$:

$$E[X^2 Y + 3X \mid X] = X^2 E[Y \mid X] + 3X = X^2 \cdot 2X + 3X = 2X^3 + 3X$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For the four customers in the Intuition, find $E[X^2 Y \mid X]$ and
$E[X^2 Y]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[X^2 Y \mid X] = X^2 E[Y \mid X]$, which is $1 \times 20 = 20$ when $X = 1$ and $4 \times 30 = 120$ when $X = 2$. So $E[X^2 Y] = \frac{1}{2}(20) + \frac{1}{2}(120) = 70$. Directly: $\frac{1(10) + 1(30) + 4(20) + 4(40)}{4} = \frac{280}{4} = 70$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Let $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and, given $X = x$, $Y \sim \text{Unif}(0, x)$.
Find $E[XY]$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[Y \mid X] = \frac{X}{2}$, so $E[XY] = E[X \cdot \frac{X}{2}] = \frac{1}{2} E[X^2] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$. With $E[X] = \frac{1}{2}$ and $E[Y] = \frac{1}{4}$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \tfrac{1}{6} - \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{6} - \tfrac{1}{8} = \tfrac{1}{24} \approx 0.0417$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Suppose $E[Y \mid X] = a + bX$. Show that $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = b \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the third fact, and since adding a constant does not change a covariance:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, a + bX) = b \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = b \operatorname{Var}(X)$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove: if $E[Y \mid X] = E[Y]$ (a constant), then $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$.
Then show that the reverse is false, using $X$ uniform on $\lbrace -1, 0, 1 \rbrace$ and
$Y = X^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If $E[Y \mid X] = c$, then by the third fact $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, c) = 0$.

For the counterexample, $E[XY] = E[X^3] = \frac{-1 + 0 + 1}{3} = 0$ and $E[X] = 0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot E[Y] = 0$. But $E[Y \mid X] = X^2$, which is not constant. So we have a chain of ever weaker conditions: independent, then constant conditional mean, then uncorrelated. Each implies the next, and none of the reverse steps holds.

</details>

</details>

## Summary

- Given $X$, every function of $X$ is a known constant.
- Known constants come out: $E[g(X) Y \mid X] = g(X) E[Y \mid X]$.
- To average a product of dependent variables, condition on one, pull it out, then
  average: $E[g(X) Y] = E[g(X) E[Y \mid X]]$.
- The residual $Y - E[Y \mid X]$ is orthogonal to every function of $X$, and
  $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, E[Y \mid X])$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E\big[g(X) \thinspace Y \mid X\big] = g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]}
$$
