# 14.5 Best Predictor Under Squared Error

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 14: Conditional Expectation](README.md) / 14.5 Best Predictor Under Squared Error

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [14.4 Law of Total Variance](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md)

> **Big idea:** Among all rules that predict $Y$ from $X$, the conditional mean $E[Y \mid X]$ has the smallest mean squared error, and that smallest error is $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$.

---

## Why It Matters

In [6.10 Expectation as Best Guess](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md)
you learned that if you know **nothing**, the best single guess for $Y$ under
squared error is its mean. Real predictions use information: features, sensor
readings, past values. This topic proves that with information $X$, the best
possible prediction rule is $E[Y \mid X]$. It is the reason a regression model
trained with mean squared error is trying to learn a conditional mean, and it sets
a floor on the error that no model, however large, can beat.

## Intuition

Your friend rolls a fair die and you must predict the roll $Y$. You pay the
squared error. How good can you be? It depends on what the friend tells you first.

| What you are told | Best prediction | Mean squared error |
|---|---|---|
| nothing | always $3.5$ | $\frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917$ |
| odd or even | 3 if odd, 4 if even | $\frac{8}{3} \approx 2.667$ |
| low (1 to 3) or high (4 to 6) | 2 if low, 5 if high | $\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$ |
| the exact roll | the roll itself | $0$ |

In every row the best prediction is the **group average**, the conditional mean of
$Y$ given what you are told. Inside each group you are back in the "know nothing"
situation of 6.10, where the mean is best. The error left over is the spread inside
the groups, the "within" part of the law of total variance
([14.4](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md)). More useful information means groups with
less spread, and a smaller error.

## Picture

```text
   mean squared error of the best predictor

   2.917 |  ###########################   told nothing     -> predict 3.5
   2.667 |  #########################     told odd / even   -> 3 or 4
   0.667 |  ######                        told low / high   -> 2 or 5
   0.000 |                                told the roll     -> the roll
         +-----------------------------
```

The more your information separates the possible values of $Y$, the smaller the
error of the best predictor.

## Definition

A **predictor** of $Y$ from $X$ is any function $h$; its prediction is $h(X)$. Its
**mean squared error** (MSE) is $E[(Y - h(X))^2]$. Let $g(X) = E[Y \mid X]$, and assume
$Y$ and $h(X)$ have finite variance. Then for every predictor $h$:

$$
E\big[(Y - h(X))^2\big] = E\big[(Y - g(X))^2\big] + E\big[(g(X) - h(X))^2\big]
$$

where:

- $E[(Y - g(X))^2]$ is the error of the conditional mean, which equals $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$,
- $E[(g(X) - h(X))^2]$ is the extra error for not using the conditional mean. It is never negative.

**In words:** the error of any rule = the error you cannot avoid + how far your rule
is from the conditional mean, squared and averaged.

So the conditional mean is the **best predictor under squared error**:

$$
\min_{h} E\big[(Y - h(X))^2\big] = E\big[(Y - E[Y \mid X])^2\big] = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big]
$$

**In words:** no rule beats $E[Y \mid X]$, and the smallest possible error is the
average conditional variance.

## Why It Is True

Fix a value $x$ and look only inside the group $X = x$. There, $h(X)$ is the fixed
number $c = h(x)$. The identity of 6.10, $E[(Y - c)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Y) + (E[Y] - c)^2$,
holds for **any** distribution, so it also holds for the conditional distribution
of $Y$ given $X = x$:

$$
E\big[(Y - h(x))^2 \mid X = x\big] = \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x) + \big(g(x) - h(x)\big)^2
$$

This is true for every $x$, so as random variables:

$$
E\big[(Y - h(X))^2 \mid X\big] = \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) + \big(g(X) - h(X)\big)^2
$$

Now take the expectation of both sides, and use the law of total expectation
([14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)) on the left:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big[(Y - h(X))^2\big] &= E\Big[ E\big[(Y - h(X))^2 \mid X\big] \Big] && \text{(law of total expectation)} \\
&= E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + E\big[(g(X) - h(X))^2\big] && \text{(the identity above, linearity)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Put $h = g$: the last term is 0, so $E[(Y - g(X))^2] = E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$.
Putting this back gives the decomposition in the Definition. The last term is an
average of squares, so it is at least 0, and it is 0 only when $h(X) = g(X)$ with
probability 1. So $g$ is the best predictor, and it is the only best one.

A useful side fact: the **residual** $Y - E[Y \mid X]$ has mean 0 and is
uncorrelated with every function of $X$. Whatever is left after the best prediction
cannot be predicted from $X$ any further. Topic
[14.6](14.6-Taking-Out-What-is-Known.md) proves this.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Die Table

**Problem.** Check one row of the Intuition table: find the MSE of the best predictor
when you are told "low or high". Then find the MSE of the rule "predict 3 if low, 4 if
high" for the same information.

**Solution.**

1. Best predictor: $E[Y \mid W]$ is 2 (low) or 5 (high). Inside the low group the
   squared errors are $(1 - 2)^2, (2 - 2)^2, (3 - 2)^2 = 1, 0, 1$. The high group is
   the same. So

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 1}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667
$$

2. This equals $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid W)] = \frac{2}{3}$ from
   [14.4](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md).
3. The other rule $h$ predicts 3 or 4. It differs from $g$ by 1 in each group, so
   by the decomposition:

$$
\text{MSE}(h) = \frac{2}{3} + E\big[(g(W) - h(W))^2\big] = \frac{2}{3} + 1 = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.667
$$

**Answer.** The best predictor has MSE $\frac{2}{3}$; the rule "3 or 4" has MSE $\frac{5}{3}$.

**Check.** Directly, the errors of "3 or 4" for the rolls 1 to 6 are $-2, -1, 0, 0, 1, 2$.
Their squares add to $4 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10$, and $\frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3}$.

### Example 2: A Curve That No Straight Line Can Follow

**Problem.** Let $X \sim \text{Unif}(-1, 1)$, let $\varepsilon$ be noise with mean 0 and
variance $0.01$, independent of $X$, and let $Y = X^2 + \varepsilon$. Find the best
predictor and its MSE. Then find the best **straight line** $a + bX$ and its MSE.

**Solution.**

1. Given $X = x$: $E[Y \mid X = x] = x^2 + E[\varepsilon] = x^2$. So the best predictor
   is $g(X) = X^2$, and its error is $Y - X^2 = \varepsilon$:

$$
\text{MSE}(g) = E[\varepsilon^2] = 0.01
$$

2. For a line, use $E[X] = 0$, $E[X^2] = \frac{1}{3}$ and $E[X^3] = 0$ (by symmetry).
   So $E[XY] = E[X^3] + E[X] E[\varepsilon] = 0$. Expand:

$$
E\big[(Y - a - bX)^2\big] = E\big[(Y - a)^2\big] - 2b \thinspace E\big[X(Y - a)\big] + b^2 E[X^2] = E\big[(Y - a)^2\big] + \frac{b^2}{3}
$$

   because $E[X(Y - a)] = E[XY] - aE[X] = 0$.
3. The term $\frac{b^2}{3}$ is smallest at $b = 0$, and $E[(Y - a)^2]$ is smallest at
   $a = E[Y] = \frac{1}{3}$ (6.10). So the best line is flat at $\frac{1}{3}$. Its MSE is
   $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X^2) + 0.01 = \left( E[X^4] - (E[X^2])^2 \right) + 0.01 = \left( \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{9} \right) + 0.01 = \frac{4}{45} + 0.01 \approx 0.0989
$$

**Answer.** The best predictor $X^2$ has MSE $0.01$. The best straight line is the
constant $\frac{1}{3}$, with MSE about $0.0989$, almost 10 times larger.

**Check.** $X$ and $Y$ have covariance 0, yet $X$ predicts $Y$ almost perfectly.
Zero correlation only means "no straight-line relation"
([11.4](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.4-Uncorrelated-vs-Independent.md)). The
conditional mean catches any shape. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 3: Predicting a Yes or No Outcome

**Problem.** Use the shop table of
[14.2](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md), Example 2
($Y = 1$ if the customer pays, $X$ = items). Compare the MSE of three rules:
(a) always predict $0.6$; (b) predict $g(X) = P(Y = 1 \mid X)$; (c) predict 1 if
$g(X) \gt 0.5$ and 0 otherwise.

**Solution.**

1. Rule (a) is the best constant, so its MSE is $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.24$.
2. Rule (b) is the best predictor. Given $X = x$, $Y$ is Bernoulli with $p = g(x)$, so
   $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x) = g(x)(1 - g(x))$:

$$
\text{MSE} = 0.30 \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{2}{3} + 0.35 \cdot \frac{4}{7} \cdot \frac{3}{7} + 0.35 \cdot \frac{6}{7} \cdot \frac{1}{7} \approx 0.0667 + 0.0857 + 0.0429 = 0.1952
$$

3. Rule (c) predicts 0 for $X = 1$ and 1 for $X = 2, 3$. Its squared error is 1 when
   it is wrong and 0 when it is right, so its MSE is the chance of being wrong:

$$
\text{MSE} = 0.30 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 0.35 \cdot \frac{3}{7} + 0.35 \cdot \frac{1}{7} = 0.10 + 0.15 + 0.05 = 0.30
$$

**Answer.** MSE is $0.24$ for (a), about $0.195$ for (b), and $0.30$ for (c). Rule (b)
is best, as the theorem says.

**Check.** For a 0/1 target, the best squared-error prediction is a **probability**,
not a hard yes or no. This MSE for probabilities is called the **Brier score**. A rule
that rounds to 0 or 1 can be worse than knowing nothing about $X$ at all.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2. It compares three predictors on simulated data: the best
constant, the least-squares straight line fitted to the data, and $X^2$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Y = X^2 + noise, with X ~ Unif(-1, 1) and noise variance 0.01.
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n_trials)
y = x**2 + rng.normal(0, 0.1, n_trials)

slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)          # best straight line for this data
mse_const = np.mean((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
mse_line = np.mean((y - (intercept + slope * x)) ** 2)
mse_best = np.mean((y - x**2) ** 2)              # E[Y | X] = X^2

print(f"Fitted line: {intercept:.4f} + {slope:.4f} x   (exact 0.3333 + 0 x)")
print(f"MSE best constant: {mse_const:.4f}  (exact {4 / 45 + 0.01:.4f})")
print(f"MSE best line:     {mse_line:.4f}  (exact {4 / 45 + 0.01:.4f})")
print(f"MSE of E[Y | X]:   {mse_best:.4f}  (exact 0.0100)")
```

Output:

```text
Fitted line: 0.3334 + 0.0004 x   (exact 0.3333 + 0 x)
MSE best constant: 0.0988  (exact 0.0989)
MSE best line:     0.0988  (exact 0.0989)
MSE of E[Y | X]:   0.0100  (exact 0.0100)
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking the best predictor makes no errors.** Its error is $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$,
  which is 0 only if $Y$ is a function of $X$. No model can beat this floor.
- **Confusing "best predictor" with "best straight line".** The best line uses only
  the covariance. $E[Y \mid X]$ can be any curve (Example 2). They agree only in
  special cases, such as the multivariate Gaussian
  ([12.7 Conditionals](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.7-Conditionals.md)).
- **Using the conditional mean for a different loss.** Under absolute error the best
  rule is the **conditional median**; under 0-1 loss it is the most likely value
  given $X$. Squared error is what makes the mean best.
- **Rounding probabilities to 0 or 1.** For a yes/no target, the best squared-error
  prediction is $P(Y = 1 \mid X)$ itself (Example 3).
- **Forgetting that a predictor may use only $X$.** Predicting $Y$ by $Y$ has error 0,
  but it is not a rule based on $X$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a neural network or any regression model trained to
  minimize mean squared error is learning an estimate of $E[Y \mid X]$. The leftover
  error $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$ is the **irreducible error** (noise floor)
  ([21.1.2 Loss Functions as Probability](../21-APPLICATIONS-OF-PROBABILITY/21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)).
- **Classification:** models that output probabilities are judged by the Brier score
  or by cross-entropy; both are minimized by the true $P(Y = 1 \mid X)$.
- **Signal processing:** the Kalman filter computes conditional means to remove
  noise from GPS, radar and other sensor signals.
- **Finance:** the fair price of a future payoff is a conditional expectation given
  the information available today.
- **Everyday life:** "people like you spent about this much" is a conditional mean
  used as a prediction.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You are told only whether a fair die shows 6. What is the best predictor
of the roll, and what is its MSE?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If the roll is 6, predict 6 (error 0). If not, the roll is uniform on 1 to 5, so predict the mean 3; the variance of 1 to 5 is $\frac{4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4}{5} = 2$. So:

$$\text{MSE} = \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{5}{6} \cdot 2 = \tfrac{5}{3} \approx 1.667$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For the laptop table of
[14.1](14.1-Conditional-Expectation-Given-a-Value.md), give the best predictor of $Y$
from $X$ and its MSE. Use the conditional variances $0.45$ and $0.56$ from 14.4,
Practice 3. Compare with the best constant guess.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Predict $0.5$ if $X = 1$ and $1.2$ if $X = 2$. Its MSE is $0.5(0.45) + 0.5(0.56) = 0.505$. The best constant is $0.85$, with MSE $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0.6275$. Knowing the age lowers the error by $0.1225$, which is exactly $\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X])$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A model always predicts $E[Y \mid X] + 0.5$. The best possible MSE for
this problem is 2. What is the model's MSE?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $h(X) - g(X) = 0.5$ always, so by the decomposition:

$$\text{MSE}(h) = 2 + E[(0.5)^2] = 2 + 0.25 = 2.25$$

A constant bias $b$ always adds $b^2$ to the error.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Roll two fair dice $X_1, X_2$ with sum $S$. Find the best predictor of
$S$ from $X_1$ and its MSE. Then find the best predictor of $X_1$ from $S$ and its MSE.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[S \mid X_1] = X_1 + 3.5$, and the error is $X_2 - 3.5$, so the MSE is $\operatorname{Var}(X_2) = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917$.

$E[X_1 \mid S] = \frac{S}{2}$. Its MSE is $E[\operatorname{Var}(X_1 \mid S)]$. By the law of total variance, with $\operatorname{Var}(S) = \frac{35}{6}$:

$$E[\operatorname{Var}(X_1 \mid S)] = \operatorname{Var}(X_1) - \operatorname{Var}(\tfrac{S}{2}) = \tfrac{35}{12} - \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \tfrac{35}{6} = \tfrac{35}{12} - \tfrac{35}{24} = \tfrac{35}{24} \approx 1.458$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For discrete $X$ and $Y$, prove that
$E[(Y - g(X)) \thinspace k(X)] = 0$ for every function $k$, where $g(X) = E[Y \mid X]$.
Then use it, with $k = g - h$, to give a second proof of the decomposition.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Inside the group $X = x$, $k(X) = k(x)$ is a constant, and the average of $Y - g(x)$ is $g(x) - g(x) = 0$. So $E[(Y - g(X)) k(X) \mid X = x] = k(x) \cdot 0 = 0$ for every $x$, and the law of total expectation gives $E[(Y - g(X)) k(X)] = 0$. Now write $Y - h = (Y - g) + (g - h)$ and expand:

$$E[(Y - h)^2] = E[(Y - g)^2] + 2E[(Y - g)(g - h)] + E[(g - h)^2] = E[(Y - g)^2] + E[(g - h)^2]$$

because the middle term is 0 by the first part with $k = g - h$.

</details>

## Summary

- A predictor is any function $h(X)$; its quality under squared error is $E[(Y - h(X))^2]$.
- The best predictor is $E[Y \mid X]$. Any other rule pays an extra $E[(g(X) - h(X))^2]$.
- The smallest possible MSE is $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$, the noise floor.
- More informative $X$ gives a lower floor. The best straight line can be much worse
  than the best curve.
- For a 0/1 target, the best squared-error prediction is the probability $P(Y = 1 \mid X)$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E\big[(Y - h(X))^2\big] = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + E\big[(E[Y \mid X] - h(X))^2\big]}
$$
