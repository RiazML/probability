## How to Use This Set

- Each problem says which topic it uses. If you get stuck, reread that topic first.
- Try every problem on paper before opening the answer. Write each step, as in the
  worked examples.
- **Warm-Up** problems check the basic formulas. **Core** problems mix two or three
  ideas. **Challenge** problems include proofs and classic puzzles. **Simulation
  Projects** ask you to write code.
- The key question in almost every problem is: **what should I condition on?** Choose
  the thing that, once known, makes the rest easy.
- After solving a problem by hand, check the number with a short simulation.

## Warm-Up

The first two problems use this joint PMF:

| | $Y = 1$ | $Y = 2$ | $Y = 3$ | $p_X(x)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $X = 0$ | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 0.40 |
| $X = 1$ | 0.30 | 0.15 | 0.15 | 0.60 |

**W1. (Easy)** *Uses [14.1 Conditional Expectation Given a Value](14.1-Conditional-Expectation-Given-a-Value.md).*
Find $E[Y \mid X = 0]$, $E[Y \mid X = 1]$ and $E[Y]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Divide each row by its sum. Row $X = 0$ gives $0.25, 0.50, 0.25$; row $X = 1$ gives $0.50, 0.25, 0.25$:

$$E[Y \mid X = 0] = 1(0.25) + 2(0.50) + 3(0.25) = 0.25 + 1.00 + 0.75 = 2$$

$$E[Y \mid X = 1] = 1(0.50) + 2(0.25) + 3(0.25) = 0.50 + 0.50 + 0.75 = 1.75$$

The column sums are $0.40, 0.35, 0.25$, so $E[Y] = 0.40 + 0.70 + 0.75 = 1.85$.

</details>

**W2. (Easy)** *Uses [14.2 Conditional Expectation as a Random Variable](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md) and [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).*
For the same table, write $E[Y \mid X]$ as a formula in $X$, give its distribution,
and check that its mean is $E[Y]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is 2 when $X = 0$ and $1.75$ when $X = 1$, so $E[Y \mid X] = 2 - 0.25X$. It takes the value 2 with probability $0.40$ and $1.75$ with probability $0.60$. Its mean:

$$E[E[Y \mid X]] = 0.40 \times 2 + 0.60 \times 1.75 = 0.80 + 1.05 = 1.85 = E[Y]$$

</details>

**W3. (Easy)** *Uses [14.3 Law of Total Expectation](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).*
70% of the users of an app are on mobile, with an average session of 4 minutes. The
other 30% are on desktop, with an average session of 10 minutes. What is the average
session length over all users?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[T] = 0.7 \times 4 + 0.3 \times 10 = 2.8 + 3.0 = 5.8 \text{ minutes}$$

</details>

**W4. (Easy)** *Uses [14.6 Taking Out What is Known](14.6-Taking-Out-What-is-Known.md).*
You know $E[Y \mid X] = \frac{X}{3}$. Simplify $E[(X + 1) Y + X^2 \mid X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$(X + 1)$ and $X^2$ are known given $X$:

$$E[(X + 1) Y + X^2 \mid X] = (X + 1) \frac{X}{3} + X^2 = \frac{X^2 + X}{3} + X^2 = \frac{4X^2 + X}{3}$$

</details>

**W5. (Easy)** *Uses [14.7 Random Sums](14.7-Random-Sums.md).*
A football team takes $N \sim \text{Pois}(12)$ shots in a match (12 is the mean). Each
shot scores with probability $0.1$, independently. What is the expected number of
goals?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Goals are a random sum of $N$ terms, each $\text{Bern}(0.1)$:

$$E[\text{goals}] = E[N] \times 0.1 = 12 \times 0.1 = 1.2$$

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium)** *Uses [14.4 Law of Total Variance](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md).*
For the table of W1, find $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 0)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 1)$.
Then check the law of total variance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Row $X = 0$: mean 2, and $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 0) = 0.25(1 - 2)^2 + 0.50(0) + 0.25(3 - 2)^2 = 0.5$.

Row $X = 1$: $E[Y^2 \mid X = 1] = 0.50(1) + 0.25(4) + 0.25(9) = 3.75$, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 1) = 3.75 - 1.75^2 = 0.6875$.

Within: $0.40(0.5) + 0.60(0.6875) = 0.2 + 0.4125 = 0.6125$. Between:

$$\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) = 0.40(2 - 1.85)^2 + 0.60(1.75 - 1.85)^2 = 0.009 + 0.006 = 0.015$$

Total $0.6125 + 0.015 = 0.6275$. Directly: $E[Y^2] = 0.40(1) + 0.35(4) + 0.25(9) = 4.05$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 4.05 - 1.85^2 = 0.6275$. They agree. Here $X$ explains very little of the spread.

</details>

**C2. (Medium)** *Uses [14.7 Random Sums](14.7-Random-Sums.md).*
A store has $N \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ customers a day, each spending an amount $X$
with mean $\mu$, independently of each other and of $N$. Find the expected total
revenue. Then, for $\lambda = 80$, $\mu = 150$ taka and a standard deviation of 50 taka
per customer, find the mean and standard deviation of the daily revenue.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Condition on $N$: $E[\text{revenue} \mid N] = N\mu$, so $E[\text{revenue}] = E[N]\mu = \lambda \mu$. With the numbers, $E = 80 \times 150 = 12000$ taka. For the compound Poisson sum, $\operatorname{Var} = \lambda E[X^2]$:

$$\operatorname{Var} = 80 \times (50^2 + 150^2) = 80 \times (2500 + 22500) = 80 \times 25000 = 2000000$$

The standard deviation is $\sqrt{2000000} \approx 1414$ taka.

</details>

**C3. (Medium)** *Uses [14.3 Law of Total Expectation](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).*
Flip a fair coin until you have seen at least one head **and** at least one tail. Find the
expected number of flips.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Condition on the first flip. Whatever it is, you then wait for the **other** face, which takes a $\text{Geom}(\frac{1}{2})$ number of flips, with mean 2. So, in both cases, $E[N \mid \text{first flip}] = 1 + 2 = 3$, and:

$$E[N] = \tfrac{1}{2}(3) + \tfrac{1}{2}(3) = 3$$

</details>

**C4. (Medium)** *Uses [14.5 Best Predictor Under Squared Error](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md) and [14.4](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md).*
$X$ is uniform on $\lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$. Given $X = x$, $Y$ is uniform on
$\lbrace 0, 1, \dots, x \rbrace$. Find the best predictor of $Y$ from $X$ and its MSE.
Compare with the MSE of the best constant guess. (A discrete uniform on
$\lbrace 0, \dots, x \rbrace$ has variance $\frac{x(x + 2)}{12}$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The best predictor is $E[Y \mid X] = \frac{X}{2}$. Its MSE is $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$. The conditional variances are $\frac{3}{12}, \frac{8}{12}, \frac{15}{12}$ for $x = 1, 2, 3$:

$$E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] = \tfrac{1}{3} \left( \tfrac{3}{12} + \tfrac{8}{12} + \tfrac{15}{12} \right) = \tfrac{26}{36} = \tfrac{13}{18} \approx 0.722$$

The best constant is $E[Y] = E[X/2] = 1$, with MSE $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$. With $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{2}{3}$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \tfrac{13}{18} + \operatorname{Var}(\tfrac{X}{2}) = \tfrac{13}{18} + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{13}{18} + \tfrac{3}{18} = \tfrac{16}{18} = \tfrac{8}{9} \approx 0.889$$

Knowing $X$ lowers the MSE from about $0.889$ to about $0.722$.

</details>

**C5. (Medium)** *Uses [14.6 Taking Out What is Known](14.6-Taking-Out-What-is-Known.md).*
A student studies $X$ hours, uniform on $\lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$. Given $X$, the exam score
has $E[Y \mid X] = 50 + 10X$. Find $E[XY]$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[X] = 2$ and $E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 4 + 9}{3} = \frac{14}{3}$. Take out what is known:

$$E[XY] = E[X(50 + 10X)] = 50 E[X] + 10 E[X^2] = 100 + \tfrac{140}{3} \approx 146.67$$

$E[Y] = 50 + 10 \times 2 = 70$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 146.67 - 2 \times 70 = 6.67$. Check: $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, 50 + 10X) = 10 \operatorname{Var}(X) = 10 \times \frac{2}{3} \approx 6.67$.

</details>

**C6. (Medium)** *Uses [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md) and [14.4](14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md).*
The number of bugs in a file is $Y$. Given a hidden "difficulty" $\Lambda$, $Y \sim \text{Pois}(\Lambda)$.
The difficulty itself is $\Lambda \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1, so mean 1 and variance 1).
Find $E[Y]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$. Is $Y$ Poisson?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[Y \mid \Lambda] = \Lambda$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid \Lambda) = \Lambda$. So:

$$E[Y] = E[\Lambda] = 1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = E[\Lambda] + \operatorname{Var}(\Lambda) = 1 + 1 = 2$$

A Poisson variable has variance equal to its mean, but here the variance is twice the mean. So $Y$ is not Poisson. Mixing over an unknown rate always adds variance; this is called **overdispersion**, and it is common in real count data.

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard)** *Uses [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md) and [14.6](14.6-Taking-Out-What-is-Known.md).*
Prove the law of total expectation for discrete $X$ and $Y$. Then use it, together with
taking out what is known, to prove $E[g(X) Y] = E[g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $m(x) = E[Y \mid X = x] = \sum_y y \thinspace p_{X,Y}(x, y) / p_X(x)$. By LOTUS, then cancelling $p_X(x)$, then swapping the sums:

$$E[m(X)] = \sum_x m(x) p_X(x) = \sum_x \sum_y y \thinspace p_{X,Y}(x, y) = \sum_y y \sum_x p_{X,Y}(x, y) = \sum_y y \thinspace p_Y(y) = E[Y]$$

For the second part, apply this law to the random variable $g(X) Y$ in place of $Y$, and then take out the known factor $g(X)$:

$$E[g(X) Y] = E\big[E[g(X) Y \mid X]\big] = E\big[g(X) \thinspace E[Y \mid X]\big]$$

</details>

**H2. (Hard)** *Uses [14.5 Best Predictor Under Squared Error](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md).*
Prove that $E[Y \mid X]$ minimizes the mean squared error $E[(Y - h(X))^2]$ over all
functions $h$, and that the minimum is $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $g(X) = E[Y \mid X]$ and $Y - h(X) = (Y - g(X)) + (g(X) - h(X))$. Expand the square and take expectations:

$$E[(Y - h)^2] = E[(Y - g)^2] + 2E[(Y - g)(g - h)] + E[(g - h)^2]$$

The middle term is 0: by H1 with the function $k(X) = g(X) - h(X)$, $E[k(X) Y] = E[k(X) g(X)]$, so $E[(Y - g) k(X)] = 0$. Hence

$$E[(Y - h)^2] = E[(Y - g)^2] + E[(g - h)^2] \ge E[(Y - g)^2]$$

with equality only when $h(X) = g(X)$ with probability 1. Finally, by the law of total expectation, $E[(Y - g(X))^2] = E\big[E[(Y - g(X))^2 \mid X]\big] = E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$.

</details>

**H3. (Hard)** *Uses [14.3 Law of Total Expectation](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md) (first-step analysis).*
In 14.3 you found that a fair coin needs on average 6 flips to show HH but only 4 to show
HT. Now find the expected number of flips until **HHT** appears, and until **HTH**
appears.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**HHT.** States: start $S$, "H", "HH", with unknowns $a, b, c$ (expected further flips). From HH, a T finishes and an H keeps us in HH. From H, a T sends us back to $S$:

$$a = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} b + \tfrac{1}{2} a, \qquad b = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} c + \tfrac{1}{2} a, \qquad c = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} c$$

So $c = 2$, $b = 2 + \frac{a}{2}$, and $\frac{a}{2} = 1 + \frac{b}{2} = 2 + \frac{a}{4}$, which gives $a = 8$.

**HTH.** States: $S$, "H", "HT", with unknowns $a, b, c$. From H, another H keeps us in H. From HT, an H finishes, and a T (making "TT") sends us back to $S$:

$$a = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} b + \tfrac{1}{2} a, \qquad b = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} b + \tfrac{1}{2} c, \qquad c = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} a$$

So $c = 1 + \frac{a}{2}$, $b = 2 + c = 3 + \frac{a}{2}$, and $a = 2 + b = 5 + \frac{a}{2}$, which gives $a = 10$.

**Answer:** 8 flips for HHT and 10 for HTH. HTH can overlap with itself (its last H can start a new HTH), and self-overlapping patterns take longer, just like HH. Simulation Project S1 checks these numbers.

</details>

**H4. (Hard)** *Uses [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).*
Two players flip one shared fair coin. Ann wins if HH appears first; Bob wins if HT
appears first. Find the probability that Ann wins. Is it strange, given that HT appears
sooner on average?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Both patterns start with H, so nothing happens until the first H. Right after the first H, the next flip decides the game: H gives HH (Ann wins), T gives HT (Bob wins). So

$$P(\text{Ann wins}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

It is not a contradiction. The mean waiting time also counts what happens **after** a failure. Bob's pattern is never "failed" once an H appears, while Ann, playing alone, would go back to the start after every HT. In a race, both stop at the first of the two patterns, so that difference never matters. Expected waiting times and race probabilities answer different questions.

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Project)** *Uses [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).*
Write code that estimates the expected number of fair coin flips until each of the
eight patterns of length 3 appears. Check HHT (8) and HTH (10) from H3. Which patterns
take longest, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from itertools import product

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, n_flips = 100_000, 300
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(n_trials, n_flips))     # 1 = H, 0 = T

for pattern in product("HT", repeat=3):
    code = [1 if c == "H" else 0 for c in pattern]
    hit = np.ones((n_trials, n_flips - 2), dtype=bool)
    for k in range(3):                                   # pattern ends at index i + 2
        hit &= flips[:, k : n_flips - 2 + k] == code[k]
    waits = hit.argmax(axis=1) + 3
    print(f"{''.join(pattern)}: {waits.mean():6.3f} flips on average")
```

Output:

```text
HHH: 14.066 flips on average
HHT:  7.981 flips on average
HTH: 10.005 flips on average
HTT:  8.011 flips on average
THH:  7.996 flips on average
THT: 10.032 flips on average
TTH:  8.016 flips on average
TTT: 13.935 flips on average
```

The exact answers are 14 for HHH and TTT, 10 for HTH and THT, and 8 for the other four. The patterns that overlap with themselves the most take the longest: after a near miss, they lose more progress.

</details>

**S2. (Project)** *Uses [14.5](14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md).*
Let $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and $Y = \sin(2\pi X) + \varepsilon$, where
$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2)$ is independent noise (variance $0.09$). Estimate
$E[Y \mid X]$ from 100,000 training points by splitting $[0, 1]$ into 20 bins and
averaging $Y$ in each bin. Measure the MSE of this estimate on fresh test data, and
compare it with the noise floor $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] = 0.09$ and with the
best constant guess.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, n_bins = 100_000, 20

def draw(size):
    x = rng.random(size)
    return x, np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, size)

x_train, y_train = draw(n)
x_test, y_test = draw(n)

# Estimate g(x) = E[Y | X = x] by the average of Y inside each bin of x.
bins_train = (x_train * n_bins).astype(int)
g_hat = np.array([y_train[bins_train == b].mean() for b in range(n_bins)])

pred = g_hat[(x_test * n_bins).astype(int)]
print(f"MSE of binned E[Y | X]: {np.mean((y_test - pred) ** 2):.4f}")
print(f"MSE of true E[Y | X]:   {np.mean((y_test - np.sin(2 * np.pi * x_test)) ** 2):.4f}  (floor 0.09)")
print(f"MSE of best constant:   {np.mean((y_test - y_train.mean()) ** 2):.4f}  (exact {0.5 + 0.09:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
MSE of binned E[Y | X]: 0.0940
MSE of true E[Y | X]:   0.0899  (floor 0.09)
MSE of best constant:   0.5904  (exact 0.5900)
```

The binned conditional mean gets close to the floor $0.09$; the small extra error comes from the curve changing inside each bin. The best constant pays the full $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{Var}(\sin 2\pi X) + 0.09 = 0.5 + 0.09 = 0.59$. No method can go below $0.09$.

</details>
