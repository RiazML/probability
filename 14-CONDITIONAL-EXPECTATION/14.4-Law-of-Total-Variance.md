# 14.4 Law of Total Variance

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 14: Conditional Expectation](README.md) / 14.4 Law of Total Variance

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [14.3 Law of Total Expectation](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)

> **Big idea:** The total spread of $Y$ is the average spread inside the groups plus the spread between the group averages: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X])$.

---

## Why It Matters

Why do exam scores vary? Partly because sections differ, partly because students
inside one section differ. Why does a model's prediction vary? Partly because of
noise in the data, partly because the model itself is unsure. The **law of total
variance** splits the variance of anything into these two parts, "within groups"
and "between groups". It is the base of analysis of variance (ANOVA), of the
"fraction of variance explained" ($R^2$) in regression, and of the variance of
random sums ([14.7](14.7-Random-Sums.md)).

## Intuition

Roll a fair die; $Y$ is the roll, with $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917$
([6.5](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)).
Split the rolls into low (1, 2, 3) and high (4, 5, 6), as in
[14.2](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md).

- **Between groups.** The group averages are 2 and 5. As a random variable,
  $E[Y \mid W]$ is 2 or 5 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Its mean is $3.5$ and
  its variance is $\frac{1}{2}(2 - 3.5)^2 + \frac{1}{2}(5 - 3.5)^2 = 2.25$.
- **Within groups.** Inside the low group the rolls 1, 2, 3 vary around 2, with
  variance $\frac{1 + 0 + 1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$. The high group 4, 5, 6 also has
  variance $\frac{2}{3}$. The average within-group variance is $\frac{2}{3}$.

Now add: $2.25 + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{27}{12} + \frac{8}{12} = \frac{35}{12}$. That is
exactly $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$. Every bit of the spread is either between the groups
or inside them.

Try the odd/even split instead. The averages 3 and 4 give between-variance
$\frac{1}{4}$. The groups $\lbrace 1, 3, 5 \rbrace$ and $\lbrace 2, 4, 6 \rbrace$ each
have variance $\frac{4 + 0 + 4}{3} = \frac{8}{3}$. Again
$\frac{1}{4} + \frac{8}{3} = \frac{3}{12} + \frac{32}{12} = \frac{35}{12}$. This split
**explains** much less of the spread: high/low tells you more about the roll than
odd/even does.

## Picture

```text
     Y      =    E[Y | W]     +    ( Y - E[Y | W] )
   (roll)       (between)            (within)

     1               2                  -1
     2               2                   0
     3               2                  +1
     4               5                  -1
     5               5                   0
     6               5                  +1

   Var: 35/12   =   9/4         +        2/3
        2.917       2.250               0.667
```

Each roll is its group average plus a leftover. The variances of the two parts add
up to the variance of the roll.

## Definition

The **conditional variance of $Y$ given $X = x$** is the variance of the conditional
distribution:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x) = E\big[(Y - E[Y \mid X = x])^2 \mid X = x\big] = E[Y^2 \mid X = x] - (E[Y \mid X = x])^2
$$

**In words:** how much $Y$ spreads around its group average, inside the group $X = x$.

As in [14.2](14.2-Conditional-Expectation-as-a-Random-Variable.md), if
$v(x) = \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x)$, then $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) = v(X)$ is a
random variable. The **law of total variance** (also called **Eve's law**) says,
for any $Y$ with finite variance:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \underbrace{E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big]}_{\text{within groups}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(E[Y \mid X]\big)}_{\text{between groups}}
$$

where:

- $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$ is the average of the group variances, weighted by the group probabilities,
- $\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X])$ is the variance of the group averages, again weighted by the group probabilities.

**In words:** total variance = average variance inside the groups + variance of
the group averages.

(The name comes from the letters: **E**xpected **V**ariance plus **V**ariance of the
**E**xpectation, "EVVE". The law of total expectation is "Adam's law".)

## Why It Is True

Write $g(X) = E[Y \mid X]$. Two facts are needed:

- the shortcut formula, used inside each group: $E[Y^2 \mid X] = \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) + g(X)^2$;
- the law of total expectation ([14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)),
  used twice: $E[Y] = E[g(X)]$ and $E[Y^2] = E\big[E[Y^2 \mid X]\big]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(Y) &= E[Y^2] - (E[Y])^2 && \text{(shortcut formula)} \\
&= E\big[E[Y^2 \mid X]\big] - \big(E[g(X)]\big)^2 && \text{(law of total expectation, twice)} \\
&= E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) + g(X)^2\big] - \big(E[g(X)]\big)^2 && \text{(shortcut inside each group)} \\
&= E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + \Big( E\big[g(X)^2\big] - \big(E[g(X)]\big)^2 \Big) && \text{(linearity, regroup)} \\
&= E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(g(X)\big) && \text{(shortcut formula for } g(X) \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Two consequences follow at once, because both parts are at least 0:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\big(E[Y \mid X]\big) \le \operatorname{Var}(Y), \qquad E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] \le \operatorname{Var}(Y)
$$

**In words:** the conditional mean is never more spread out than $Y$ itself, and on
average, knowing $X$ never increases the variance.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Exam Sections

**Problem.** Section A has 30 students with mean score 70 and standard deviation 10.
Section B has 20 students with mean 80 and standard deviation 5. Find the variance
and standard deviation of the score of a random student from the whole course.

**Solution.**

1. Let $X$ be the section: $P(A) = \frac{30}{50} = 0.6$ and $P(B) = 0.4$. From
   [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md), $E[Y] = 74$.
2. Within groups: the group variances are $10^2 = 100$ and $5^2 = 25$:

$$
E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] = 0.6 \times 100 + 0.4 \times 25 = 60 + 10 = 70
$$

3. Between groups: $E[Y \mid X]$ is 70 or 80, with mean 74:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\big(E[Y \mid X]\big) = 0.6 \times (70 - 74)^2 + 0.4 \times (80 - 74)^2 = 0.6 \times 16 + 0.4 \times 36 = 9.6 + 14.4 = 24
$$

4. Add: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 70 + 24 = 94$, so $\sigma = \sqrt{94} \approx 9.70$.

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 94$ and the standard deviation is about $9.70$ points.

**Check.** A common wrong answer is to average the standard deviations,
$0.6 \times 10 + 0.4 \times 5 = 8$. That misses the between-group part, and it
averages standard deviations instead of variances. The between part here is
$\frac{24}{94} \approx 0.26$ (about 26%) of the total: the section explains about a quarter of the spread.

### Example 2: A Coin With an Unknown Bias

**Problem.** As in [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md), Example 4: $P \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$
and, given $P = p$, $Y \sim \text{Bin}(10, p)$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ and compare it
with a fair coin, $\text{Bin}(10, \frac{1}{2})$.

**Solution.**

1. Given $P$, the binomial formulas give $E[Y \mid P] = 10P$ and
   $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid P) = 10P(1 - P)$.
2. For $P \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$: $E[P] = \frac{1}{2}$, $E[P^2] = \frac{1}{3}$, $\operatorname{Var}(P) = \frac{1}{12}$.
3. Within:

$$
E\big[10P(1 - P)\big] = 10\big(E[P] - E[P^2]\big) = 10 \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} \right) = \frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3}
$$

4. Between:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(10P) = 100 \operatorname{Var}(P) = \frac{100}{12} = \frac{25}{3}
$$

5. Add: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{5}{3} + \frac{25}{3} = \frac{30}{3} = 10$.

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 10$, four times the variance $10 \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = 2.5$
of a fair coin.

**Check.** Most of the variance ($\frac{25}{3}$ of 10) comes from not knowing the
coin. In fact $Y$ is uniform on $\lbrace 0, 1, \dots, 10 \rbrace$ here, and a discrete
uniform on 11 values has variance $\frac{11^2 - 1}{12} = 10$
([7.7 Discrete Uniform](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.7-Discrete-Uniform.md)). The
simulation agrees.

### Example 3: Noise Versus Model Uncertainty

**Problem.** An ensemble of machine learning models predicts tomorrow's temperature.
A randomly chosen model $X$ predicts a normal distribution. Model 1 (chosen with
probability $\frac{1}{2}$) says mean 20, variance 4. Model 2 (probability $\frac{1}{2}$) says
mean 26, variance 4. Find the total predictive variance, and the part due to the
models disagreeing.

**Solution.**

1. Within (the noise each model expects): $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] = \frac{1}{2} \times 4 + \frac{1}{2} \times 4 = 4$.
2. Between (the models disagree): the means 20 and 26 average to 23, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}\big(E[Y \mid X]\big) = \frac{1}{2}(20 - 23)^2 + \frac{1}{2}(26 - 23)^2 = \frac{9 + 9}{2} = 9
$$

3. Total: $4 + 9 = 13$.

**Answer.** Total predictive variance 13, of which 9 comes from model disagreement.

**Check.** In machine learning the first part is called **aleatoric** uncertainty
(noise that no model can remove) and the second **epistemic** uncertainty (lack of
knowledge, which more data can reduce). Here the models disagree a lot, so more
data would help.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2. It estimates both parts of the law and the total.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Random bias P, then 10 flips of a coin with that bias.
p = rng.random(n_trials)
y = rng.binomial(10, p)

within = np.mean(10 * p * (1 - p))     # E[Var(Y | P)]
between = np.var(10 * p)               # Var(E[Y | P])

print(f"Within  E[Var(Y | P)]: simulation {within:.4f}, exact {5 / 3:.4f}")
print(f"Between Var(E[Y | P]): simulation {between:.4f}, exact {25 / 3:.4f}")
print(f"Sum of the two parts:  simulation {within + between:.4f}, exact 10.0000")
print(f"Var(Y) directly:       simulation {np.var(y):.4f}, exact 10.0000")
```

Output:

```text
Within  E[Var(Y | P)]: simulation 1.6669, exact 1.6667
Between Var(E[Y | P]): simulation 8.3310, exact 8.3333
Sum of the two parts:  simulation 9.9979, exact 10.0000
Var(Y) directly:       simulation 10.0020, exact 10.0000
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the between-group part.** $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ is not just the
  average of the group variances. If the group means differ, that difference adds
  variance.
- **Averaging standard deviations.** The law adds **variances**. Convert standard
  deviations to variances first, then take the square root at the end.
- **Using unweighted averages.** Both parts are weighted by the group probabilities,
  like everything in [14.3](14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md).
- **Thinking $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)$ is a number.** It is a random variable,
  one value per group. The law uses its **mean**.
- **Expecting conditioning to reduce variance in every group.** On average it does,
  but one group can have a larger variance than $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$. Only the
  weighted average is guaranteed to be smaller.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** $R^2 = \operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) / \operatorname{Var}(Y)$
  for the true regression function is the "fraction of variance explained" by the
  features. Ensembles and Bayesian models split predictive variance into aleatoric
  and epistemic parts, as in Example 3.
- **Statistics:** **ANOVA** (analysis of variance) tests whether group means differ by
  comparing the between part with the within part.
- **Mixtures:** the "inside plus between" formula of
  [10.8 Mixture Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)
  is this law with $X$ = the hidden group.
- **Insurance and queues:** the variance of a total built from a random number of
  parts comes from this law ([14.7](14.7-Random-Sums.md)).
- **Everyday life:** if prices vary a lot between shops but little inside each shop,
  choosing the shop matters more than choosing the day.

$\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) = 5$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$, the standard deviation of
$Y$, and the fraction of the variance explained by $X$.

$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 4 + 5 = 9$, so the standard deviation is 3. The fraction explained is $\frac{5}{9} \approx 0.556$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A die is split into odd and even rolls. Verify the law of total
variance with the numbers in the Intuition, writing every step.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Within: each group has variance $\frac{8}{3}$, so $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] = \frac{8}{3}$. Between: $E[Y \mid X]$ is 3 or 4 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, mean $3.5$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) = \tfrac{1}{2}(3 - 3.5)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(4 - 3.5)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$$

$$\tfrac{8}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{32}{12} + \tfrac{3}{12} = \tfrac{35}{12} = \operatorname{Var}(Y)$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the laptop table of
[14.1](14.1-Conditional-Expectation-Given-a-Value.md) (row 1: $0.30, 0.15, 0.05$;
row 2: $0.10, 0.20, 0.20$ for $Y = 0, 1, 2$), find $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 1)$,
$\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 2)$, and check the law.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Row 1 (conditional PMF $0.6, 0.3, 0.1$): $E[Y^2 \mid X = 1] = 0.3 + 4(0.1) = 0.7$, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 1) = 0.7 - 0.5^2 = 0.45$. Row 2 (PMF $0.2, 0.4, 0.4$): $E[Y^2 \mid X = 2] = 0.4 + 4(0.4) = 2.0$, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = 2) = 2.0 - 1.2^2 = 0.56$.

$$E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) = 0.5(0.45) + 0.5(0.56) + 0.5(0.5 - 0.85)^2 + 0.5(1.2 - 0.85)^2 = 0.505 + 0.1225 = 0.6275$$

Directly: $E[Y^2] = 0.35 + 4(0.25) = 1.35$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 1.35 - 0.85^2 = 0.6275$. They agree.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A response time is a cache hit with probability $0.8$ (mean 1 ms,
variance 1) or a miss with probability $0.2$ (mean 10 ms, variance 100). Find
$\operatorname{Var}(T)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $0.8(1) + 0.2(10) = 2.8$. Within: $0.8(1) + 0.2(100) = 20.8$. Between:

$$0.8(1 - 2.8)^2 + 0.2(10 - 2.8)^2 = 0.8(3.24) + 0.2(51.84) = 2.592 + 10.368 = 12.96$$

So $\operatorname{Var}(T) = 20.8 + 12.96 = 33.76$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ ($\lambda$ is the mean) and, given
$X = n$, let $Y \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$. Use the two laws to find $E[Y]$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(Y)$. When is $\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) = \operatorname{Var}(Y)$ in
general?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$E[Y \mid X] = Xp$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) = Xp(1 - p)$. So $E[Y] = p E[X] = \lambda p$ and

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = E[Xp(1 - p)] + \operatorname{Var}(Xp) = \lambda p(1 - p) + p^2 \lambda = \lambda p$$

Mean equal to variance fits the fact that $Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda p)$. In general, $\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) = \operatorname{Var}(Y)$ exactly when $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] = 0$, that is, when $Y$ has no spread inside any group: $Y$ is a function of $X$ (with probability 1).

</details>

## Summary

- $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x)$ is the spread of $Y$ inside the group $X = x$;
  $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)$ is a random variable.
- Total variance = average within-group variance + variance of the group means.
- Conditioning never increases variance on average, and $E[Y \mid X]$ is never more
  spread than $Y$.
- The between part over the total is the fraction of variance that $X$ explains.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(Y) = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(E[Y \mid X]\big)}
$$
