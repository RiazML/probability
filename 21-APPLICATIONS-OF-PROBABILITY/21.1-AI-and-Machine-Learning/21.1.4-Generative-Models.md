> **Big idea:** A generative model is a probability distribution that you can sample from, usually built in two steps (first draw a hidden cause $z$, then draw the data $x$ given $z$), and every question about it, from "how likely is this data?" to "what hidden cause made it?", is the law of total probability or Bayes' theorem.

---

## Why It Matters

A **classifier** ([21.1.1](21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md)) answers "what label goes with this input?". A **generative model** answers a bigger question: "how is the data itself produced?" Once you have a model of how the data are produced, you can create new data (new faces, new sentences, new sounds), you can score how typical a data point is, and you can work backward from data to a hidden cause. Modern image and text generators are very large generative models. Under the size, they use the same few probability ideas from earlier modules: mixtures, the Gaussian, conditioning and sampling.

## Intuition

Imagine a bakery that sells two kinds of loaves, small and big. Each morning, a baker picks the kind (small 40% of the time, big 60%), and then bakes a loaf whose weight is random around the typical weight of that kind. You only see the weights. The weights of all loaves form a histogram with two humps. The baker's **hidden choice** (small or big) is the **latent variable**. You never see it, but it explains why the data have two humps.

Two questions come up at once. **Forward:** how do I make a new loaf weight? Pick the kind, then draw a weight. **Backward:** a loaf weighs 1.5 units; which kind was it probably? That is Bayes' theorem, with the kind as the unknown. The same two questions are asked in every generative model. In an image model the hidden cause is a short list of numbers, and the data is a whole picture.

## Picture

```text
  FORWARD (sampling)                      BACKWARD (inference)

   hidden z      -- draw -->    data x     data x   -- Bayes -->  P(z | x)
   z ~ p(z)                     x ~ p(x|z)

   Gaussian mixture: p(x) = 0.4 N(0, 1) + 0.6 N(4, 1)

   |                    ###
   |                   #####
   |        ###       #######
   |       #####     ########
   |     #########  ##########
   |   #########################
   +-------------------------------> x
       -2    0     2     4     6
```

Left: draw the hidden $z$ first, then the data. Right: given the data, find the hidden cause. Bottom: two small bells mixed together give two humps.

## Definition

A **generative model** is a probability distribution $p(x)$ over data $x$ from which we can draw samples. A **latent variable model** builds $p(x)$ from a hidden variable $z$, the **latent variable**, in two steps: draw $z$ from a **prior** $p(z)$, then draw $x$ from the **likelihood** $p(x \mid z)$. This is called **ancestral sampling**. The three quantities that matter are:

$$
p(x) = \sum_{z} p(x \mid z) \thinspace p(z) \ \ \text{(or } \int p(x \mid z) \thinspace p(z) \thinspace dz \text{)}, \qquad p(z \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid z) \thinspace p(z)}{p(x)}
$$

where $p(x)$ is the **marginal likelihood** (also called the evidence) and $p(z \mid x)$ is the **posterior** ([10.7](../../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)).

**In words:** the chance of the data is an average of the chance of the data given each hidden cause, weighted by how likely each cause is; and the posterior asks which causes explain this data.

Two models are simple enough to solve completely.

**Model A: the Gaussian mixture.** The latent $z$ is a category with $P(z = k) = \pi_k$, and $x \mid z = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$ ([10.8](../../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md)). The posterior probabilities $r_k(x) = P(z = k \mid x)$ are called **responsibilities**:

$$
p(x) = \sum_{k} \pi_k \thinspace \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2), \qquad r_k(x) = \frac{\pi_k \thinspace \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{m} \pi_m \thinspace \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_m, \sigma_m^2)}
$$

where $\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2)$ means the normal density at $x$.

**Model B: the linear Gaussian model.** The latent $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ is a single number, and the data is a $d$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{w} z + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, where $\mathbf{w}$ is a fixed vector and the noise is $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 I)$, independent of $z$. Then:

$$
\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^T + \sigma^2 I), \qquad z \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \frac{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2}, \ \frac{\sigma^2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2} \right)
$$

**In words:** the data lie along the direction $\mathbf{w}$ with a little noise around it, and the best guess of the hidden $z$ is a scaled projection of $\mathbf{x}$ on $\mathbf{w}$. The posterior is narrower than the prior (variance below 1), because the data carry information.

Two tools from the probability of the model are used often. The **reparametrization trick** writes a draw from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ as $z = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, so all randomness sits in a fixed noise variable. The **evidence lower bound** (ELBO) says that for any distribution $q(z)$ that we choose:

$$
\ln p(x) \ge E_{q}\left[ \ln \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} \right]
$$

with equality exactly when $q(z) = p(z \mid x)$. A **variational autoencoder** (VAE) is the latent variable model where $p(x \mid z)$ and $q(z)$ are computed by neural networks and the ELBO is the training objective. Only the probability model is described here.

## Why It Is True

**The marginal and the posterior.** The formula for $p(x)$ is the law of total probability ([4.3](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)) over the partition given by the values of $z$. The posterior is Bayes' theorem. Nothing else is assumed.

**Mean and variance of a mixture.** Use the law of total expectation and Eve's law ([14.3](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md), [14.4](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md)), conditioning on the hidden $z$:

$$
E[x] = E[E[x \mid z]] = \sum_k \pi_k \mu_k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(x) = E[\operatorname{Var}(x \mid z)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[x \mid z]) = \sum_k \pi_k \sigma_k^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\mu_z)
$$

**In words:** the spread of the data is the average spread inside each component plus the spread of the component centres.

**Model B: the distribution of $\mathbf{x}$.** The vector $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{w} z + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is a linear function of the independent Gaussian vector $(z, \boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$, so it is Gaussian ([12.5](../../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.5-Affine-Transformations.md)). Its mean is $\mathbf{0}$. Its covariance, using $z \perp \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(z) = 1$ ([11.7](../../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.7-Linear-Transformations.md)), is:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w} \operatorname{Var}(z) \mathbf{w}^T + \sigma^2 I = \mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^T + \sigma^2 I
$$

**Model B: the posterior.** The pair $(z, \mathbf{x})$ is jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}(z, \mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{Cov}(z, \mathbf{w} z + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \mathbf{w}^T$. The Gaussian conditioning formula ([12.7](../../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.7-Conditionals.md)) gives a conditional mean $\mathbf{w}^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{x}$ and a conditional variance $1 - \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{w}$, with $\Sigma = \mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^T + \sigma^2 I$. To avoid inverting $\Sigma$, multiply $\Sigma$ by $\mathbf{w}^T$ from the left:

$$
\mathbf{w}^T \Sigma = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w} \mathbf{w}^T + \sigma^2 \mathbf{w}^T = (\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2) \mathbf{w}^T \implies \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{\mathbf{w}^T}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2}
$$

So the conditional mean is $\frac{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2}$, and the conditional variance is $1 - \frac{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2}$. This is the claim.

**The reparametrization trick.** If $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ then $\mu + \sigma \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ for $\sigma \gt 0$, because an affine map of a Gaussian is Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ ([9.2](../../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)). This is also how a computer makes any normal draw from a standard one.

**The ELBO.** Write $p(x) = \sum_z p(x, z) = \sum_z q(z) \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} = E_q\left[ \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} \right]$. Since $\ln$ is concave, Jensen's inequality ([16.3](../../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)) gives:

$$
\ln p(x) = \ln E_q\left[ \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} \right] \ge E_q\left[ \ln \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} \right]
$$

Equality holds when the ratio is constant. If $q(z) = p(z \mid x)$, the ratio $\frac{p(x, z)}{p(z \mid x)} = p(x)$ is constant, so the bound is tight. Raising the ELBO moves $q$ toward the true posterior.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Two-Hump Mixture

**Problem.** A mixture has $\pi = (0.4, 0.6)$, with components $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(4, 1)$. (a) Find the mean and variance of $x$. (b) A data point is $x = 1.5$. Find the probability that it came from the first component.

**Solution.**

1. Mean: $E[x] = 0.4 \times 0 + 0.6 \times 4 = 2.4$.
2. Variance. Inside each component the variance is 1, so the average is 1. The centres are 0 (probability 0.4) and 4 (probability 0.6), which is a two-point distribution with variance $0.4 \times 0.6 \times (4 - 0)^2 = 3.84$. Add:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(x) = 1 + 3.84 = 4.84
$$

3. The normal density is $\varphi(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2}$. At $x = 1.5$: the first component has $\varphi(1.5) = 0.1295$ and the second has $\varphi(1.5 - 4) = \varphi(-2.5) = 0.0175$.
4. Weight by the priors: $0.4 \times 0.1295 = 0.05181$ and $0.6 \times 0.0175 = 0.01052$.
5. Normalize:

$$
r_1(1.5) = \frac{0.05181}{0.05181 + 0.01052} = \frac{0.05181}{0.06233} \approx 0.831
$$

**Answer.** $E[x] = 2.4$, $\operatorname{Var}(x) = 4.84$, and $r_1(1.5) \approx 0.831$.

**Check.** The point 1.5 is 1.5 units from the first centre and 2.5 from the second, so it favours the first component even though that component has a smaller prior. The simulation below agrees with all three numbers.

### Example 2: Inverting a Linear Gaussian Model

**Problem.** Take $\mathbf{w} = (1, 2)$, $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. (a) Find $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{x})$ and the correlation of the two coordinates. (b) We observe $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$. Find the posterior of $z$.

**Solution.**

1. $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 = 1 + 4 = 5$. The matrix $\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^T$ has entries $1, 2, 2, 4$, so:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \cr 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \cr 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \cr 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

2. The correlation is $\rho = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2 \times 5}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.632$.
3. The posterior mean is $\frac{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2} = \frac{1 \times 1 + 2 \times 2}{5 + 1} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833$.
4. The posterior variance is $\frac{\sigma^2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + \sigma^2} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$.

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{x})$ has rows $(2, 2)$ and $(2, 5)$, and the correlation is about 0.632. The posterior is $z \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.833, 0.167)$.

**Check.** The prior of $z$ had mean 0 and variance 1. After seeing a point in the direction of $\mathbf{w}$, the belief moves up to 0.833 and the variance shrinks six times. The simulation finds the same slopes and residual variance.

### Example 3: The ELBO in a Tiny Model

**Problem.** A hidden $z \in \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$ has $p(z) = (0.4, 0.6)$. For the observed data $x$, the likelihoods are $p(x \mid z = 1) = 0.5$ and $p(x \mid z = 2) = 0.1$. Compute $\ln p(x)$, and the ELBO for $q = (0.5, 0.5)$ and for $q$ equal to the posterior.

**Solution.**

1. The joint values are $p(x, 1) = 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.2$ and $p(x, 2) = 0.6 \times 0.1 = 0.06$. So $p(x) = 0.26$ and $\ln p(x) = -1.347$.
2. With $q = (0.5, 0.5)$:

$$
\text{ELBO} = 0.5 \ln \frac{0.2}{0.5} + 0.5 \ln \frac{0.06}{0.5} = 0.5 (-0.916) + 0.5 (-2.120) = -1.518
$$

3. The posterior is $\left( \frac{0.2}{0.26}, \frac{0.06}{0.26} \right) = (0.769, 0.231)$. Each ratio $\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}$ equals $0.26$, so the ELBO is $\ln 0.26 = -1.347$.

**Answer.** $\ln p(x) = -1.347$. The ELBO is $-1.518$ for the uniform $q$, and exactly $-1.347$ for the posterior.

**Check.** $-1.518 \le -1.347$, as the bound requires, and the gap $0.171$ is the KL divergence of $q$ from the posterior (it is the same quantity as in [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)).

## Simulate It

The code samples from both models with one million draws each. It checks the mixture mean, variance and responsibility at $x = 1.5$ (using a thin window around 1.5), and for Model B it checks the covariance of $\mathbf{x}$ and the best linear guess of $z$ from $\mathbf{x}$.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Model A: Gaussian mixture. Pick a component z, then draw x given z.
pi = np.array([0.4, 0.6])
mu = np.array([0.0, 4.0])                      # both components have standard deviation 1
z = rng.choice(2, size=n, p=pi)
x = rng.normal(mu[z], 1.0)
print(f"Mixture mean: {x.mean():.3f} (exact 2.400), variance: {x.var():.3f} (exact 4.840)")

# Responsibility P(z = 0 | x near 1.5): keep draws with x in a thin window around 1.5.
near = np.abs(x - 1.5) < 0.02
exact_r = pi[0] * norm.pdf(1.5, 0, 1) / (pi[0] * norm.pdf(1.5, 0, 1) + pi[1] * norm.pdf(1.5, 4, 1))
print(f"P(z=0 | x=1.5): simulated {np.mean(z[near] == 0):.4f}, exact {exact_r:.4f}")

# Model B: z ~ N(0, 1) and x = w z + noise, noise ~ N(0, sigma^2 I) in 2-D.
w, sigma2 = np.array([1.0, 2.0]), 1.0
z = rng.normal(size=n)
x = np.outer(z, w) + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(sigma2), size=(n, 2))
print("Cov(x):", np.round(np.cov(x.T), 2).tolist(), "(exact [[2, 2], [2, 5]])")

# Best linear guess of z from x: slope should be w / (|w|^2 + sigma^2), residual variance sigma^2 / (|w|^2 + sigma^2).
slope = np.linalg.lstsq(x, z, rcond=None)[0]
resid_var = np.var(z - x @ slope)
print(f"Slope: {np.round(slope, 3).tolist()} (exact [0.167, 0.333])")
print(f"Residual variance: {resid_var:.3f} (exact 0.167)")
```

Output:

```text
Mixture mean: 2.402 (exact 2.400), variance: 4.839 (exact 4.840)
P(z=0 | x=1.5): simulated 0.8297, exact 0.8313
Cov(x): [[1.99, 2.0], [2.0, 5.0]] (exact [[2, 2], [2, 5]])
Slope: [0.166, 0.333] (exact [0.167, 0.333])
Residual variance: 0.167 (exact 0.167)
```

All five lines agree with the formulas. The slope and residual variance are the posterior mean coefficients and the posterior variance of Example 2, because for jointly Gaussian variables the best linear guess is the conditional mean ([14.5](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)).

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the latent variable as observed.** The hidden $z$ is not in the data. To get $p(x)$ we must sum or integrate over it. Forgetting the sum gives a wrong likelihood.
- **Averaging the component variances only.** The variance of a mixture also has the spread of the centres (Example 1: $1 + 3.84$, not 1). This is Eve's law.
- **Thinking a mixture of two bells is one bell.** A mixture of Gaussians is not Gaussian. Only a sum of Gaussian variables is Gaussian.
- **Confusing $p(z \mid x)$ with $p(x \mid z)$.** The first is the posterior (inference). The second is the likelihood (generation). They are linked by Bayes' theorem, not equal.
- **Expecting a tight ELBO for free.** The bound is exact only when $q$ is the true posterior. A poor $q$ gives a loose bound.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** Gaussian mixture models cluster data. Variational autoencoders and other latent models generate images and sounds. Models that add Gaussian noise in many small steps and learn to remove it use the fact that a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian ([12.9](../../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md)).
- **Statistics:** factor analysis and probabilistic PCA are Model B with more latent variables. Clustering is a mixture with an unknown component.
- **Simulation:** sampling from a hard distribution by first sampling a simple latent variable, as in [Module 19](../../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md) and in the Box-Muller transform ([13.8](../../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md)).
- **Hidden Markov models** in speech and biology are latent variable models where the hidden variable is a Markov chain ([Module 18](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/README.md)).
- **Everyday life:** every time you reason from an observation to a hidden cause ("the lights are off, so probably a power cut"), you are inverting a generative story.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A mixture has $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$ with components $\mathcal{N}(-1, 1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(3, 1)$. Find $E[x]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(x)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[x] = 0.5 \times (-1) + 0.5 \times 3 = 1$$

The centres $-1$ and $3$ each have probability $\frac{1}{2}$, so their variance is $0.5 \times 0.5 \times 4^2 = 4$. Inside the components the variance is 1:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x) = 1 + 4 = 5$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Let $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Use the reparametrization $z = 1 + 2\varepsilon$. What is the distribution of $z$, and what is $P(z \gt 3)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$z \sim \mathcal{N}(1, 4)$ (mean 1, variance $2^2 = 4$). The event $z \gt 3$ means $1 + 2\varepsilon \gt 3$, which is $\varepsilon \gt 1$:

$$P(z \gt 3) = P(\varepsilon \gt 1) \approx 0.1587$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In Model A of Example 1, find the responsibility $r_1(2)$ at the midpoint $x = 2$. What would it be if the priors were $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

At $x = 2$, both centres are 2 units away (0 and 4). So $\varphi(2) = \varphi(-2) = 0.0540$ for both components. With $\pi = (0.4, 0.6)$:

$$r_1(2) = \frac{0.4 \times 0.0540}{0.4 \times 0.0540 + 0.6 \times 0.0540} = \frac{0.4}{1} = 0.4$$

At the midpoint the likelihoods cancel, so the responsibility equals the prior. With $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$ it is $0.5$ at the midpoint.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In Model B with $\mathbf{w} = (1, 2)$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$, find the posterior mean of $z$ when $\mathbf{x} = (0, 3)$. What happens to the posterior variance if the noise grows to $\sigma^2 = 5$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} = 0 + 6 = 6$, so the posterior mean is $\frac{6}{5 + 1} = 1$ and the variance is $\frac{1}{6}$.

With $\sigma^2 = 5$ the variance is $\frac{5}{5 + 5} = 0.5$, and the posterior mean of the same data becomes $\frac{6}{10} = 0.6$. More noise means the data say less about $z$, so the posterior stays closer to the prior (mean 0, variance 1).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that, in any latent variable model, the gap between $\ln p(x)$ and the ELBO equals the KL divergence $D(q \Vert p(\cdot \mid x)) = \sum_z q(z) \ln \frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x)}$, for a discrete $z$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Start from $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x) p(x)$. Then:

$$\text{ELBO} = \sum_z q(z) \ln \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} = \sum_z q(z) \ln \frac{p(z \mid x)}{q(z)} + \sum_z q(z) \ln p(x)$$

The second sum is $\ln p(x)$, because $q$ sums to 1. The first sum is $-D(q \Vert p(\cdot \mid x))$. So:

$$\ln p(x) - \text{ELBO} = D(q \Vert p(\cdot \mid x)) \ge 0$$

This is never negative (see [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)), and it is 0 only when $q = p(\cdot \mid x)$. In Example 3 the gap is $-1.347 - (-1.518) = 0.171$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check that $z = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon$ with $\mu = 1$ and $\sigma = 2$ gives $P(z \gt 3) \approx 0.1587$ by drawing a million values of $\varepsilon$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
epsilon = rng.normal(size=1_000_000)
z = 1 + 2 * epsilon                      # reparametrization: all randomness is in epsilon
print(f"Mean {z.mean():.3f}, variance {z.var():.3f}, P(z > 3) = {np.mean(z > 3):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Mean 1.000, variance 4.004, P(z > 3) = 0.1586
```

The values are close to the exact mean 1, variance 4 and probability 0.1587.

</details>

## Summary

- A generative model is a distribution you can sample from. Latent variable models draw a hidden $z$ first, then the data $x$ given $z$.
- The chance of the data is a total probability over the hidden cause, and the hidden cause given the data is a Bayes posterior.
- A Gaussian mixture has mean $\sum_k \pi_k \mu_k$, a variance that includes the spread of its centres, and responsibilities from Bayes.
- In the linear Gaussian model, everything stays Gaussian: $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^T + \sigma^2 I)$ and the posterior of $z$ is Gaussian with the closed form above.
- The ELBO is a lower bound on $\ln p(x)$ from Jensen's inequality. The gap is a KL divergence, which is 0 for the true posterior.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{p(x) = \sum_{z} p(x \mid z) \thinspace p(z), \qquad p(z \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid z) \thinspace p(z)}{p(x)}}
$$
