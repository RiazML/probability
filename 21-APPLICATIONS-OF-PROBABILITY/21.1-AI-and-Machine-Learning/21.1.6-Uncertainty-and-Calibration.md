> **Big idea:** A probability is only useful if it means what it says: a model is calibrated when, among all cases where it says "70%", about 70% really happen, and the Brier score splits into a calibration part and a part that measures how much the model knows.

---

## Why It Matters

A classifier says "this scan shows a tumour with probability 0.9". A doctor will decide what to do using that number. If the model says 0.9 but is right only 70% of the time in such cases, the number is misleading, even if the model's top choice is often correct. **Calibration** checks whether predicted probabilities match real frequencies. This is the frequency meaning of probability ([3.1](../../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.1-Interpretations-of-Probability.md)) turned into a test. The same idea scores weather forecasts and tells us when to trust a model.

## Intuition

A weather forecaster says "70% chance of rain" on many days. Collect all those days. If it rained on about 70 of 100 of them, the forecaster is honest about the 70%. If it rained on only 55 of them, the forecaster is **overconfident**: the stated 70% was too high. A forecaster who says "30%" on days when it rains half the time is **underconfident** in the other direction.

Notice that calibration is not the same as being useful. A forecaster who says "44% chance of rain" every single day, in a city where it rains on 44% of days, is perfectly calibrated. But the forecast tells you nothing about tomorrow. A good forecast must be calibrated **and** must separate the rainy days from the dry days. The Brier score measures both at once.

A second question is where the uncertainty comes from. Some of it is noise that no model can remove: even with perfect knowledge, a fair coin is 50-50. Some of it is lack of knowledge: several good models disagree because they saw little data. An **ensemble** of models, whose disagreement can be measured, tells these two apart.

## Picture

```text
  observed
  frequency
   1.0 |                                        .
   0.9 |                                    .
   0.8 |                                .   o
   0.7 |                            .
   0.6 |                        .   o
   0.5 |                    o
   0.4 |            o   .
   0.3 |            .
   0.2 |    o   .
   0.1 |    .
   0.0 |.
       +---------------------------------------------> predicted probability
        0                   0.5                 1

   . = perfect calibration (the diagonal)      o = an overconfident model
```

Each "o" is a group of cases with the same predicted probability. For a calibrated model every "o" would sit on the diagonal. This model says 0.9 but is right only 80% of the time, and says 0.1 when the real frequency is 20%.

## Definition

Let $Y \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$ be the true outcome, and let $\hat{p}$ be the probability of $Y = 1$ that a model reports. The model is **calibrated** if:

$$
P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{p} = p) = p \qquad \text{for every } p
$$

**In words:** among all cases where the model says $p$, the fraction of "yes" is $p$. Equivalently, $E[Y \mid \hat{p}] = \hat{p}$, the conditional expectation of Module 14.

To check this with data, group cases into **bins** by $\hat{p}$ and compare, in each bin $b$, the average prediction (the **confidence**, $\text{conf}_b$) with the fraction of "yes" (the **accuracy**, $\text{acc}_b$). The plot of $\text{acc}_b$ against $\text{conf}_b$ is the **reliability diagram** of the Picture. The **expected calibration error** (ECE) summarizes it:

$$
\text{ECE} = \sum_{b} \frac{n_b}{n} \thinspace \lvert \text{acc}_b - \text{conf}_b \rvert
$$

where $n_b$ is the number of cases in bin $b$ and $n$ is the total.

**In words:** the average gap between what the model said and what happened, weighted by how many cases are in each bin.

The **Brier score** is the mean squared error of the probability, and it has a neat split. Let $q(\hat{p}) = E[Y \mid \hat{p}]$ be the true frequency among cases with report $\hat{p}$:

$$
E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2] = \underbrace{E[(\hat{p} - q(\hat{p}))^2]}_{\text{calibration error}} + \underbrace{E[q(\hat{p})(1 - q(\hat{p}))]}_{\text{refinement}}
$$

**In words:** the Brier score is the calibration error (how far the reports are from the true frequencies) plus the refinement (the unavoidable noise left after the reports have been used). Smaller is better for both.

To split uncertainty, take an ensemble of $M$ models, where model $m$ gives probability $p_m$ for "yes". Pick a model at random, each with weight $\frac{1}{M}$. With $\bar{p}$ their average:

$$
\underbrace{\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m} p_m (1 - p_m)}_{\text{noise inside each model (aleatoric)}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(p_m)}_{\text{disagreement (epistemic)}}
$$

**In words:** the total uncertainty about a yes/no outcome is the average noise that each model admits, plus the variance of the models' answers.

## Why It Is True

**The Brier decomposition.** Condition on the reported value $\hat{p}$. Given $\hat{p}$, the outcome $Y$ is Bernoulli with success probability $q = q(\hat{p})$, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid \hat{p}) = q(1 - q)$ ([7.1](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.1-Bernoulli.md)). For a random variable, $E[(Y - c)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Y) + (E[Y] - c)^2$ for a constant $c$ ([6.10](../../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md)). Here $c = \hat{p}$ is a constant once we condition on it, so:

$$
E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2 \mid \hat{p}] = q(1 - q) + (q - \hat{p})^2
$$

Take the expectation over $\hat{p}$ (the tower rule, [14.3](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.3-Law-of-Total-Expectation.md)) and the formula follows. The same computation works on a data set, exactly, when we group the cases by the reported value: in each group with accuracy $\text{acc}$ and report $v$, the mean of $(v - y)^2$ is $(v - \text{acc})^2 + \text{acc}(1 - \text{acc})$.

**Honest reports are best.** The total is smallest when the calibration term is zero, which happens exactly when $\hat{p} = q(\hat{p})$. This is the best-predictor result of [14.5](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md): under squared error, the best prediction of $Y$ is $E[Y \mid \text{what you know}]$. So the Brier score is a **proper** score: it rewards a forecaster for saying what they really believe.

**Calibrated is not enough.** A forecaster who always says the base rate $\pi = P(Y = 1)$ has $q = \pi$ for all cases, so the calibration term is 0 and the refinement is $\pi (1 - \pi)$, which is the largest refinement possible. A better forecast uses information to make the $q$ values spread out toward 0 and 1 (then $q(1 - q)$ is small) while staying calibrated.

**The ensemble identity.** Let the model index be a random variable $M$, uniform on $\lbrace 1, \dots, M \rbrace$. The outcome given the model is Bernoulli($p_M$), so $E[Y \mid M] = p_M$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid M) = p_M (1 - p_M)$. Eve's law ([14.4](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.4-Law-of-Total-Variance.md)) says:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y) = E[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid M)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid M]) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_m p_m (1 - p_m) + \operatorname{Var}(p_M)
$$

and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})$ because the mixed prediction is Bernoulli($\bar{p}$). This is the identity of the Definition.

**Fixing overconfidence with one number.** Many models report $\hat{p} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ for a score $z$. If the scores are too large, dividing by a constant $T \gt 1$ shrinks the reports toward $\frac{1}{2}$. Choose $T$ to minimize the log loss of [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md) on held-out data. This **temperature scaling** changes no ranking, only the probabilities. The log loss is a proper score, so its minimizer is the calibrating value.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Reading a Reliability Table

**Problem.** A model made 1,000 predictions. They fall in five bins:

| Bin confidence | Cases | "Yes" cases |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 200 | 40 |
| 0.3 | 300 | 120 |
| 0.5 | 200 | 100 |
| 0.7 | 200 | 120 |
| 0.9 | 100 | 80 |

Find the accuracy of each bin and the ECE. Is the model over- or underconfident?

**Solution.**

1. Accuracy is "yes" divided by cases: $\frac{40}{200} = 0.2$, $\frac{120}{300} = 0.4$, $\frac{100}{200} = 0.5$, $\frac{120}{200} = 0.6$, $\frac{80}{100} = 0.8$.
2. The gaps $\lvert \text{acc} - \text{conf} \rvert$ are $0.1, 0.1, 0, 0.1, 0.1$.
3. Weight each gap by the share of cases, $\frac{n_b}{1000}$:

$$
\text{ECE} = 0.2 \times 0.1 + 0.3 \times 0.1 + 0.2 \times 0 + 0.2 \times 0.1 + 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.02 + 0.03 + 0 + 0.02 + 0.01 = 0.08
$$

**Answer.** The accuracies are $0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8$ and ECE $= 0.08$. The model is overconfident: at the high end (0.7 and 0.9) it is right less often than it says, and at the low end (0.1 and 0.3) the "yes" cases are more common than it says. These are the points of the Picture.

**Check.** On average the model is off by 8 percentage points. The mid bin is exactly right. All the gaps are on the "too extreme" side, which is the typical pattern of overconfidence.

### Example 2: Brier Score and Its Two Parts

**Problem.** Use the data of Example 1, and treat every case in a bin as having exactly the bin's confidence. Compute the Brier score, its calibration part and its refinement part.

**Solution.**

1. In each bin, the mean squared error is $(\text{conf} - \text{acc})^2 + \text{acc}(1 - \text{acc})$. Bin 0.1: $0.01 + 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.17$. Bin 0.3: $0.01 + 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.25$. Bin 0.5: $0 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$. Bin 0.7: $0.01 + 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.25$. Bin 0.9: $0.01 + 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.17$.
2. Weight by the bin shares:

$$
\text{Brier} = 0.2 \times 0.17 + 0.3 \times 0.25 + 0.2 \times 0.25 + 0.2 \times 0.25 + 0.1 \times 0.17 = 0.034 + 0.075 + 0.05 + 0.05 + 0.017 = 0.226
$$

3. The calibration part: every bin except the middle has $(\text{conf} - \text{acc})^2 = 0.01$, and these bins hold 80% of the cases. So the calibration part is $0.8 \times 0.01 = 0.008$.
4. The refinement part is $0.226 - 0.008 = 0.218$.
5. For comparison, the overall "yes" rate is $\frac{40 + 120 + 100 + 120 + 80}{1000} = 0.46$. A forecaster who always says 0.46 has Brier score $0.46 \times 0.54 = 0.2484$.

**Answer.** Brier $= 0.226$, made of $0.008$ calibration error and $0.218$ refinement. This beats the constant forecaster's $0.2484$.

**Check.** Recalibrating the bins (reporting 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8) would remove the $0.008$ and leave $0.218$. The model has some skill: it separates cases a little. The simulation below checks the same split on a large data set.

### Example 3: Two Kinds of Uncertainty in an Ensemble

**Problem.** Three models give the probability of "yes" as $0.9$, $0.5$ and $0.1$. Split the total uncertainty into noise and disagreement.

**Solution.**

1. The average is $\bar{p} = \frac{0.9 + 0.5 + 0.1}{3} = 0.5$, so the total is $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$.
2. The noise inside each model: $p(1 - p)$ is $0.09$, $0.25$ and $0.09$. The average is $\frac{0.43}{3} \approx 0.1433$.
3. The disagreement: the variance of $0.9, 0.5, 0.1$ around 0.5 is $\frac{0.16 + 0 + 0.16}{3} \approx 0.1067$.
4. Check: $0.1433 + 0.1067 = 0.25$.

**Answer.** Total $0.25$ = noise $0.1433$ + disagreement $0.1067$.

**Check.** If all three models said 0.5, the disagreement would be 0 and the noise would be the whole 0.25. If they said 0.9, 0.9 and 0.9, both the disagreement and the total would be small (total $0.09$). More data would shrink the disagreement but not the noise.

## Simulate It

Each case has a true chance $q$ of "yes" (drawn from a $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ distribution, so cases differ). The model reports an overconfident probability: it doubles the log-odds of $q$. The code measures ECE and Brier score, fixes the model with temperature scaling (choosing $T$ by log loss), and checks the exact Brier split.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 200_000

def sigmoid(t): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t))
def logit(p): return np.log(p / (1 - p))

# The true chance of "yes" is q. The model is overconfident: it doubles the log-odds.
q = rng.beta(2, 2, size=n)
y = rng.random(n) < q
p_over = sigmoid(2 * logit(q))

def ece(p, y, n_bins=10):
    bins = np.minimum((p * n_bins).astype(int), n_bins - 1)
    return sum(np.mean(bins == b) * abs(p[bins == b].mean() - y[bins == b].mean())
               for b in range(n_bins) if np.any(bins == b))

def nll(p, y): return -np.mean(np.where(y, np.log(p), np.log(1 - p)))

# Temperature scaling: divide the log-odds by T, choosing T to minimize the NLL (log loss).
grid = np.linspace(0.5, 4, 351)
best_T = grid[np.argmin([nll(sigmoid(logit(p_over) / T), y) for T in grid])]
p_fixed = sigmoid(logit(p_over) / best_T)
print(f"Best T = {best_T:.2f}")
for name, p in [("overconfident", p_over), ("after scaling", p_fixed)]:
    print(f"{name:>13}: ECE {ece(p, y):.3f}, Brier {np.mean((p - y) ** 2):.4f}, log loss {nll(p, y):.4f}")

# Brier = calibration + refinement, exactly, when we group by the reported value.
levels = np.round(p_over, 1)
total, calib, refine = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
for v in np.unique(levels):
    g = levels == v
    acc = y[g].mean()
    calib += g.mean() * (v - acc) ** 2
    refine += g.mean() * acc * (1 - acc)
    total += g.mean() * np.mean((v - y[g]) ** 2)
print(f"Brier {total:.4f} = calibration {calib:.4f} + refinement {refine:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Best T = 2.03
overconfident: ECE 0.103, Brier 0.2136, log loss 0.6458
after scaling: ECE 0.002, Brier 0.2008, log loss 0.5853
Brier 0.2147 = calibration 0.0132 + refinement 0.2015
```

The best $T$ is close to the true value 2, because we doubled the log-odds. After scaling, the ECE falls from 0.103 to 0.002, and both the Brier score and the log loss improve. The last line uses reports rounded to one decimal (so the total, 0.2147, differs slightly from 0.2136), and it shows the exact split: the sum of the two parts equals the Brier score.

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing accuracy with calibration.** A model can have high accuracy and bad probabilities. Accuracy looks only at the top choice, and calibration looks at the number.
- **Thinking that "calibrated" means "good".** The constant base-rate forecaster is perfectly calibrated and useless. Always look at refinement too (for example with the Brier score).
- **Trusting a bin with few cases.** A bin of 100 cases at 0.7 has a standard deviation of $\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3 / 100} \approx 0.046$ even if the model is perfect. Observing 0.6 is then unusual but not impossible (see problem 6). Use many cases, or wide bins, and say how noisy the estimate is ([16.7](../../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.7-Hoeffding-Inequality.md)).
- **Fixing calibration on the training data.** Choose $T$ on held-out data. On the training data, a model may be too sure just because it has seen those cases.
- **Reading ECE as a single truth.** The number depends on how many bins you use. Report it with a reliability diagram.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** checking that a classifier's confidence can be trusted, and deciding when to hand a case to a person. Ensembles and other methods that estimate disagreement are used to flag cases outside the training data.
- **Weather forecasting:** "30% chance of rain" is scored with exactly these tools ([21.11 Weather and Forecasting](../21.11-Weather-and-Forecasting.md)).
- **Medicine:** a risk score for disease is useful only if a score of 20% means about 20 in 100 ([21.6 Medicine and Diagnostic Testing](../21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)).
- **Betting and finance:** a model that is overconfident loses money when it bets on its own probabilities ([21.10 Games, Gambling and Sports](../21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a friend who says "I am 100% sure" and is often wrong is badly calibrated. Keeping score of your own predictions teaches you how sure to be.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A forecaster says "70% chance of rain" on 100 days. It rains on 60 of those days. What is the accuracy of this bin, and is the forecaster over- or underconfident?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The accuracy is $\frac{60}{100} = 0.6$, below the stated confidence $0.7$. The forecaster is overconfident in rain: the stated chance was too high by 10 points.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A model says $\hat{p} = 0.8$ for "yes". Find its squared error (Brier loss) if the true outcome is "no", and if it is "yes".

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If the outcome is "no" ($Y = 0$): $(0.8 - 0)^2 = 0.64$. If it is "yes" ($Y = 1$): $(0.8 - 1)^2 = 0.04$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A model made 100 predictions. In bin 0.2 there are 40 cases with 10 "yes". In bin 0.8 there are 60 cases with 42 "yes". Find the ECE.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Bin 0.2: accuracy $\frac{10}{40} = 0.25$, gap $0.05$. Bin 0.8: accuracy $\frac{42}{60} = 0.7$, gap $0.1$.

$$\text{ECE} = 0.4 \times 0.05 + 0.6 \times 0.1 = 0.02 + 0.06 = 0.08$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In a city it rains on 30% of days. A forecaster says "30%" every day. Is the forecast calibrated? Find its Brier score and say how it splits.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Among all days with report 0.3, the rain frequency is 0.3, so it is calibrated and the calibration part is 0. The refinement part is $q(1 - q) = 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21$, so the Brier score is $0.21$. The forecast is honest but gives no information about particular days.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove directly (without Eve's law) that for numbers $p_1, \dots, p_M$ with average $\bar{p}$: $\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p}) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_m p_m (1 - p_m) + \frac{1}{M} \sum_m (p_m - \bar{p})^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Expand each part. The first term is $\frac{1}{M} \sum_m p_m - \frac{1}{M} \sum_m p_m^2 = \bar{p} - \overline{p^2}$, where $\overline{p^2}$ is the average of $p_m^2$. The second term is the variance, $\overline{p^2} - \bar{p}^2$. Add them:

$$\left( \bar{p} - \overline{p^2} \right) + \left( \overline{p^2} - \bar{p}^2 \right) = \bar{p} - \bar{p}^2 = \bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Suppose the forecaster of problem 1 is perfectly calibrated. How likely is it to see 60 or fewer rainy days among 100 days at "70%"? Simulate it, and compare with the exact value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# If a forecaster is perfectly calibrated, 100 days at "70% rain" give Bin(100, 0.7) rainy days.
rainy_days = rng.binomial(100, 0.7, size=1_000_000)
print(f"P(60 or fewer rainy days): simulated {np.mean(rainy_days <= 60):.4f}, exact {binom.cdf(60, 100, 0.7):.4f}")
print(f"Standard deviation of the rain rate: {np.sqrt(0.7 * 0.3 / 100):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(60 or fewer rainy days): simulated 0.0211, exact 0.0210
Standard deviation of the rain rate: 0.0458
```

About 2% of the time a calibrated forecaster looks this bad, so one bin of 100 days is weak evidence of overconfidence, but a consistent pattern over many bins is strong evidence.

</details>

## Summary

- A model is calibrated when $P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{p} = p) = p$: among cases where it says $p$, the fraction of "yes" is $p$. Test it with a reliability diagram and the ECE.
- The Brier score equals the calibration error plus the refinement: $E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2] = E[(\hat{p} - q)^2] + E[q(1 - q)]$. It is a proper score, so honest reports are best.
- Calibration alone is not enough. A constant forecast of the base rate is calibrated and has no skill.
- Overconfidence can often be fixed by one number, the temperature, chosen by log loss on held-out data.
- For an ensemble, total uncertainty is the average noise inside each model plus the disagreement between models (Eve's law).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2] = E[(\hat{p} - q(\hat{p}))^2] + E[q(\hat{p})(1 - q(\hat{p}))], \qquad q(\hat{p}) = E[Y \mid \hat{p}]}
$$
