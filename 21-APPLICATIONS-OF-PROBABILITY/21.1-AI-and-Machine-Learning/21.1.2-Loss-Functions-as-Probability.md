> **Big idea:** Training a model by minimizing a loss is the same as choosing the model under which the data are most probable: cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of a categorical, and squared error is the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian.

---

## Why It Matters

Every machine learning model is trained by making some number small. That number is the **loss**. It can look arbitrary: why "cross-entropy" for classes, why "squared error" for prices? Probability gives one answer for all of them. A loss is the **negative log-likelihood** of the data under the model. Once you see this, you can pick a loss by asking "what kind of random variable is my target?", and you can understand why some losses punish confident mistakes so hard.

## Intuition

Two friends forecast the weather for three days. Friend A says "rain with probability 0.9" on all three days, and it rains all three times. Friend B says "rain with probability 0.5" on all three days. How probable were the three outcomes, according to each friend? Friend A gave them $0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 = 0.729$. Friend B gave them $0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.125$. Friend A had the better model, because the data were more probable under it.

This number is the **likelihood** of the data. It is a product, and products of many small numbers are awkward. So we take the logarithm, which turns a product into a sum, and we put a minus sign in front so that "better" means "smaller". The result is a loss: for Friend A it is $-\ln 0.729 = 0.316$, for Friend B it is $-\ln 0.125 = 2.079$. Smaller is better. This is the whole idea.

## Picture

```text
   data (x, y)  --->  model gives p(y | x)  --->  loss = -ln p(y | x)

   loss when the model gives probability q to the TRUE class:

   loss = -ln q
   4.61 |*                         q = 0.01
   3.00 |  *                       q = 0.05
   2.30 |    *                     q = 0.10
   1.61 |        *                 q = 0.20
   1.20 |            *             q = 0.30
   0.69 |                    *     q = 0.50
   0.36 |                            *       q = 0.70
   0.11 |                                    *     q = 0.90
        +----+---------------+-------------------+- q
             0.1             0.5                 1
```

The loss is small when the model gave a high probability to what really happened. It grows without limit as that probability goes to 0.

## Definition

Let the data be pairs $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$, and let the model give a probability (or a density) $p_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ for the target $y$ given the input $x$, where $\theta$ stands for the model's parameters. If the pairs are independent, the **likelihood** is a product, and the **negative log-likelihood** (NLL) is a sum:

$$
L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{\theta}(y_i \mid x_i), \qquad \text{NLL}(\theta) = -\ln L(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln p_{\theta}(y_i \mid x_i)
$$

**In words:** the loss of a model is the sum, over all training examples, of the surprise (minus the log of the probability) that the model felt when it saw the true answer.

The **maximum likelihood estimate** (MLE) is the $\theta$ that makes $L$ largest. Since $-\ln$ is a decreasing function, this is the same $\theta$ that makes NLL smallest. Each common loss is the NLL of one distribution:

| Target $y$ | Distribution used | Loss per example |
|---|---|---|
| a class from $k$ options | $\text{Cat}(q_1, \dots, q_k)$ | $-\ln q_y$ (**cross-entropy**) |
| yes or no, $y \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$ | $\text{Bern}(q)$ | $-y \ln q - (1 - y) \ln (1 - q)$ (**log loss**) |
| a real number | $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\sigma$ fixed | $\frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ plus a constant (**squared error**) |
| a real number | Laplace with centre $\mu$ | proportional to $\lvert y - \mu \rvert$ plus a constant (**absolute error**) |

For a classifier, the model usually produces **logits** $z_1, \dots, z_k$ (any real numbers) and turns them into probabilities with the **softmax**:

$$
q_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_{m=1}^{k} e^{z_m}}
$$

**In words:** make every logit positive with $e^{z}$, then divide by the total so that the $k$ numbers add up to 1. This gives a categorical distribution ([7.8](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.8-Categorical.md)).

Three more words are needed. Let $p = (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ be the true distribution and $q$ the model's. The **entropy** of $p$ is the average surprise, $H(p) = -\sum_j p_j \ln p_j$. The **cross-entropy** is the average surprise of the model on data from $p$, $H(p, q) = -\sum_j p_j \ln q_j$. The **KL divergence** is the extra surprise caused by using $q$ instead of $p$:

$$
D(p \Vert q) = \sum_{j} p_j \ln \frac{p_j}{q_j} = H(p, q) - H(p)
$$

**In words:** cross-entropy equals the unavoidable surprise $H(p)$ plus the penalty $D(p \Vert q)$ for having the wrong model.

## Why It Is True

**Cross-entropy is a categorical NLL.** One example has class $y$. A categorical model gives it probability $q_y$, so the NLL is $-\ln q_y$. In one-hot form (see [7.8](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.8-Categorical.md)), $P(y) = \prod_j q_j^{I_j}$ where $I_j$ is 1 for the true class and 0 otherwise, and so $-\ln P(y) = -\sum_j I_j \ln q_j$. If the true classes appear with frequencies $p_j$ in the data, the average loss is $-\sum_j p_j \ln q_j = H(p, q)$.

**Squared error is a Gaussian NLL.** The Gaussian density is $\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(y - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2)}$. Take $-\ln$:

$$
-\ln f(y) = \ln (\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}) + \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
$$

The first term does not depend on the model's guess $\mu$, so minimizing the NLL means minimizing $(y - \mu)^2$. Over $n$ examples, this is the sum of squared errors. By [6.10](../../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md), the best constant guess is the mean. The same argument with the Laplace density ([9.7](../../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.7-Laplace.md)), which has $e^{-\lvert y - \mu \rvert / b}$ in it, gives absolute error, whose best constant guess is the median.

**The Bernoulli MLE is the sample mean.** If $k$ of $n$ yes/no results are 1, the log-likelihood is $\ell(q) = k \ln q + (n - k) \ln (1 - q)$. Set the derivative to zero:

$$
\ell'(q) = \frac{k}{q} - \frac{n - k}{1 - q} = 0 \implies k(1 - q) = (n - k) q \implies q = \frac{k}{n}
$$

**Cross-entropy is smallest at the truth.** We must show $D(p \Vert q) \ge 0$, with equality only when $q = p$. Use Jensen's inequality ([16.3](../../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)) for the convex function $-\ln$, where $J$ is the set of classes with $p_j \gt 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D(p \Vert q) &= \sum_{j \in J} p_j \left( -\ln \frac{q_j}{p_j} \right) && \text{(flip the fraction)} \\
&\ge -\ln \sum_{j \in J} p_j \frac{q_j}{p_j} && \text{(Jensen, the weights } p_j \text{ add to 1)} \\
&= -\ln \sum_{j \in J} q_j \ge -\ln 1 = 0 && \text{(these } q_j \text{ add to at most 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $H(p, q) = H(p) + D(p \Vert q) \ge H(p)$. The model cannot beat the entropy of the data, and it reaches it only by telling the truth. A loss with this property is called **proper**: it rewards honest probabilities.

**The softmax gradient is "prediction minus truth".** For one example with true class $y$, the loss is $-z_y + \ln \sum_m e^{z_m}$. Differentiate with respect to $z_j$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_j} \left( -z_y + \ln \sum_m e^{z_m} \right) = -I_j + \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_m e^{z_m}} = q_j - I_j
$$

where $I_j = 1$ if $j = y$ and $0$ otherwise. This is why a classifier trained with cross-entropy has such a simple and stable update: push each logit by how much the model's probability differs from the truth.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Cross-Entropy for One Example

**Problem.** A model produces logits $z = (2, 1, 0)$ for three classes. (a) Find the probabilities. (b) Find the loss if the true class is class 2. (c) Find the gradient with respect to the logits.

**Solution.**

1. Exponentiate: $e^2 \approx 7.389$, $e^1 \approx 2.718$, $e^0 = 1$. The total is $11.107$.
2. Divide by the total:

$$
q = \left( \frac{7.389}{11.107}, \frac{2.718}{11.107}, \frac{1}{11.107} \right) \approx (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)
$$

3. The true class is 2, so the loss is $-\ln q_2 = -\ln 0.2447 \approx 1.408$.
4. The gradient is $q - I$ with $I = (0, 1, 0)$:

$$
q - I \approx (0.665, \thinspace 0.245 - 1, \thinspace 0.090) = (0.665, -0.755, 0.090)
$$

**Answer.** $q \approx (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, loss $\approx 1.408$, gradient $\approx (0.665, -0.755, 0.090)$.

**Check.** If the true class had been 1, the loss would be $-\ln 0.665 = 0.408$, much smaller, because the model favoured class 1. The gradient entries add up to 0, because the probabilities add to 1 and so do the one-hot entries.

### Example 2: Squared Error from a Gaussian

**Problem.** Three measurements are $y = 2, 4, 9$. We model them as $\mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)$. Show that $\mu = 5$ has a smaller NLL than $\mu = 4$ or $\mu = 6$, and find the best $\mu$.

**Solution.**

1. With $\sigma = 1$, each example has NLL $\ln \sqrt{2\pi} + \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2}$, and $\ln \sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.919$.
2. At $\mu = 5$: the squared errors are $9, 1, 16$, which add to $26$.

$$
\text{NLL}(5) = 3 \times 0.919 + \frac{26}{2} = 2.757 + 13 = 15.757
$$

3. At $\mu = 4$: squared errors $4, 0, 25$, total $29$, so $\text{NLL}(4) = 2.757 + 14.5 = 17.257$. At $\mu = 6$: squared errors $16, 4, 9$, total $29$, so $\text{NLL}(6) = 17.257$.
4. The sum of squared errors is smallest at the mean, $\bar{y} = \frac{2 + 4 + 9}{3} = 5$.

**Answer.** The best $\mu$ is the sample mean $5$, with NLL $15.757$.

**Check.** The two neighbours $\mu = 4$ and $\mu = 6$ are tied at $17.257$, because $5$ is exactly in the middle of them and the loss is symmetric around the mean. The simulation below also finds the mean.

### Example 3: Confident Mistakes Cost a Lot

**Problem.** A model must give the probability of "yes" for an email that really is spam. Compare the log loss when the model says 0.9, 0.5, 0.1 and 0.01.

**Solution.**

1. The true answer is "yes", so the loss is $-\ln q$ where $q$ is the probability given to "yes".
2. Compute each: $-\ln 0.9 = 0.105$, $-\ln 0.5 = 0.693$, $-\ln 0.1 = 2.303$, $-\ln 0.01 = 4.605$.

**Answer.** The losses are $0.105$, $0.693$, $2.303$ and $4.605$.

**Check.** Going from "unsure" (0.5) to "sure and wrong" (0.01) costs six times more than being unsure. This is good: the loss teaches a model not to be sure unless it has a reason. The same property pushes a model toward probabilities that match frequencies, which is the topic of [21.1.6](21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md).

## Simulate It

Part 1 draws labels from $p = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ and measures the average cross-entropy of three models. The exact value is $H(p) + D(p \Vert q)$. Part 2 checks that the mean minimizes the squared error of Gaussian data.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 200_000

# Labels come from the true categorical distribution p = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2).
p_true = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
labels = rng.choice(3, size=n_samples, p=p_true)

# Average cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood) of three candidate models q.
entropy = -np.sum(p_true * np.log(p_true))
for q in [p_true, np.ones(3) / 3, np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])]:
    simulated = -np.mean(np.log(q[labels]))                  # average of -ln q(y)
    exact = entropy + np.sum(p_true * np.log(p_true / q))    # H(p) + KL(p || q)
    print(f"q = {np.round(q, 3)}: simulated {simulated:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

# Gaussian data: the mean that minimizes squared error is the sample mean.
y = rng.normal(5.0, 2.0, size=1_000)
grid = np.linspace(3, 7, 4001)
mse = ((y[:, None] - grid) ** 2).mean(axis=0)
print(f"Best mu on the grid: {grid[mse.argmin()]:.3f}, sample mean: {y.mean():.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
q = [0.5 0.3 0.2]: simulated 1.0295, exact 1.0297
q = [0.333 0.333 0.333]: simulated 1.0986, exact 1.0986
q = [0.7 0.2 0.1]: simulated 1.1215, exact 1.1217
Best mu on the grid: 5.015, sample mean: 5.015
```

The true distribution has the smallest loss, $H(p) = 1.0297$. The two wrong models lose by $D(p \Vert q)$: $0.069$ for the uniform guess and $0.092$ for the third model.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking the loss is a random choice.** Cross-entropy and squared error come from a model of the target: a categorical and a Gaussian. If the target is a count, a Poisson NLL fits better. Choose the loss from the distribution of the target.
- **Applying cross-entropy to values that are not probabilities.** The $q_j$ must be non-negative and add to 1. This is why the softmax is placed before the log. (Taking the log of a raw logit is meaningless.)
- **Forgetting the minus sign.** The likelihood is maximized, so the NLL is minimized. A loss that goes up when the model improves has a sign error.
- **Saying "squared error needs a normal target".** The loss is a Gaussian NLL, but the minimizer of squared error is the mean of $Y$ for any distribution with a finite variance (see [14.5](../../14-CONDITIONAL-EXPECTATION/14.5-Best-Predictor-Under-Squared-Error.md)). The Gaussian view just explains the loss.
- **Comparing losses of different models by the training data only.** A flexible model can reach a tiny training loss by memorizing. Compare on new data from the same source.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** classification networks, language models ([21.1.3](21.1.3-Language-Models.md)) and many more are trained on cross-entropy. Regression models use squared error. Count models use a Poisson NLL.
- **Logistic regression:** the Bernoulli log loss with $q = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, whose CDF form is the logistic distribution of [9.10](../../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md).
- **Statistics:** maximum likelihood is the standard way to fit a distribution to data (see [21.2](../21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Information theory:** entropy and KL divergence measure how many bits are needed to describe data, and how far one distribution is from another.
- **Everyday life:** "scoring" a forecaster by the probability they gave to what happened is fair, and it is exactly the log loss.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A classifier gives the true class a probability of 0.5 on one example and 0.9 on another. Find the cross-entropy loss of each, and their average.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$-\ln 0.5 = 0.693, \qquad -\ln 0.9 = 0.105, \qquad \text{average} = \frac{0.693 + 0.105}{2} \approx 0.399$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You flip a coin 10 times and see 7 heads. What is the MLE of the heads probability, and what is the average log loss of the model with $q = 0.7$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The MLE is the sample proportion, $\hat{q} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7$. The average log loss is:

$$-\left( 0.7 \ln 0.7 + 0.3 \ln 0.3 \right) = 0.7 \times 0.3567 + 0.3 \times 1.2040 \approx 0.611$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** For the data $y = (1, 3, 5)$ and the model $\mathcal{N}(3, 2^2)$ (so $\sigma = 2$), compute the NLL of the three data points.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each point contributes $\ln (2 \sqrt{2\pi}) + \frac{(y - 3)^2}{8}$, with $\ln(2 \sqrt{2\pi}) \approx 1.612$. The squared errors are $4, 0, 4$, total $8$:

$$\text{NLL} = 3 \times 1.612 + \frac{8}{8} = 4.836 + 1 = 5.836$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A model gives logits $z = (2, 1, 0)$ as in Example 1, and the true class is class 3. Find the loss and the gradient with respect to the logits.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

From Example 1, $q \approx (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$. The loss is $-\ln 0.090 \approx 2.408$. The gradient is $q - I$ with $I = (0, 0, 1)$:

$$q - I \approx (0.665, 0.245, -0.910)$$

The model gave class 3 only 9% and so receives a strong push to raise its logit.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for two outcomes with true probability $p$ of "yes", the expected log loss $-p \ln q - (1 - p) \ln (1 - q)$ is smallest at $q = p$ (use calculus, not Jensen).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $g(q) = -p \ln q - (1 - p) \ln (1 - q)$ for $0 \lt q \lt 1$. Differentiate:

$$g'(q) = -\frac{p}{q} + \frac{1 - p}{1 - q} = \frac{q - p}{q (1 - q)}$$

The denominator is positive. So $g'(q) \lt 0$ for $q \lt p$ and $g'(q) \gt 0$ for $q \gt p$. The function decreases and then increases, so its minimum is at $q = p$. Its value there is the entropy $-p \ln p - (1 - p) \ln (1 - p)$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Draw 1,000 values from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and add 5 outliers equal to 100. Find the constant $c$ that minimizes the squared error and the constant that minimizes the absolute error. Which one moves toward the outliers?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
y = np.concatenate([rng.normal(0, 1, size=1_000), np.full(5, 100.0)])

grid = np.linspace(-5, 5, 10_001)
squared = ((y[:, None] - grid) ** 2).sum(axis=0)
absolute = np.abs(y[:, None] - grid).sum(axis=0)

print(f"Minimizer of squared error:  {grid[squared.argmin()]:.3f}  (mean   {y.mean():.3f})")
print(f"Minimizer of absolute error: {grid[absolute.argmin()]:.3f}  (median {np.median(y):.3f})")
```

Output:

```text
Minimizer of squared error:  0.469  (mean   0.469)
Minimizer of absolute error: 0.011  (median 0.011)
```

The mean (squared error) is pulled toward the outliers, about $\frac{500}{1005} \approx 0.5$ away from 0. The median (absolute error) hardly moves. This is the Gaussian versus Laplace choice: the Laplace model expects outliers.

</details>

## Summary

- A loss is a negative log-likelihood: the surprise of the model on the true answer, summed over the data.
- Cross-entropy is the NLL of a categorical model, log loss is the NLL of a Bernoulli, and squared error is the NLL of a Gaussian with fixed spread. Absolute error comes from the Laplace distribution.
- Cross-entropy equals entropy plus KL divergence, and KL is never negative (Jensen). So the loss is smallest when the model tells the truth.
- For softmax outputs, the gradient is the prediction minus the one-hot truth.
- Confident mistakes are punished heavily, because $-\ln q$ goes to infinity as $q$ goes to 0.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{H(p, q) = -\sum_{j} p_j \ln q_j = H(p) + D(p \Vert q) \ge H(p)}
$$
