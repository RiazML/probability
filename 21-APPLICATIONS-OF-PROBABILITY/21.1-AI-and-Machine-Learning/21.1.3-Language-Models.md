> **Big idea:** A language model assigns a probability to every piece of text by the chain rule, one categorical distribution for the next token at a time, and generating text means drawing from those distributions, with a temperature that sharpens or flattens them.

---

## Why It Matters

A language model predicts the next piece of text. It powers autocomplete, translation and chat systems. Behind the large systems, the probability is simple. The probability of a whole sentence is a product of next-word probabilities (the chain rule from Module 04). Each next-word probability is a categorical distribution over a list of **tokens**, the small pieces of text such as words or word parts. To write text, the model draws a token, adds it to the text, and draws again. Settings such as "temperature" change only how the categorical distribution is shaped before the draw.

## Intuition

Your phone suggests three words while you type "see you". Say it offers "soon" (50%), "later" (30%) and "tomorrow" (20%). These three numbers are a categorical distribution over the next word. If you always tap the first suggestion, the phone always writes "soon". If the phone picked at random in proportion to the numbers, you would see "soon" half the time, "later" 3 times in 10, and so on. That second way gives varied text.

Now imagine a slider called **temperature**. Turn it down and the phone almost always picks the top word. Turn it up and all three words become nearly equally likely. The slider does not change which word the model likes best. It only changes how strongly that preference is used.

A sentence gets its probability by going word by word. "See you soon" is probable if "see" is a probable start, and "you" is probable after "see", and "soon" is probable after "see you". Multiply the three numbers.

## Definition

A text is a sequence of tokens $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T$, where each token comes from a fixed list called the **vocabulary**. A **language model** is a rule that gives, for every **context** (the tokens so far), a categorical distribution for the next token ([7.8](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.8-Categorical.md)). By the chain rule ([4.2](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)), the probability of the whole text is:

$$
P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})
$$

where the factor for $t = 1$ is $P(w_1)$, the chance of the first token.

**In words:** the probability of a text is the probability of its first token, times the probability of the second given the first, and so on to the end.

In a neural model, the context goes in and a list of **logits** $z_1, \dots, z_k$ (one per vocabulary token) comes out. The next-token distribution with **temperature** $T \gt 0$ is the softmax of the scaled logits:

$$
q_j(T) = \frac{e^{z_j / T}}{\sum_{m=1}^{k} e^{z_m / T}}
$$

where $k$ is the vocabulary size. At $T = 1$ this is the plain softmax of [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md).

**In words:** divide every logit by $T$, then apply the softmax. Small $T$ sharpens the distribution, large $T$ flattens it.

The model is trained with the cross-entropy loss of [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md) on every next token. The average loss per token, turned back into a number of choices, is the **perplexity**:

$$
\text{perplexity} = \exp \left( -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln P(w_t \mid w_{\lt t}) \right)
$$

where $w_{\lt t}$ means the tokens before position $t$ (and $T$ here is the length of the text, not the temperature).

**In words:** perplexity is $e$ to the power of the average surprise per token. It is the size of a fair die that would be just as surprised.

The simplest language model is the **bigram model**. It assumes the next token depends only on the one before it: $P(w_t \mid w_{\lt t}) = P(w_t \mid w_{t-1})$. This is the Markov property ([18.3.1](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.1-Markov-Property.md)), so the text is a path of a Markov chain whose states are tokens. Its probabilities are learned by counting: $\hat{P}(b \mid a) = \frac{\text{count}(a, b)}{\text{count}(a)}$.

## Why It Is True

**The chain rule.** For events $A_1, \dots, A_T$ the multiplication rule says $P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_T) = P(A_1) P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdots P(A_T \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{T-1})$, which is proved by repeating $P(A \cap B) = P(B) P(A \mid B)$ ([4.2](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)). Take $A_t$ to be the event that token $t$ is $w_t$. Nothing is assumed about the model. **Any** probability model of text can be written this way, so a language model loses no generality by predicting one token at a time.

**Training on next tokens is maximum likelihood on the whole text.** The negative log-likelihood of the text is:

$$
-\ln P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln P(w_t \mid w_{\lt t})
$$

The right side is the sum of the cross-entropy losses of the next-token predictions ([21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)). So minimizing next-token cross-entropy is exactly maximizing the likelihood of the text. It also follows that perplexity is $\exp(\text{NLL} / T)$: a model that is uniform over $k$ tokens has NLL $T \ln k$, so its perplexity is $e^{\ln k} = k$.

**What temperature does.** Take two tokens $i$ and $j$. The ratio of their probabilities is:

$$
\frac{q_i(T)}{q_j(T)} = e^{(z_i - z_j)/T}
$$

Suppose $z_i$ is the one largest logit. For every other $j$, the gap $z_i - z_j$ is positive, so $e^{-(z_i - z_j)/T} \to 0$ as $T \to 0$, and:

$$
q_i(T) = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{j \ne i} e^{-(z_i - z_j)/T}} \to 1 \qquad (T \to 0)
$$

As $T \to \infty$, every exponent $z_j / T$ goes to 0, every $e^{z_j/T}$ goes to 1, and $q_j(T) \to \frac{1}{k}$: the uniform distribution. At $T = 1$ the model's own distribution is used.

**Top-k and top-p sampling are conditioning.** Sometimes only the likely tokens are allowed. Let $S$ be the set of allowed tokens. Sampling from the renormalized probabilities gives:

$$
P(\text{token} = j \mid \text{token} \in S) = \frac{q_j}{\sum_{m \in S} q_m}, \qquad j \in S
$$

This is the definition of conditional probability ([4.1](../../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.1-Conditional-Probability.md)) with the event that the token is in $S$.

**The Gumbel-max trick.** To sample from $q(T)$ with a computer, one can add independent $\text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$ noise ([9.10](../../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md)) to $z_j / T$ and take the largest. The index of the largest has exactly the probability $q_j(T)$. The simulation below checks this.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Probability of a Sentence

**Problem.** A bigram model is learned from six sentences: *the cat sat*, *the cat sat*, *the cat ran*, *the dog ran*, *the dog sat*, *a dog ran*. Each sentence ends with a special token "end". Find the model's probabilities, then the probability and perplexity of "the dog sat".

**Solution.**

1. Count the first words: "the" starts 5 sentences, "a" starts 1. So $P(\text{the}) = \frac{5}{6}$ and $P(\text{a}) = \frac{1}{6}$.
2. After "the" come cat (3 times) and dog (2 times): $P(\text{cat} \mid \text{the}) = \frac{3}{5}$, $P(\text{dog} \mid \text{the}) = \frac{2}{5}$.
3. After "dog" come ran (2 times) and sat (1 time): $P(\text{ran} \mid \text{dog}) = \frac{2}{3}$, $P(\text{sat} \mid \text{dog}) = \frac{1}{3}$. Every sentence ends right after "sat" or "ran", so $P(\text{end} \mid \text{sat}) = 1$.
4. Multiply along the path:

$$
P(\text{the dog sat end}) = \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{1}{3} \times 1 = \frac{10}{90} = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111
$$

5. The NLL is $\ln 9 = 2.197$ over 4 tokens, so the average is $0.549$ and the perplexity is $e^{0.549} = 9^{1/4} \approx 1.732$.

**Answer.** The probability is $\frac{1}{9}$, and the perplexity is about 1.73 (about 1.7 equally likely choices per token).

**Check.** The simulation below generates sentences from this chain and finds "the dog sat" with frequency 0.1116. Note also that "a cat sat" gets probability 0, because "a cat" never appeared in the training text. This is why real models smooth their counts, as in [21.1.1](21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md).

### Example 2: Temperature on Three Tokens

**Problem.** A model gives logits $z = (2, 1, 0)$ for tokens A, B and C. Find the next-token distribution at $T = 0.5$ and at $T = 2$.

**Solution.**

1. For $T = 0.5$, the scaled logits are $z / T = (4, 2, 0)$. Exponentiate: $e^4 = 54.598$, $e^2 = 7.389$, $e^0 = 1$. The total is $62.987$.

$$
q(0.5) = \left( \frac{54.598}{62.987}, \frac{7.389}{62.987}, \frac{1}{62.987} \right) \approx (0.867, 0.117, 0.016)
$$

2. For $T = 2$, the scaled logits are $(1, 0.5, 0)$. Exponentiate: $e^1 = 2.718$, $e^{0.5} = 1.649$, $1$. The total is $5.367$.

$$
q(2) = \left( \frac{2.718}{5.367}, \frac{1.649}{5.367}, \frac{1}{5.367} \right) \approx (0.506, 0.307, 0.186)
$$

**Answer.** $q(0.5) \approx (0.867, 0.117, 0.016)$ and $q(2) \approx (0.506, 0.307, 0.186)$. At $T = 1$ it is $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$ (Example 1 of [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md)).

**Check.** Each row adds to 1. The order A, B, C never changes: temperature changes only the spread. The top token has 87% at $T = 0.5$ and 51% at $T = 2$.

### Example 3: Top-2 Sampling

**Problem.** Using the $T = 1$ distribution $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, only tokens A and B are allowed. What is the chance of drawing A?

**Solution.**

1. The allowed set is $S = \lbrace A, B \rbrace$ with total probability $0.665 + 0.245 = 0.910$.
2. Renormalize: $P(A \mid S) = \frac{0.665}{0.910} \approx 0.731$ and $P(B \mid S) = \frac{0.245}{0.910} \approx 0.269$.

**Answer.** Token A is drawn with probability about 0.731.

**Check.** The two numbers add to 1, and the ratio $\frac{0.665}{0.245}$ is the same as before. Cutting off the tail removed the chance of the rare token C but did not change the relative odds of A and B.

## Simulate It

Part 1 draws next tokens with the Gumbel-max trick at three temperatures and compares the frequencies with the softmax formula. Part 2 generates sentences from the bigram chain of Example 1 and estimates $P(\text{the dog sat})$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def softmax(z, temperature=1.0):
    e = np.exp((z - z.max()) / temperature)       # subtract the max for stability
    return e / e.sum()

# Part 1: temperature sampling with the Gumbel-max trick matches softmax(z / T).
logits = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.0])
n_draws = 200_000
for temperature in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    gumbel = rng.gumbel(size=(n_draws, 3))
    picks = np.argmax(logits / temperature + gumbel, axis=1)
    freq = np.bincount(picks, minlength=3) / n_draws
    print(f"T={temperature}: sampled {np.round(freq, 3)}, softmax {np.round(softmax(logits, temperature), 3)}")

# Part 2: a bigram model learned from six tiny sentences, then sampled.
words = ["the", "a", "cat", "dog", "sat", "ran", "<end>"]
P = np.zeros((7, 7))                                  # row = current word, column = next word
P[0] = [0, 0, 3/5, 2/5, 0, 0, 0]                     # after "the"
P[1] = [0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]                         # after "a"
P[2] = [0, 0, 0, 0, 2/3, 1/3, 0]                     # after "cat"
P[3] = [0, 0, 0, 0, 1/3, 2/3, 0]                     # after "dog"
P[4] = P[5] = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1]                  # after "sat" or "ran"
n_sentences = 100_000
count = 0
for _ in range(n_sentences):
    word = rng.choice(2, p=[5/6, 1/6])               # first word: "the" or "a"
    sentence = [word]
    while word != 6:
        word = rng.choice(7, p=P[word])
        sentence.append(word)
    count += sentence == [0, 3, 4, 6]                 # the dog sat <end>
print(f"P('the dog sat'): simulated {count / n_sentences:.4f}, chain rule {5/6 * 2/5 * 1/3:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
T=0.5: sampled [0.867 0.117 0.016], softmax [0.867 0.117 0.016]
T=1.0: sampled [0.666 0.244 0.089], softmax [0.665 0.245 0.09 ]
T=2.0: sampled [0.507 0.307 0.186], softmax [0.506 0.307 0.186]
P('the dog sat'): simulated 0.1116, chain rule 0.1111
```

Both parts agree with the formulas, within the noise of sampling.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking temperature changes the ranking.** Dividing all logits by the same positive $T$ keeps their order. The best token stays the best, but its probability changes.
- **Using $T = 0$ in the formula.** Division by zero. In practice "temperature 0" means "always take the largest logit", which is the limit $T \to 0$.
- **Multiplying many probabilities directly.** A text of 200 tokens has a probability like $10^{-300}$, which underflows. Add the log-probabilities, as in [21.1.1](21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md).
- **Believing the model "knows" the next word.** It gives a distribution. Two runs with sampling give different texts, and both are valid draws from the same model.
- **Confusing the bigram assumption with the chain rule.** The chain rule is always true. The bigram assumption (only the last token matters) is a modelling choice, and it is often false for real language.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** autocomplete, translation, and chat systems all generate text token by token from next-token distributions. Settings such as temperature, top-k and top-p are the sampling knobs of Examples 2 and 3.
- **Evaluation:** perplexity measures how well a model predicts held-out text. It is the exponential of the cross-entropy of [21.1.2](21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md).
- **Computer science:** a bigram model is a Markov chain ([Module 18](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/README.md)); the project "Markov chain text generator" in the syllabus is Part 2 of the simulation above, with a bigger corpus.
- **Compression and information theory:** a model that predicts text well can compress it well. The ideal code length of a token is $-\log_2 q$ bits.
- **Everyday life:** when a phone suggests the next word, it is showing the top of a categorical distribution.

**3. (Medium)** Two tokens have logits $(1, 0)$. Find the probability of the first token at $T = 1$ and at $T = 2$. What are the limits as $T \to 0$ and $T \to \infty$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

At $T = 1$: $\frac{e^1}{e^1 + e^0} = \frac{2.718}{3.718} \approx 0.731$. At $T = 2$: $\frac{e^{0.5}}{e^{0.5} + 1} = \frac{1.649}{2.649} \approx 0.622$.

As $T \to 0$ the probability goes to 1 (the first token has the larger logit). As $T \to \infty$ it goes to $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A model gives probabilities $(0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05)$ to four tokens. With top-2 sampling, what are the probabilities of the two allowed tokens? What is the chance of the first token under top-3?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Top-2: the total is $0.8$, so the probabilities are $\frac{0.5}{0.8} = 0.625$ and $\frac{0.3}{0.8} = 0.375$.

Top-3: the total is $0.5 + 0.3 + 0.15 = 0.95$, so the first token has $\frac{0.5}{0.95} \approx 0.526$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that a language model that gives the same probability $\frac{1}{k}$ to every token, at every position, has perplexity exactly $k$ on any text.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every factor in the chain rule is $\frac{1}{k}$, so a text of length $T$ has probability $k^{-T}$ and:

$$-\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{T} \cdot T \ln k = \ln k$$

Then perplexity $= e^{\ln k} = k$. A model that knows nothing is as confused as a fair $k$-sided die, and any better model has a smaller perplexity on the text it predicts well.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** In the bigram chain of Example 1, find $P(\text{the last word is "ran"})$ exactly, and check it by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the law of total probability, split on the first two words. For "the": $\frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{5} + \frac{4}{15} = \frac{7}{15}$. For "a dog": $\frac{2}{3}$.

$$P(\text{ran}) = \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{7}{15} + \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{7}{18} + \frac{2}{18} = \frac{1}{2}$$

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_sentences = 200_000

# Word 1: "the" with probability 5/6, "a" with probability 1/6.
is_the = rng.random(n_sentences) < 5 / 6
# Word 2: after "the": cat 3/5, dog 2/5. After "a": always dog.
is_cat = is_the & (rng.random(n_sentences) < 3 / 5)
# Word 3: after "cat": sat 2/3, ran 1/3. After "dog": ran 2/3, sat 1/3.
ends_with_ran = rng.random(n_sentences) < np.where(is_cat, 1 / 3, 2 / 3)

print(f"P(last word is 'ran'): simulation {ends_with_ran.mean():.4f}, exact {0.5:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(last word is 'ran'): simulation 0.5004, exact 0.5000
```

</details>

## Summary

- By the chain rule, a language model gives every text a probability: the product of next-token probabilities. This loses no generality.
- Each next-token distribution is categorical. Text is generated by drawing from it, one token at a time.
- Training on next-token cross-entropy is maximum likelihood on the whole text, and perplexity is $e$ to the average surprise per token.
- Temperature rescales the logits: $T \to 0$ gives the top token, $T \to \infty$ gives the uniform distribution, and the ranking never changes. Top-k and top-p are conditioning on a set of allowed tokens.
- A bigram model is a Markov chain on tokens, learned by counting.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(w_t \mid w_{\lt t}), \qquad q_j(T) = \frac{e^{z_j / T}}{\sum_m e^{z_m / T}}}
$$
