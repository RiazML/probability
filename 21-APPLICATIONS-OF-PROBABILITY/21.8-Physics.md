> **Big idea:** Many physical laws are statements about huge numbers of random events: a particle that takes random steps drifts away like $\sqrt{n}$ (diffusion), atoms that decay independently follow an exponential law with Poisson counting noise, and a particle that crosses a material is stopped with an exponential free path, which a Monte Carlo program can follow one particle at a time.

---

## Why It Matters

A drop of ink spreads in water. A radioactive sample ticks in a counter. Light is dimmed by a window. In each case the law that we see is the average of an enormous number of tiny random events. Probability explains the law and also how noisy it is. This file shows three models that are used all over physics: the random walk and diffusion, radioactive decay with counting noise, and the random free path of a particle in matter. They are the same tools as in Modules 07, 09, 17 and 18, applied to physical stories. Monte Carlo ([Module 19](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/README.md)) is the tool that physicists use when the model has no simple formula.

## Intuition

Imagine a drunk walker on a long straight road who takes one step to the right or to the left, with equal probability, once per second. After 100 steps, where is the walker? On average, back at the start: the left and right steps cancel. But in a typical run, the walker is not exactly at the start. The distance from the start is typically about 10 steps, which is $\sqrt{100}$. After 400 steps it is about 20, and after 1,600 steps about 40. To go 4 times farther, the walker needs 16 times more time. This slow spreading is **diffusion**, and it is why a smell takes a long time to cross a room if the air is still.

For decay, think of a million atoms of a substance. Each atom decays at a random time, with no memory of how long it has lived. In one half-life, half of the atoms decay, and in a second half-life, half of the rest. A counter near the sample clicks at random: if it clicks 20 times per minute on average, one minute gives 20, another gives 17, another 25. The noise is not a defect of the counter, it is part of the physics: the number of clicks has a standard deviation of $\sqrt{20} \approx 4.5$.

For light through a window, a particle goes forward until it hits something, and the distance to the first hit is random. If there is no hit in the first millimeter, the chance of a hit in the next millimeter is the same (the material does not remember). This is the exponential law again.

## Definition

**The random walk.** Let $\xi_1, \xi_2, \dots$ be independent steps with $P(\xi_i = +1) = P(\xi_i = -1) = \frac{1}{2}$, and let $S_n = \xi_1 + \dots + \xi_n$ be the position after $n$ steps. Then:

$$
E[S_n] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n, \qquad S_n \approx \mathcal{N}(0, n) \text{ for large } n
$$

**In words:** the average position is the start, the typical distance is $\sqrt{n}$, and the shape of the spreading is a bell curve. If each step has length $\ell$, then $\operatorname{Var} = n \ell^2$.

**Diffusion.** Say a particle makes a step of length $\ell$ every $\tau$ seconds. After time $t$ it has made $n = \frac{t}{\tau}$ steps, and its position $X_t$ has variance $\frac{t}{\tau} \ell^2$. Write $D = \frac{\ell^2}{2 \tau}$, the **diffusion constant**:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = 2 D t, \qquad X_t \approx \mathcal{N}(0, 2 D t)
$$

**In words:** the variance grows in proportion to the time, so the typical distance grows like $\sqrt{2 D t}$. When the steps become very small and very frequent, with $D$ fixed, this is the Brownian motion of [18.8](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.8-Brownian-Motion.md).

**Radioactive decay.** Each atom has a lifetime $T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, independent of the other atoms, where $\lambda$ is the **decay rate** (mean lifetime $\frac{1}{\lambda}$). The **half-life** is $t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$. Of $N_0$ atoms at the start, the number $N(t)$ still undecayed at time $t$ satisfies:

$$
N(t) \sim \text{Bin}\left( N_0, e^{-\lambda t} \right), \qquad E[N(t)] = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}
$$

The number of decays counted in a time window, when each of many atoms has a tiny chance of decaying in that window, is $\text{Pois}(\mu)$, where $\mu$ is the mean count. Its standard deviation is $\sqrt{\mu}$.

**In words:** on average the sample shrinks by half every half-life, and the counts are noisy with a standard deviation equal to the square root of the mean.

**Free path of a particle.** A particle travels through a material that stops it at a rate of $\mu$ per unit length (the **attenuation coefficient**; the mean free path is $\frac{1}{\mu}$). The distance $L$ to the first interaction is $\text{Exp}(\mu)$, so for a slab of thickness $d$:

$$
P(\text{particle crosses without any interaction}) = P(L \gt d) = e^{-\mu d}
$$

**In words:** the intensity of the unstopped beam falls exponentially with the thickness.

## Why It Is True

**The random walk.** The steps are independent with mean 0 and variance $E[\xi^2] - 0 = 1$. By linearity of expectation, $E[S_n] = 0$. By independence, the variances add ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)): $\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n$. The Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) gives the bell shape. The walk is also a binomial: if $B$ is the number of $+1$ steps, then $B \sim \text{Bin}(n, \frac{1}{2})$ and $S_n = B - (n - B) = 2B - n$, so $\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = 4 \cdot \frac{n}{4} = n$ and $P(S_n = 2k - n) = \binom{n}{k} 2^{-n}$.

**Diffusion.** With step $\ell$ and $n = \frac{t}{\tau}$ steps, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = n \ell^2 = \frac{\ell^2}{\tau} t = 2 D t$. Only the combination $D = \frac{\ell^2}{2 \tau}$ matters for the spreading: two microscopic stories with the same $D$ give the same large-scale picture. In 2 or 3 dimensions, each coordinate spreads independently, and the mean squared distance is $2 D t$ times the number of dimensions.

**Decay.** The lifetime of an atom is exponential, so $P(T \gt t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ is the chance that the atom survives to time $t$. The atoms are independent, so the number of survivors is a count of successes in $N_0$ independent trials with probability $e^{-\lambda t}$: binomial. The half-life solves $e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2}$, which gives $t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$. For a short window, each of $N_0$ atoms decays with a small probability $p$, with $N_0 p = \mu$ fixed, and the binomial count is close to $\text{Pois}(\mu)$ ([7.10](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.10-Poisson-Approximation-to-Binomial.md), [17.10](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.10-Poisson-Limit-and-Law-of-Rare-Events.md)), whose mean and variance are both $\mu$. The relative noise is $\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{\mu} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}$: it is small only when many events are counted.

**The free path.** Cut the slab into $m$ thin layers of thickness $\frac{d}{m}$. In each layer the particle is stopped with probability about $\frac{\mu d}{m}$, independently of the other layers, so it passes all of them with probability

$$
\left( 1 - \frac{\mu d}{m} \right)^m \to e^{-\mu d} \quad (m \to \infty)
$$

This is the survival function of an exponential variable, and it has the memoryless property ([9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)).

**Monte Carlo.** To estimate $p = P(L \gt d)$ we follow $n$ independent particles and count the fraction $\hat{p}$ that cross. By the Law of Large Numbers, $\hat{p} \to p$. The standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}$ ([19.3](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md)), so ten times more precision needs a hundred times more particles.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Random Walk of 100 Steps

**Problem.** A walker takes 100 steps of $\pm 1$. Find the mean and the standard deviation of the final position $S_{100}$, and the probability that the walker ends within 10 steps of the start.

**Solution.**

1. $E[S_{100}] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_{100}) = 100$, so the standard deviation is $10$.
2. Let $B$ be the number of steps to the right, so $B \sim \text{Bin}(100, \frac{1}{2})$ with mean 50 and standard deviation $\sqrt{100 \times 0.25} = 5$. Since $S = 2B - 100$, the event $\lvert S \rvert \le 10$ is $45 \le B \le 55$.
3. With the normal approximation and the continuity correction ([17.6](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.6-Normal-Approximation-and-Continuity-Correction.md)), $P(44.5 \le B \le 55.5) = P\left( \lvert Z \rvert \le \frac{5.5}{5} \right) = P(\lvert Z \rvert \le 1.1) \approx 0.7287$.
4. The exact binomial sum gives $P(45 \le B \le 55) = 0.7287$.

**Answer.** Mean 0, standard deviation 10, and a probability of $0.7287$ of ending within 10 steps of the start.

**Check.** The probability is a little above the value $0.68$ for one standard deviation, because the end points $\pm 10$ are included (the continuity correction adds half a step on each side). The simulation below gives $0.7300$.

### Example 2: How Fast Does a Particle Spread?

**Problem.** A particle makes a step of length $\ell = 2$ (in micrometers, made-up units) every $\tau = 0.01$ second. Find the diffusion constant, and the standard deviation of its position after 4 seconds. How long does it take to reach a standard deviation 10 times larger?

**Solution.**

1. $D = \frac{\ell^2}{2 \tau} = \frac{4}{0.02} = 200$ square micrometers per second.
2. After $t = 4$ s, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = 2 D t = 2 \times 200 \times 4 = 1600$, so the standard deviation is $40$.
3. Check by counting steps: $n = \frac{4}{0.01} = 400$ steps, and $\operatorname{Var} = 400 \times 2^2 = 1600$. The two agree.
4. A standard deviation of $400$ needs $2 D t = 160{,}000$, so $t = \frac{160{,}000}{400} = 400$ seconds.

**Answer.** $D = 200$ units per second, a standard deviation of 40 after 4 seconds, and 400 seconds to reach a standard deviation of 400.

**Check.** A spread 10 times larger needs $10^2 = 100$ times more time ($4 \to 400$ seconds). This is the square-root law of the Picture.

### Example 3: Decay and Counting Noise

**Problem.** A sample has $N_0 = 1{,}000{,}000$ atoms and a half-life of 10 days (made-up). (a) What is the mean and standard deviation of the number of atoms left after 30 days? (b) A counter records 20 decays per minute on average. Find the standard deviation of one minute's count, and the number of counts needed for a relative error of 1%.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The decay rate is $\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{10} \approx 0.0693$ per day. After 30 days (3 half-lives) the survival probability is $e^{-\lambda \cdot 30} = 2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125$.
2. $N(30) \sim \text{Bin}(10^6, 0.125)$, with mean $125{,}000$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{10^6 \times 0.125 \times 0.875} \approx 330.7$.
3. (b) The count per minute is Poisson with mean 20, so its standard deviation is $\sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$, a relative error of $\frac{4.47}{20} \approx 0.22$ (22%).
4. The relative error is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}$. For 1% we need $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}} = 0.01$, which gives $\mu = 10{,}000$ counts.

**Answer.** (a) About $125{,}000 \pm 331$ atoms. (b) A standard deviation of about 4.5 counts (22% of the mean), and about 10,000 counts for a 1% relative error. At 20 counts per minute, this takes 500 minutes.

**Check.** The number left after 30 days is very steady (relative error 0.26%), since there are so many atoms. The count in one minute is noisy (22%), since there are few events. The simulation below gives $P(\text{count} \ge 30) = 0.0217$ against the exact $0.0218$.

### Example 4: A Photon Through a Slab

**Problem.** A material stops light at rate $\mu = 0.5$ per centimeter. What fraction passes through a slab of thickness $2$ cm without interacting? If a Monte Carlo program follows $n = 10{,}000$ photons, what is the standard error of the estimate? How many photons are needed for half this error?

**Solution.**

1. $P(L \gt 2) = e^{-0.5 \times 2} = e^{-1} \approx 0.3679$.
2. The standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.3679 \times 0.6321}{10{,}000}} = \sqrt{0.00002326} \approx 0.00482$.
3. The error is proportional to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. Half the error needs 4 times as many photons: $n = 40{,}000$.

**Answer.** About 36.8% pass, with a standard error of $0.0048$ for 10,000 photons, and 40,000 photons are needed to halve it.

**Check.** The simulation below repeats the estimate 500 times and finds a spread of $0.0048$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks the random walk (Examples 1 and 2). Part 2 checks decay, counting and the slab (Examples 3 and 4).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_walks, n_steps = 20_000, 1600

# A walker takes steps of +1 or -1 with probability 1/2 each, independently.
steps = rng.choice(np.array([-1, 1], dtype=np.int16), size=(n_walks, n_steps))
position = steps.cumsum(axis=1)

for n in [100, 400, 1600]:
    s = position[:, n - 1]
    print(f"n = {n:>4}: mean square {np.mean(s ** 2):7.1f} (exact {n:>4}), "
          f"rms {np.sqrt(np.mean(s ** 2)):5.2f} (exact {np.sqrt(n):5.2f})")

s100 = position[:, 99]
print(f"P(|S_100| <= 10): simulation {np.mean(np.abs(s100) <= 10):.4f}, exact {binom.cdf(55, 100, 0.5) - binom.cdf(44, 100, 0.5):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
n =  100: mean square    98.4 (exact  100), rms  9.92 (exact 10.00)
n =  400: mean square   396.1 (exact  400), rms 19.90 (exact 20.00)
n = 1600: mean square  1594.5 (exact 1600), rms 39.93 (exact 40.00)
P(|S_100| <= 10): simulation 0.7300, exact 0.7287
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import poisson

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Radioactive decay: each atom lives an Exp(lam) time. Half-life 10 days, so lam = ln 2 / 10.
lam = np.log(2) / 10
lifetimes = rng.exponential(1 / lam, size=1_000_000)
print(f"Median lifetime (half-life): {np.median(lifetimes):.2f} days   (exact 10.00)")
print(f"Fraction left after 30 days: {np.mean(lifetimes > 30):.4f}   (exact {np.exp(-lam * 30):.4f} = 1/8)")

# Counting: a source gives 20 decays per minute on average. Counts per minute are Poisson.
counts = rng.poisson(20, size=200_000)
print(f"Counts per minute: mean {counts.mean():.2f}, variance {counts.var():.2f} (both 20 in theory)")
print(f"P(count >= 30): simulation {np.mean(counts >= 30):.4f}, exact {poisson.sf(29, 20):.4f}")

# Photons through a slab 2 cm thick: free path ~ Exp(mu) with mu = 0.5 per cm. Passes if the path exceeds 2 cm.
mu, thickness, n_photons = 0.5, 2.0, 10_000
p = np.exp(-mu * thickness)
estimates = np.array([np.mean(rng.exponential(1 / mu, n_photons) > thickness) for _ in range(500)])
print(f"Transmitted fraction: mean of estimates {estimates.mean():.4f} (exact {p:.4f})")
print(f"Spread of the estimate with {n_photons} photons: {estimates.std():.4f} (exact {np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n_photons):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Median lifetime (half-life): 9.99 days   (exact 10.00)
Fraction left after 30 days: 0.1251   (exact 0.1250 = 1/8)
Counts per minute: mean 19.98, variance 19.93 (both 20 in theory)
P(count >= 30): simulation 0.0217, exact 0.0218
Transmitted fraction: mean of estimates 0.3679 (exact 0.3679)
Spread of the estimate with 10000 photons: 0.0048 (exact 0.0048)
```

The mean square position equals $n$, so the typical distance is $\sqrt{n}$: 10, 20 and 40 for $n = 100, 400, 1600$. The median lifetime is the half-life, the counts have equal mean and variance (Poisson), and the Monte Carlo spread follows $\sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Expecting the walker to drift away at a steady speed.** The mean position stays at 0, and the typical distance grows like $\sqrt{n}$, not like $n$. A random walk is slow. Only a walk with a bias (a drift) moves at a constant speed.
- **Thinking that the walker returns "to even out".** The walk has no memory. After 100 steps to the right there is no force that pulls it back. The walk only looks like it comes back because the typical distance grows slowly (the full story is in [18.2.4](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.4-Recurrence-and-Transience.md)).
- **Saying that "after two half-lives all atoms are gone".** After one half-life half remain, after two a quarter remains. The expected number halves each time and never reaches exactly zero.
- **Ignoring the counting noise.** A count of 20 has a standard deviation of 4.5. Comparing two counts of 20 and 25 does not show a change in the source.
- **Expecting a guaranteed time of the next decay.** An atom that has survived for a long time is not "due". Its remaining life has the same law as a new atom's, so the memoryless property holds.
- **Forgetting that Monte Carlo error falls slowly.** The error is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, so 100 times more particles give only 10 times more precision.

## Where It Is Used

- **Physics:** diffusion of particles and heat; Brownian motion; the noise of a counter (shot noise); the speed distribution of gas molecules, where each velocity component of a molecule is a normal variable and the speed is the square root of a sum of three squares ([9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)).
- **Monte Carlo in physics:** following particles through detectors and shields, and sampling states of a many-particle system with Markov chain Monte Carlo ([19.7](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.7-MCMC/README.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** the Gaussian noise that generative models add to data ([21.1.4](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.4-Generative-Models.md)), and the step noise of stochastic gradient descent, have the same $\sqrt{n}$ growth as a random walk when many small random steps add up.
- **Computer science:** random walks on graphs for ranking and sampling, and the $\sqrt{n}$ law for the spread of a sum of random values.
- **Everyday life:** why a smell crosses a room slowly, and why a lamp shade or a window dims light by a fixed fraction per layer.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A walker takes 400 steps of $\pm 1$. Find the mean and the standard deviation of its final position.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[S_{400}] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_{400}) = 400, \qquad \text{sd} = 20$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A substance has a half-life of 5 days. What fraction is left after 15 days, and after 12 days?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

After 15 days (3 half-lives) the fraction is $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125$. After 12 days (2.4 half-lives) it is

$$2^{-2.4} \approx 0.1895$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A particle makes a step of length 2 every 0.01 seconds. Find $D$ and the variance of its position after 9 seconds.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$D = \frac{\ell^2}{2 \tau} = \frac{4}{0.02} = 200$. Then:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X_9) = 2 D t = 2 \times 200 \times 9 = 3600, \qquad \text{sd} = 60$$

Check with steps: $\frac{9}{0.01} = 900$ steps, and $900 \times 4 = 3600$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A counter records about 400 decays in one measurement. What is the standard deviation of the count and the relative error? How many counts give a relative error of 1%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a Poisson count, the standard deviation is $\sqrt{400} = 20$, and the relative error is $\frac{20}{400} = 0.05$ (5%). The relative error is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}$, so for 1% we need

$$\mu = \left( \frac{1}{0.01} \right)^2 = 10{,}000 \text{ counts}$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that the free path through a material is exponential: if the particle is stopped in a thin layer of thickness $h$ with probability $\mu h$ (independently for each layer), then the probability of passing through a thickness $d$ is $e^{-\mu d}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cut the thickness $d$ into $m$ layers of thickness $h = \frac{d}{m}$. The particle passes each layer with probability $1 - \mu h = 1 - \frac{\mu d}{m}$, independently, so it passes all of them with probability $\left( 1 - \frac{\mu d}{m} \right)^m$. As $m \to \infty$, using $\left( 1 + \frac{x}{m} \right)^m \to e^{x}$ with $x = -\mu d$:

$$P(L \gt d) = \lim_{m \to \infty} \left( 1 - \frac{\mu d}{m} \right)^m = e^{-\mu d}$$

This is the survival function of $\text{Exp}(\mu)$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** A walker moves on a grid: each step is up, down, left or right, with probability $\frac{1}{4}$ each. Simulate 400 steps and check that the mean squared distance from the start is 400 and that the variance of the $x$-coordinate is 200.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_walks, n_steps = 50_000, 400

# A walker on a grid moves one step up, down, left or right, each with probability 1/4.
direction = rng.integers(0, 4, size=(n_walks, n_steps))
dx = np.where(direction == 0, 1, 0) - np.where(direction == 1, 1, 0)
dy = np.where(direction == 2, 1, 0) - np.where(direction == 3, 1, 0)
x, y = dx.sum(axis=1), dy.sum(axis=1)

print(f"Mean squared distance: simulation {np.mean(x ** 2 + y ** 2):.1f}, exact {n_steps}")
print(f"Variance of x: simulation {x.var():.1f}, exact {n_steps / 2:.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
Mean squared distance: simulation 397.5, exact 400
Variance of x: simulation 200.5, exact 200.0
```

Each step moves in $x$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and then by $\pm 1$. So each step adds $\frac{1}{2}$ to the variance of $x$, and each step adds exactly 1 to the squared distance.

</details>

## Summary

- A random walk of $n$ steps of $\pm 1$ has mean $0$ and variance $n$. The typical distance grows like $\sqrt{n}$, and the shape is a bell curve (CLT).
- With step $\ell$ every $\tau$ seconds, the variance of the position is $2 D t$ with $D = \frac{\ell^2}{2 \tau}$. This is diffusion, and its limit is Brownian motion.
- Atoms decay independently with exponential lifetimes: $N(t) \sim \text{Bin}(N_0, e^{-\lambda t})$ and the half-life is $\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$. Counts in a window are Poisson, with relative noise $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}$.
- A particle in matter has an exponential free path, so the unstopped fraction is $e^{-\mu d}$. Monte Carlo follows particles one by one, with an error that falls like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[S_n] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n, \qquad \text{typical distance} = \sqrt{n}}
$$
