> **Big idea:** Packets and requests arrive at random, so a server that is busy only 80% of the time still builds a line: with Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$ and exponential service at rate $\mu$, the number in the system is geometric with mean $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ where $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}$, and a finite buffer drops a packet with a probability we can compute.

---

## Why It Matters

Every router, web server, database and call center has a line in front of it. Engineers must answer questions such as: how long will a request wait, how big must a buffer be, and is it better to have one fast server or two slow ones? These questions have probability answers. [18.7](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.7-Queues-Intuition.md) developed the M/M/1 queue. This file uses it as a tool for networks: the load and delay, the buffer size and the chance of dropping a packet, and the effect of splitting a stream of requests over several servers.

## Intuition

A router forwards 1,000 packets per second on average. If packets arrived exactly every millisecond, it would never have a line: each packet is done just in time. But real packets arrive at random, sometimes 3 in a row, sometimes none for 5 ms. During a burst, packets wait. During a quiet moment, the router sits idle, and this lost time cannot be saved. The line is a result of randomness, and it grows quickly when the router is nearly always busy.

With 900 packets per second, the router is busy 90% of the time. The average number of packets in the router is not 0.9, but 9, and the average time spent in it is 10 times the time to forward one packet. At 99% load it is 99 packets. This is why engineers keep a link well below its full capacity.

## Definition

Packets arrive as a **Poisson process** with rate $\lambda$ (arrivals per second): the gaps between arrivals are independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ variables, with mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ ([18.5](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.5-Poisson-Process.md)). One server handles one packet at a time, and each service time is $\text{Exp}(\mu)$, with mean $\frac{1}{\mu}$. This is the **M/M/1 queue**: M for memoryless arrivals, M for memoryless service, 1 for one server. The **load** is

$$
\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}
$$

and the queue is stable only if $\rho \lt 1$. Let $N$ be the number of packets in the system (including the one in service). In the long run, for $\rho \lt 1$:

$$
P(N = n) = (1 - \rho) \rho^n, \qquad E[N] = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}, \qquad W = \frac{E[N]}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}
$$

where $W$ is the mean time a packet spends in the system, and the last formula is **Little's law**, $E[N] = \lambda W$.

**In words:** the server is idle with probability $1 - \rho$, the chance of having $n$ packets in the system drops by the factor $\rho$ at each step, and the mean number is $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$.

A router has only a finite memory. In the **M/M/1/K queue** the system holds at most $K$ packets (including the one in service), and an arriving packet that finds the system full is dropped. Then, for any $\rho \ne 1$:

$$
P(N = n) = \frac{(1 - \rho) \rho^n}{1 - \rho^{K+1}}, \quad n = 0, 1, \dots, K, \qquad P(\text{packet dropped}) = P(N = K)
$$

**In words:** it is the same geometric shape, cut off at $K$ and renormalized. A packet is dropped when it arrives and finds the system full.

Two facts about streams are used to build networks ([18.5](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.5-Poisson-Process.md)). **Merging** two independent Poisson streams with rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ gives a Poisson stream with rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. **Splitting** a Poisson stream with rate $\lambda$, by sending each packet independently to stream A with probability $p$, gives two independent Poisson streams with rates $p \lambda$ and $(1 - p) \lambda$.

## Why It Is True

**The stationary law of M/M/1.** The number $N$ goes up by 1 at rate $\lambda$ and, when $N \gt 0$, down by 1 at rate $\mu$ (this is a continuous-time Markov chain, [18.4](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.4-Continuous-Time-Markov-Chains.md)). Look at the line between state $n$ and state $n + 1$. The path crosses this line up and down in turn, so in the long run the number of upward and downward crossings is almost equal. Upward crossings happen at rate $\lambda \pi_n$ and downward ones at rate $\mu \pi_{n+1}$, where $\pi_n = P(N = n)$. So:

$$
\lambda \pi_n = \mu \pi_{n+1} \implies \pi_{n+1} = \rho \thinspace \pi_n \implies \pi_n = \rho^n \pi_0
$$

The probabilities sum to 1, and $\sum_{n \ge 0} \rho^n = \frac{1}{1 - \rho}$ for $\rho \lt 1$, so $\pi_0 = 1 - \rho$. This is the geometric law on $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$ ([7.3](../../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md)). Its mean is $\sum_{n} n (1 - \rho) \rho^n = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$, found in [18.7](../../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.7-Queues-Intuition.md), and Little's law gives $W = \frac{E[N]}{\lambda} = \frac{\rho}{\lambda (1 - \rho)} = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$.

**The finite buffer.** The same balance, $\pi_{n+1} = \rho \pi_n$, holds for $n = 0, \dots, K - 1$, and there are no states above $K$. So $\pi_n = \rho^n \pi_0$ for $n \le K$, and the sum of the finite geometric series gives $\pi_0 \cdot \frac{1 - \rho^{K+1}}{1 - \rho} = 1$, which is the formula above. (For $\rho = 1$, all $K + 1$ states have probability $\frac{1}{K+1}$.) The loss probability is $\pi_K$, because arrivals are Poisson: the future arrivals do not depend on the past, so an arriving packet sees the system as it is at a random moment ("Poisson arrivals see time averages"). A dropped packet is one that sees the state $K$.

**Merging and splitting.** Merging: the sum of independent $\text{Pois}(\lambda_1 t)$ and $\text{Pois}(\lambda_2 t)$ is $\text{Pois}((\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) t)$ ([15.3](../../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md)). Splitting: let $N$ be the number of arrivals in time $t$, so $N \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda t)$. Given $N = a + b$, the number sent to A is binomial. With $q = 1 - p$:

$$
P(N_A = a, N_B = b) = e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^{a+b}}{(a+b)!} \binom{a+b}{a} p^a q^b = \left( e^{-p \lambda t} \frac{(p \lambda t)^a}{a!} \right) \left( e^{-q \lambda t} \frac{(q \lambda t)^b}{b!} \right)
$$

This is a product of two Poisson PMFs, so $N_A \sim \text{Pois}(p \lambda t)$ and $N_B \sim \text{Pois}(q \lambda t)$ are independent.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Router at 90% Load

**Problem.** A router forwards packets at rate $\mu = 1000$ per second, and packets arrive at rate $\lambda = 900$ per second. Find $\rho$, the mean number in the router, the mean time in the router, and the probability that 20 or more packets are in it.

**Solution.**

1. $\rho = \frac{900}{1000} = 0.9$. The router is idle $1 - \rho = 0.1$ of the time (10%).
2. The mean number in the system: $E[N] = \frac{0.9}{1 - 0.9} = 9$.
3. The mean time: $W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{1}{100} = 0.01$ seconds, which is 10 milliseconds. Check with Little's law: $\lambda W = 900 \times 0.01 = 9 = E[N]$.
4. The tail of the geometric law is $P(N \ge k) = \rho^k$, so $P(N \ge 20) = 0.9^{20} \approx 0.1216$.

**Answer.** $\rho = 0.9$, on average 9 packets in the router, a mean time of 10 ms (10 times the service time of 1 ms), and a 12% chance of having 20 or more packets in it.

**Check.** Little's law agrees. At $\rho = 0.5$ the same router would have $E[N] = 1$ and $W = 2$ ms. Going from 50% to 90% load makes the delay 5 times longer.

### Example 2: How Big a Buffer?

**Problem.** A link has $\lambda = 8$ and $\mu = 10$ packets per millisecond ($\rho = 0.8$), and room for $K = 10$ packets in total. (a) What fraction of packets is dropped? (b) What is the smallest $K$ that drops at most 1%?

**Solution.**

1. (a) The loss probability is $\pi_K = \frac{(1 - \rho) \rho^K}{1 - \rho^{K+1}}$. With $K = 10$: $0.8^{10} = 0.10737$ and $0.8^{11} = 0.08590$, so

$$
\pi_{10} = \frac{0.2 \times 0.10737}{1 - 0.08590} = \frac{0.021475}{0.91410} \approx 0.0235
$$

2. (b) Try larger $K$. For $K = 13$: $0.8^{13} = 0.05498$, $0.8^{14} = 0.04398$, so $\pi_{13} = \frac{0.2 \times 0.05498}{1 - 0.04398} = \frac{0.010995}{0.95602} \approx 0.0115$, which is above 1%. For $K = 14$: $0.8^{14} = 0.04398$, $0.8^{15} = 0.03518$, so $\pi_{14} = \frac{0.2 \times 0.04398}{1 - 0.03518} = \frac{0.008796}{0.96482} \approx 0.0091$, which is below 1%.

**Answer.** About 2.35% of the packets are dropped with $K = 10$. The smallest buffer with a loss of at most 1% is $K = 14$.

**Check.** The loss is smaller than the infinite-buffer tail $P(N \ge 10) = 0.8^{10} \approx 0.1074$, because the infinite system can go beyond 10, and the finite one just drops packets. The simulation below gives $0.0232$. Each extra place in the buffer multiplies the loss by about $\rho = 0.8$ (from $K = 10$ to $K = 14$ the loss falls from 2.35% to 0.91%, which is a factor $0.39 \approx 0.8^4 = 0.41$).

### Example 3: One Fast Server or Two Slow Ones?

**Problem.** Requests arrive at rate $\lambda = 8$ per second. Option A: one server with rate $\mu = 10$. Option B: two servers with rate $5$ each, and every request is sent to one of them by a fair coin flip. Compare the mean time in the system.

**Solution.**

1. Option A: $\rho = 0.8$ and $W_A = \frac{1}{10 - 8} = 0.5$ seconds. The mean number of requests in the system is $\frac{0.8}{0.2} = 4$.
2. Option B: by splitting, each server gets an independent Poisson stream of rate $\frac{8}{2} = 4$. For each server $\rho = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$ and $W_B = \frac{1}{5 - 4} = 1$ second. The mean number is $\frac{0.8}{0.2} = 4$ at each server, so $8$ in total.
3. Check with Little's law for B: $\lambda W_B = 8 \times 1 = 8$.

**Answer.** Option A has a mean time of $0.5$ s and Option B has $1$ s. The one fast server gives half the delay.

**Check.** Both options have the same total capacity (10 per second) and the same load $\rho = 0.8$. The difference is that in B one server can be idle while requests wait at the other, so capacity is wasted. A single shared line for the two slow servers would waste no capacity, and is better than B. The simulation below confirms $0.5$ and $1.0$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 follows the M/M/1/K queue of Example 2 event by event and counts dropped packets. Part 2 simulates Example 3: one Poisson stream of rate 8, run through one fast server, or split by coin flips over two slow servers.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
lam, mu, K = 8.0, 10.0, 10                  # arrival rate, service rate, room for K packets in total
n_events = 3_000_000

# Follow the chain event by event: each event is an arrival with probability lam / (lam + mu).
is_arrival = rng.random(n_events) < lam / (lam + mu)
n, n_arrivals, n_lost = 0, 0, 0
time_in_state = np.zeros(K + 1)
for arrival in is_arrival:
    if arrival:
        n_arrivals += 1
        if n == K:
            n_lost += 1                     # buffer full: the packet is dropped
        else:
            n += 1
    elif n > 0:
        n -= 1                              # a service finishes
    time_in_state[n] += 1

rho = lam / mu
pi = rho ** np.arange(K + 1) * (1 - rho) / (1 - rho ** (K + 1))
print(f"P(packet lost):  simulation {n_lost / n_arrivals:.4f}, exact {pi[K]:.4f}")
print(f"P(N = 0):        simulation {time_in_state[0] / n_events:.4f}, exact {pi[0]:.4f}")
print(f"Infinite buffer: P(N >= {K}) = rho^{K} = {rho ** K:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(packet lost):  simulation 0.0232, exact 0.0235
P(N = 0):        simulation 0.2179, exact 0.2188
Infinite buffer: P(N >= 10) = rho^10 = 0.1074
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def mean_time_in_system(gaps, services):
    # Lindley recursion: wait[i] = max(0, wait[i-1] + service[i-1] - gap[i]); time in system = wait + service.
    wait = np.zeros(len(gaps))
    for i in range(1, len(gaps)):
        wait[i] = max(0.0, wait[i - 1] + services[i - 1] - gaps[i])
    return (wait + services).mean()

n, lam = 2_000_000, 8.0
arrive = rng.exponential(1 / lam, n).cumsum()          # one Poisson stream, rate 8

# (a) One fast server with service rate 10.
w_one = mean_time_in_system(np.diff(arrive, prepend=0), rng.exponential(1 / 10, n))

# (b) Two slow servers (rate 5 each); every packet goes to one of them by a fair coin flip.
to_a = rng.random(n) < 0.5
times = []
for mask in (to_a, ~to_a):
    t = arrive[mask]
    times.append(mean_time_in_system(np.diff(t, prepend=0), rng.exponential(1 / 5, len(t))))
w_two = np.mean(times)

print(f"One server, rate 10:                 W = {w_one:.3f}   exact {1 / (10 - 8):.3f}")
print(f"Two servers, rate 5, random split:   W = {w_two:.3f}   exact {1 / (5 - 4):.3f}")
```

Output:

```text
One server, rate 10:                 W = 0.497   exact 0.500
Two servers, rate 5, random split:   W = 1.006   exact 1.000
```

The simulated loss, 2.32%, is close to the exact 2.35%. In Part 2, the two halves of the stream behave as independent queues with rate 4 each (this is the splitting result), and the delay doubles compared with one fast server. At 80% load, a queue needs very many customers to settle, so the estimates are within about 1% of the exact values.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that a load below 100% means no line.** At $\rho = 0.8$ the mean number in the system is 4, and at $\rho = 0.95$ it is 19. Lines come from random bursts, not only from overload.
- **Using the M/M/1 formulas for $\rho \ge 1$.** Then there is no stationary law and the line grows without limit. The formula for $E[N]$ would give a negative number or infinity.
- **Confusing the rate with the mean time.** $\lambda = 8$ per second means a mean gap of $\frac{1}{8}$ second. $\mu = 10$ means a mean service time of $0.1$ second. Mixing them gives $\rho$ upside down.
- **Believing that the model fits all traffic.** Poisson arrivals and exponential services are a model. Traffic that comes in bursts has the same mean rate but makes longer lines than the formula says, so treat $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ as a lower estimate for bursty traffic.
- **Mixing the loss probability with the tail of the infinite queue.** The loss with a finite buffer is $\pi_K$ (here 2.35%), and the infinite-queue tail $P(N \ge K) = \rho^K$ (here 10.7%) is a different number.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science and networks:** buffers of routers and switches, thread pools and connection pools of servers, disk and database queues, and the choice between one shared queue and several separate ones. Little's law, $E[N] = \lambda W$, needs no model: if 200 requests per second each stay 0.5 seconds, then 100 requests are in the system on average.
- **AI and machine learning:** serving a model to many users is a queue. Requests arrive at random, and the delay grows sharply as the servers approach full load.
- **Telecommunications:** the same models are used for the number of calls in a telephone switch.
- **Operations and business:** call centers, checkout counters and hospital emergency rooms; one shared line for several counters is better than one line for each counter (compare Example 3).
- **Everyday life:** why a road or a bus stop is slow at rush hour, even when the road is not completely full.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Requests arrive at a server at rate $\lambda = 6$ per second and the server has rate $\mu = 8$ per second. Find $\rho$, the mean number in the system and the mean time in the system.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\rho = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75$. Then:

$$E[N] = \frac{0.75}{0.25} = 3, \qquad W = \frac{1}{8 - 6} = 0.5 \text{ seconds}$$

Little's law agrees: $\lambda W = 6 \times 0.5 = 3$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Two independent Poisson streams of 120 and 80 requests per second are merged and sent to one server with rate 250 per second. Find the load and the mean number in the system.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The merged stream is Poisson with rate $120 + 80 = 200$ per second. Then $\rho = \frac{200}{250} = 0.8$ and:

$$E[N] = \frac{0.8}{1 - 0.8} = 4$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A system holds at most $K = 3$ packets, with $\rho = 0.5$. Find the stationary distribution and the probability that an arriving packet is dropped.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The probabilities are proportional to $1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125$, which add up to $1.875$. So $\pi_0 = \frac{1}{1.875} = 0.5333$, $\pi_1 = 0.2667$, $\pi_2 = 0.1333$, and:

$$\pi_3 = \frac{0.125}{1.875} = \frac{1}{15} \approx 0.0667$$

The formula agrees: $\frac{0.5 \times 0.125}{1 - 0.0625} = \frac{0.0625}{0.9375} = \frac{1}{15}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A link has $\mu = 1000$ packets per second. What is the largest arrival rate for which the mean time in the system is at most 20 ms?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need $W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} \le 0.02$, so $\mu - \lambda \ge 50$:

$$\lambda \le 1000 - 50 = 950 \text{ packets per second}$$

This is a load of $\rho = 0.95$. The mean number in the system at that load is 19.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For the M/M/1/K queue with $\rho \gt 1$, show that the loss probability tends to $1 - \frac{1}{\rho}$ as $K \to \infty$. Explain this answer in words.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $\rho \ne 1$ the loss is $\pi_K = \frac{(1 - \rho) \rho^K}{1 - \rho^{K+1}}$. For $\rho \gt 1$, divide the top and the bottom by $\rho^{K+1}$:

$$\pi_K = \frac{(1 - \rho) \rho^{-1}}{\rho^{-(K+1)} - 1} \to \frac{(1 - \rho)/\rho}{-1} = \frac{\rho - 1}{\rho} = 1 - \frac{1}{\rho}$$

In words: the system is almost always full, so it serves at the full rate $\mu$. The accepted packets then arrive at rate $\lambda (1 - \pi_K) \to \lambda \cdot \frac{1}{\rho} = \mu$. The server can do no more than $\mu$ per second, so the extra traffic $\lambda - \mu$ is dropped.

</details>

## Summary

- Random arrivals make lines even below full load. For Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$ and exponential service at rate $\mu$, with $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} \lt 1$, the number in the system is geometric: $P(N = n) = (1 - \rho) \rho^n$, with mean $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ and mean time $W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$.
- Little's law, $E[N] = \lambda W$, links the line length to the delay, and holds for any stable queue.
- A buffer of size $K$ gives the same geometric shape cut at $K$. The drop probability is $\pi_K = \frac{(1 - \rho) \rho^K}{1 - \rho^{K+1}}$, and each extra place cuts it by about the factor $\rho$.
- Merging Poisson streams adds the rates. Random splitting gives independent Poisson streams. A single fast server beats the same capacity split over separate queues.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[N] = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}, \qquad W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}, \qquad \rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}}
$$
