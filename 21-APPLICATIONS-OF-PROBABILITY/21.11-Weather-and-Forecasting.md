> **Big idea:** A weather forecast is a conditional probability (the chance of rain given what we know today) that fades toward the long-run average as we look further ahead, an ensemble of simulated futures estimates it with a Monte Carlo error of $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{M}}$, and the Brier score $E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2]$ checks whether the forecasts are any good.

---

## Why It Matters

"There is a 70% chance of rain tomorrow." What does this mean, how is it computed, and how can we tell whether the forecaster is any good? All three questions are probability questions. The meaning is a conditional probability and a long-run frequency (Modules 03 and 04). The computation uses a model of how today's weather leads to tomorrow's, and a Monte Carlo ensemble of simulated tomorrows (Modules 18 and 19). The check is a score such as the Brier score, which was introduced in [21.1.6](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md). Here we use a tiny weather model with exact answers, so that every step can be checked. Real forecasting models are far more complex, and the numbers here are made up.

## Intuition

Look at two days in a row. If today is wet, tomorrow is more likely to be wet than on an average day, since weather systems last for a while. If today is dry, tomorrow is more likely to be dry. So the best forecast for tomorrow is not the average rate of rain, but the rain rate **given today's weather**. That is a conditional probability.

How much does today help for the day after tomorrow? A little less. And for a day 10 days ahead? Almost nothing: the effect of today has faded, and the best forecast is the plain long-run rate of rain, called the **climatology**. This is why forecasts for a few days ahead are useful and forecasts for a month ahead are only about averages.

How do forecasters get a probability from a complicated model? They run the model many times, each time with a slightly different starting point, and count: if 14 of 20 runs (**ensemble members**) show rain, the forecast is 70%. That is a Monte Carlo estimate. Finally, to judge a forecaster, we collect many forecasts and outcomes and compute the average squared error of the probabilities, the Brier score.

## Definition

**The weather chain.** Let $Y_t = 1$ if day $t$ is wet and $0$ if it is dry. Suppose

$$
P(Y_{t+1} = 1 \mid Y_t = 1) = a, \qquad P(Y_{t+1} = 1 \mid Y_t = 0) = b
$$

and that tomorrow depends on the past only through today (a Markov chain, [18.3.1](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.3-Markov-Chains/18.3.1-Markov-Property.md)). In the examples, $a = 0.7$ and $b = 0.2$. The long-run fraction of wet days, the **climatology**, is $\pi = \frac{b}{1 - a + b}$, and the chance of a wet day $k$ days after today is

$$
P(Y_{t+k} = 1 \mid Y_t = y) = \pi + (y - \pi)(a - b)^k
$$

**In words:** the forecast starts at today's state ($y = 1$ or $0$) and moves toward the climatology $\pi$, losing the fraction $\lvert a - b \rvert$ of the remaining gap at each day.

**An ensemble forecast.** Run $M$ independent simulations of tomorrow (members). If $N$ members show rain, the forecast probability is $\hat{p} = \frac{N}{M}$. When each member shows rain with probability $p$:

$$
N \sim \text{Bin}(M, p), \qquad E[\hat{p}] = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1 - p)}{M}
$$

**In words:** the ensemble gives an unbiased estimate of the forecast probability, with a Monte Carlo error that shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}$.

**The Brier score.** For forecasts $\hat{p}_1, \dots, \hat{p}_n$ of events with outcomes $Y_i \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$:

$$
\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{p}_i - Y_i)^2 \approx E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2]
$$

Smaller is better. A **reference forecast** is the climatology, which always says $\pi$ and has $\text{BS}_{\text{ref}} = \pi(1 - \pi)$. The **Brier skill score** is

$$
\text{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\text{BS}}{\text{BS}_{\text{ref}}}
$$

**In words:** the skill score is 0 for a forecaster who is no better than the long-run rate, and 1 for a perfect forecaster. The Brier score is a **proper** score: a forecaster who believes the chance is $q$ gets the best expected score by reporting $q$ (problem 5).

## Why It Is True

**The forecast $k$ days ahead.** Let $p_k = P(Y_{t+k} = 1 \mid Y_t = y)$ and $p_0 = y$. By the law of total probability ([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)), conditioning on the state on day $k$:

$$
p_{k+1} = a \thinspace p_k + b (1 - p_k) = b + (a - b) p_k
$$

The long-run value $\pi$ solves $\pi = b + (a - b) \pi$, so $\pi = \frac{b}{1 - a + b}$. Subtract this equation from the previous one:

$$
p_{k+1} - \pi = (a - b)(p_k - \pi) \implies p_k - \pi = (a - b)^k (p_0 - \pi)
$$

With $a = 0.7$, $b = 0.2$: $\pi = \frac{0.2}{0.5} = 0.4$ and $a - b = 0.5$, so $p_k = 0.4 + (y - 0.4) \cdot 0.5^k$. After a wet day this is $0.4 + 0.6 \cdot 0.5^k$, and after a dry day it is $0.4 - 0.4 \cdot 0.5^k$. Because $\lvert a - b \rvert \lt 1$, the memory fades geometrically.

**An uncertain start.** If today's state is not known, but wet with probability $w$, then by the law of total probability the forecast for tomorrow is $w \cdot a + (1 - w) \cdot b$. An ensemble handles this by letting each member first draw today's state, and then tomorrow's.

**The ensemble.** Each member is an independent yes/no event with the same probability $p$, so the count is binomial and the variance of $\hat{p}$ is $\frac{p(1-p)}{M}$ (the binomial variance $Mp(1-p)$ divided by $M^2$). The ensemble is a Monte Carlo estimate of the probability ([19.3](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md)).

**The Brier score of an ensemble.** Say the exact forecast probability is $p$ (which can depend on today), the ensemble gives $\hat{p}$ with $E[\hat{p} \mid p] = p$, and the outcome $Y$ is Bernoulli($p$), independent of the ensemble given $p$. Then $\hat{p} - p$ and $p - Y$ are uncorrelated given $p$, so

$$
E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2] = E[(p - Y)^2] + E[(\hat{p} - p)^2] = E[p(1 - p)] + \frac{E[p(1 - p)]}{M} = E[p(1 - p)] \left( 1 + \frac{1}{M} \right)
$$

The first term is the Brier score of the best possible forecast (it is the refinement term of [21.1.6](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)). The second is the price of having only $M$ members.

**The proper score.** The forecaster believes that $P(Y = 1) = q$ and reports $r$. The expected score is $E[(r - Y)^2] = q(1 - r)^2 + (1 - q) r^2$. Its derivative with respect to $r$ is $-2q(1 - r) + 2(1 - q) r = 2(r - q)$, which is zero at $r = q$, and the second derivative is $2 \gt 0$. So the minimum is at $r = q$, with value $q(1 - q)$. A forecaster who "hedges" or exaggerates pays a price.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Looking Ahead From a Wet Day

**Problem.** In the weather chain with $a = 0.7$ and $b = 0.2$, today is wet. Find the chance of rain on each of the next 1, 2, 3 and 7 days. Find also the probability of rain on both of the next two days, and the probability that three days in a row after a dry day are dry.

**Solution.**

1. The climatology is $\pi = \frac{0.2}{1 - 0.7 + 0.2} = \frac{0.2}{0.5} = 0.4$, and $a - b = 0.5$.
2. After a wet day: $p_k = 0.4 + 0.6 \times 0.5^k$. So $p_1 = 0.4 + 0.3 = 0.70$, $p_2 = 0.4 + 0.15 = 0.55$, $p_3 = 0.4 + 0.075 = 0.475$ and $p_7 = 0.4 + 0.6 \times 0.0078125 = 0.4047$.
3. Check $p_2$ directly: $0.7 \times 0.7 + 0.3 \times 0.2 = 0.49 + 0.06 = 0.55$ (wet then wet, or dry then wet).
4. Rain on both of the next two days: $0.7 \times 0.7 = 0.49$ (the chain rule, [4.2](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.2-Multiplication-Rule-and-Chain-Rule.md)).
5. Three dry days in a row after a dry day: $0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.512$.

**Answer.** The chance of rain is $0.70$, $0.55$, $0.475$ and $0.405$ on days 1, 2, 3 and 7. Two wet days in a row has probability $0.49$, and three dry days after a dry day $0.512$.

**Check.** The values fall toward the climatology $0.4$, the gap shrinking by half each day. The simulation below gives $0.7003$, $0.5499$, $0.4747$ and $0.4048$.

### Example 2: How Many Ensemble Members?

**Problem.** An ensemble forecasts tomorrow's rain, and the true probability is $p = 0.7$. Find the standard deviation of the ensemble probability for $M = 20$, $M = 100$ and $M = 500$. How many members give a standard deviation of at most $0.02$? If today's state is uncertain (wet with probability $0.6$), what is the forecast probability?

**Solution.**

1. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.21}{M}}$. For $M = 20$: $\sqrt{0.0105} = 0.1025$. For $M = 100$: $\sqrt{0.0021} = 0.0458$. For $M = 500$: $\sqrt{0.00042} = 0.0205$.
2. For a standard deviation of at most $0.02$: $\frac{0.21}{M} \le 0.0004$, so $M \ge 525$.
3. With an uncertain start: $P(\text{wet tomorrow}) = 0.6 \times 0.7 + 0.4 \times 0.2 = 0.42 + 0.08 = 0.50$.

**Answer.** Standard deviations $0.1025$, $0.0458$ and $0.0205$; at least 525 members for $0.02$; and a forecast of $0.50$ if today's state is uncertain.

**Check.** With 20 members, the forecast of "70%" can easily be 60% or 80% by chance (one standard deviation is 10 points). Four times as many members only halve the error. The simulation below gives $0.1025$, $0.0459$ and $0.0205$, and $0.4972$ for the uncertain start.

### Example 3: Scoring Four Forecasters

**Problem.** (a) Five days have forecasts $0.9, 0.8, 0.1, 0.3, 0.6$ and outcomes (1 = rain) $1, 1, 0, 1, 0$. Find the Brier score. (b) In the weather chain, compare four forecasters of tomorrow's rain: climatology (always $0.4$), the model ($0.7$ after a wet day and $0.2$ after a dry day), an overconfident forecaster ($0.95$ and $0.05$), and persistence (1 after a wet day, 0 after a dry day).

**Solution.**

1. (a) The squared errors are $(0.9 - 1)^2 = 0.01$, $(0.8 - 1)^2 = 0.04$, $(0.1 - 0)^2 = 0.01$, $(0.3 - 1)^2 = 0.49$, $(0.6 - 0)^2 = 0.36$. The sum is $0.91$, and the Brier score is $\frac{0.91}{5} = 0.182$.
2. (b) Today is wet with probability $0.4$ and dry with probability $0.6$. For a forecaster who says $f$ when today is wet: the squared error is $0.7(1 - f)^2 + 0.3 f^2$. For one who says $f$ when today is dry: $0.2(1 - f)^2 + 0.8 f^2$.
3. Climatology ($f = 0.4$ always): $\text{BS} = \pi(1 - \pi) = 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.24$.
4. Model: $0.4 \times (0.7 \times 0.3) + 0.6 \times (0.2 \times 0.8) = 0.4 \times 0.21 + 0.6 \times 0.16 = 0.084 + 0.096 = 0.18$.
5. Overconfident: wet today, $f = 0.95$: $0.7 \times 0.0025 + 0.3 \times 0.9025 = 0.00175 + 0.27075 = 0.2725$. Dry today, $f = 0.05$: $0.2 \times 0.9025 + 0.8 \times 0.0025 = 0.1805 + 0.002 = 0.1825$. Then $\text{BS} = 0.4 \times 0.2725 + 0.6 \times 0.1825 = 0.109 + 0.1095 = 0.2185$.
6. Persistence: the forecast is wrong when the weather changes, which has probability $0.4 \times 0.3 + 0.6 \times 0.2 = 0.24$, and each mistake costs 1. So $\text{BS} = 0.24$.
7. Skill scores, with $\text{BS}_{\text{ref}} = 0.24$: model $1 - \frac{0.18}{0.24} = 0.25$; overconfident $1 - \frac{0.2185}{0.24} \approx 0.09$; persistence $0$.

**Answer.** (a) $0.182$. (b) Brier scores $0.24$ (climatology), $0.18$ (model), $0.2185$ (overconfident), $0.24$ (persistence). Only the model, which says exactly the conditional probability, has a real skill of $0.25$.

**Check.** The honest model wins, as the proper-score argument says. The overconfident forecaster uses the right information but exaggerates it, and loses most of its skill. Persistence never says "maybe", and is no better than the climatology. The simulation below gives $0.2401$, $0.1800$, $0.2184$ and $0.2399$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 simulates a million days of the weather chain, and checks Examples 1 and 3. Part 2 checks the ensemble of Example 2, and the Brier score of an ensemble forecast.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Wet/dry model: P(wet tomorrow | wet today) = 0.7 and P(wet tomorrow | dry today) = 0.2.
p_wet_given = {1: 0.7, 0: 0.2}
n_days = 1_000_000
u = rng.random(n_days)
wet = np.zeros(n_days, dtype=int)
for t in range(1, n_days):
    wet[t] = u[t] < p_wet_given[wet[t - 1]]

print(f"Fraction of wet days: {wet.mean():.4f}   (exact 0.4)")

# Chance of rain k days after a wet day: 0.4 + 0.6 * 0.5^k.
for k in [1, 2, 3, 7]:
    outcome = wet[k:][wet[:-k] == 1]
    print(f"k = {k}: P(wet | wet {k} day(s) ago) = {outcome.mean():.4f}   (exact {0.4 + 0.6 * 0.5 ** k:.4f})")

# Brier scores for four forecasters of tomorrow's rain, on the same days.
today, tomorrow = wet[:-1], wet[1:]
forecasts = {
    "climatology (always 0.4)":       (np.full(n_days - 1, 0.4), 0.24),
    "model (0.7 if wet, 0.2 if dry)": (np.where(today == 1, 0.7, 0.2), 0.18),
    "overconfident (0.95 / 0.05)":    (np.where(today == 1, 0.95, 0.05), 0.2185),
    "persistence (1 if wet, else 0)": (today.astype(float), 0.24),
}
for name, (f, exact) in forecasts.items():
    print(f"{name:<32} Brier {np.mean((f - tomorrow) ** 2):.4f} (exact {exact:.4f})")
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_forecasts = 100_000

# Ensemble: M members simulate tomorrow from today's state (wet), each wet with probability 0.7.
for n_members in [20, 100, 500]:
    p_hat = rng.binomial(n_members, 0.7, size=n_forecasts) / n_members       # fraction of members that are wet
    print(f"M = {n_members:>3}: sd of the ensemble probability {p_hat.std():.4f}   (exact {np.sqrt(0.7 * 0.3 / n_members):.4f})")

# Today's state is uncertain: wet with probability 0.6. Each member first draws today's state, then tomorrow's.
n_members = 100_000
today_wet = rng.random(n_members) < 0.6
tomorrow_wet = rng.random(n_members) < np.where(today_wet, 0.7, 0.2)
print(f"Uncertain start: P(wet tomorrow) = {tomorrow_wet.mean():.4f}   (exact 0.5000)")

# Brier score of an ensemble forecast with M members, against the best possible forecast (0.7 or 0.2).
today = rng.random(n_forecasts) < 0.4
true_p = np.where(today, 0.7, 0.2)
outcome = rng.random(n_forecasts) < true_p
for n_members in [5, 20, 100]:
    p_hat = rng.binomial(n_members, true_p) / n_members
    print(f"M = {n_members:>3}: Brier score {np.mean((p_hat - outcome) ** 2):.4f}   (exact {0.18 * (1 + 1 / n_members):.4f})")
```

M =  20: sd of the ensemble probability 0.1025   (exact 0.1025)
M = 100: sd of the ensemble probability 0.0459   (exact 0.0458)
M = 500: sd of the ensemble probability 0.0205   (exact 0.0205)
Uncertain start: P(wet tomorrow) = 0.4972   (exact 0.5000)

The forecast moves from today's state toward $0.4$ as the horizon grows. The honest model has the best Brier score, $0.18$, the overconfident forecaster is worse, and persistence is no better than the climatology. The ensemble Brier score follows the formula $0.18 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{M} \right)$: a small ensemble costs accuracy, and 100 members is already close to the best possible $0.18$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading "70% chance of rain" as "70% of the area" or "rain 70% of the time".** The usual meaning is the probability that rain happens at a given place (and in a given period). A forecaster with many such forecasts is judged by whether it rains on about 70% of the days with that forecast.
- **Judging a forecaster by one day.** A forecast of 70% is not "wrong" if it stays dry: 30% of the time it should. Collect many forecasts and look at the Brier score or at calibration ([21.1.6](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)).
- **Confusing calibration with skill.** The climatology (always $0.4$) is perfectly calibrated and has zero skill. A forecast must also separate the wet days from the dry days.
- **Rounding forecasts to 0 and 1.** A forecaster who says "rain" or "no rain" has a Brier score equal to the fraction of mistakes ($0.24$ for persistence). Honest probabilities, such as $0.7$ and $0.2$, score better ($0.18$).
- **Believing that more ensemble members give a better model.** More members only reduce the Monte Carlo error $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{M}}$. If the model itself is wrong, a million members give a precise wrong answer.
- **Expecting skill far into the future.** Here the effect of today is cut by half every day. Real weather has a similar loss of memory, and beyond some days the best forecast is the climatology.

## Where It Is Used

- **Weather and climate:** probability of precipitation, ensembles of simulated futures, and the Brier score and skill score to compare forecasting systems.
- **AI and machine learning:** a classifier's probability should be calibrated and is scored with the Brier score or the log loss ([21.1.2](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.2-Loss-Functions-as-Probability.md), [21.1.6](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)). A model ensemble is the same idea as a weather ensemble.
- **Statistics and decision-making:** probabilities of forecasts are used to decide: carry an umbrella if the chance of rain times the cost of getting wet is larger than the cost of carrying it (expected value, [21.10](21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).
- **Engineering and business:** forecasts of demand, traffic, or server load that give a probability, not a single number, and are checked in the same way.
- **Everyday life:** keep a note of your own predictions ("I am 80% sure") and check how often you were right.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\pi = \frac{b}{1 - a + b} = \frac{0.1}{0.3} = \frac{1}{3}$ and $a - b = 0.7$. After a wet day:

$$p_3 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} \times 0.7^3 = 0.3333 + 0.6667 \times 0.343 = 0.562$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** An ensemble forecast of a probability near $0.7$ should have a standard deviation of at most $0.02$ from the Monte Carlo error. How many members are needed? Is the number different for $p = 0.5$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need $\frac{p(1-p)}{M} \le 0.0004$. For $p = 0.7$: $M \ge \frac{0.21}{0.0004} = 525$. For $p = 0.5$ the product is $0.25$, so $M \ge \frac{0.25}{0.0004} = 625$. The worst case is $p = 0.5$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A forecaster believes that the probability of rain is $q$, and reports $r$. Show that the expected Brier score is smallest at $r = q$, and find its value there. Why does this make the score "proper"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The expected score is $f(r) = q(1 - r)^2 + (1 - q) r^2$. Its derivative is $f'(r) = -2q(1 - r) + 2(1 - q) r = 2(r - q)$, which is negative for $r \lt q$, zero at $r = q$, and positive for $r \gt q$. So $r = q$ is the minimum, and

$$f(q) = q(1 - q)^2 + (1 - q) q^2 = q(1 - q)\big( (1 - q) + q \big) = q(1 - q)$$

A forecaster who wants a small score cannot gain by exaggerating or hedging: honesty is the best policy.

</details>

The smallest score is at the honest report $0.3$, with the value $q(1 - q) = 0.21$.

</details>

## Summary

- A forecast is a conditional probability. In a wet/dry Markov chain, the chance of rain $k$ days after today is $\pi + (y - \pi)(a - b)^k$: it starts at today's state and moves to the climatology $\pi$ as the horizon grows.
- An ensemble of $M$ simulated futures estimates the forecast probability by the fraction of members that show the event. It is unbiased, with a Monte Carlo standard deviation $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{M}}$, and an uncertain start is handled by the law of total probability.
- The Brier score $E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2]$ is a proper score: honesty gives the best expected score. The skill score $1 - \frac{\text{BS}}{\text{BS}_{\text{ref}}}$ compares a forecast with the climatology.
- A forecast must be both calibrated and sharp: the climatology is calibrated and has no skill, and overconfidence loses skill.
- These are simplified models with made-up numbers.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{BS} = E[(\hat{p} - Y)^2], \qquad P(\text{wet on day } k) = \pi + (y - \pi)(a - b)^k}
$$
