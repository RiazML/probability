> **Big idea:** Heredity is a sequence of fair coin flips (each parent passes on one of its two gene copies at random), so genotypes follow the binomial law and, with random mating, the Hardy-Weinberg proportions $p^2$, $2pq$ and $q^2$; and the fate of a population or of a new mutation is a random process, a resampling with drift or a branching process that dies out with the smallest root of $s = G(s)$.

---

## Why It Matters

Biology is full of chance. Which of its parent's two gene copies does a child receive? How common is a recessive disease carrier in a population? Will a new mutation spread or disappear? Each question is a probability question with an exact answer. The tools are the ones you know: independent trials and the binomial law, the multinomial law for counts, conditional expectation and martingales, and the generating function of a branching process. This file shows four small models: Mendel's rules, the Hardy-Weinberg law, genetic drift in a small population, and the survival of a new mutation. They are simplified models, so we state their assumptions clearly. The numbers are made up for learning.

## Intuition

Each person has two copies of most genes, one from each parent. Suppose a gene has two versions (**alleles**), $A$ and $a$, and a parent has the pair $Aa$. The parent passes on $A$ or $a$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, like a coin flip. If both parents are $Aa$, then each child gets one coin flip from each parent. The child is $AA$ with probability $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$, $aa$ with probability $\frac{1}{4}$, and $Aa$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ (two ways).

Now zoom out to a whole population. If 30% of all gene copies are $A$, and people pair up without regard to this gene, then a child's two copies are like two independent draws from a bag with 30% $A$ stones. So the child is $AA$ with probability $0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$. Notice that the 70% of $a$ copies hide in many $Aa$ carriers: a recessive trait that is rare can have many carriers.

In a small population, the next generation is a random sample of the copies in this generation, so the frequency of $A$ jumps up and down by chance, until one version takes over. This is **genetic drift**. And a brand new mutation starts as one copy. Whether it survives is like asking whether a family name survives: a branching process.

## Picture

```text
                 A (0.3)        a (0.7)

```

Top: the Punnett square of one cross. Bottom: the same square with unequal frequencies. The cell probabilities multiply because the two alleles are independent draws.

## Definition

**Mendel's rule.** Each parent passes on one of its two alleles, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, independently of the other parent and of the other children. In a cross $Aa \times Aa$, a child is $aa$ with probability $\frac{1}{4}$. For $n$ children, the number of $aa$ children is:

$$
N_{aa} \sim \text{Bin}\left( n, \frac{1}{4} \right)
$$

**The Hardy-Weinberg law.** In a population, let $p$ be the fraction of all gene copies that are $A$, and $q = 1 - p$ the fraction that are $a$ (the **allele frequencies**). Under **random mating** (each child gets two alleles drawn independently from the gene pool), with a large population, no selection, no mutation and no migration, the genotype probabilities are:

$$
P(AA) = p^2, \qquad P(Aa) = 2pq, \qquad P(aa) = q^2
$$

and the allele frequency in the next generation is still $p$. Among $n$ people, the genotype counts are $\text{Mult}(n, p^2, 2pq, q^2)$.

**In words:** the two alleles of a child are independent draws, so the genotype frequencies are the products of the allele frequencies, and they do not change from one generation to the next.

**Genetic drift (the Wright-Fisher model).** A population of $N$ individuals has $2N$ gene copies. Let $X_t$ be the number of copies of $A$ in generation $t$. The next generation draws $2N$ copies at random, with replacement, from this one:

$$
X_{t+1} \mid X_t = x \sim \text{Bin}\left( 2N, \frac{x}{2N} \right)
$$

**In words:** the next generation is a random sample of the present one, so the frequency of $A$ is a random walk that stops when $A$ is lost ($X = 0$) or takes over (**fixation**, $X = 2N$).

**A branching process for a new mutation.** A new mutant copy leaves a random number $\xi$ of copies in the next generation, independently for each copy, with mean $m = E[\xi]$. If $\xi \sim \text{Pois}(m)$, the probability generating function is $G(s) = e^{m(s - 1)}$. The probability $q$ that the line of copies dies out is the smallest root of:

$$
s = G(s) = e^{m(s - 1)}
$$

**In words:** a mutation with $m \le 1$ always dies out. With $m \gt 1$ it survives with probability $1 - q \gt 0$ (a beneficial mutation can still be lost by bad luck).

## Why It Is True

**Mendel.** The choices of the two parents are independent, so $P(aa) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$. Children are independent of each other, so the number of $aa$ children among $n$ is a count of successes in $n$ independent trials: binomial ([7.2](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md)).

**Hardy-Weinberg.** A random allele from the gene pool is $A$ with probability $p$. The two alleles of a child are independent draws ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)). So $P(AA) = p \cdot p$, $P(aa) = q \cdot q$, and $Aa$ happens in two ways (first allele $A$ and second $a$, or the other order): $P(Aa) = pq + qp = 2pq$. The new allele frequency of $A$ is the share of $A$ copies among the $2n$ copies of $n$ children. Each $AA$ child has two, each $Aa$ child has one:

$$
p^2 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2pq = p^2 + pq = p(p + q) = p
$$

So the frequency does not change, and neither do the genotype frequencies: they are reached after one generation of random mating, from any starting genotype frequencies (problem 5).

**Drift: the mean stays and the variance is $\frac{p(1-p)}{2N}$.** Given $X_t = x$, the variable $X_{t+1}$ is binomial with $2N$ trials and probability $\frac{x}{2N}$, so $E[X_{t+1} \mid X_t] = x$. The frequency $\frac{X_t}{2N}$ is therefore a **martingale** ([20.8](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.8-Martingales.md)): its expected next value equals its present value. The variance of the next frequency, given the present frequency $p$, is $\frac{2N p(1-p)}{(2N)^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}$ (binomial variance divided by $(2N)^2$). The walk is bounded between $0$ and $2N$, and the states $0$ and $2N$ are absorbing. Eventually it ends at one of them, and by optional stopping for bounded martingales ([20.9](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.9-Stopping-Times-and-Optional-Stopping.md)) the mean at the end is the mean at the start:

$$
2N \cdot P(\text{fixation}) + 0 \cdot P(\text{loss}) = x_0 \implies P(\text{fixation of } A) = \frac{x_0}{2N} = p_0
$$

So an allele with frequency $p_0$ takes over with probability exactly $p_0$, even though neither allele has any advantage. This is the same fair-game argument as for the gambler's ruin ([18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md)).

**Branching.** From [18.6](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.6-Branching-Processes.md), the extinction probability $q$ of a branching process is the smallest root in $[0, 1]$ of $s = G(s)$, and $q = 1$ if and only if $m \le 1$. For the Poisson law, $G(s) = \sum_{k} e^{-m} \frac{m^k}{k!} s^k = e^{-m} e^{m s} = e^{m(s - 1)}$. For $m$ slightly above 1, the survival probability $1 - q$ is roughly $2(m - 1)$. Each of $k$ independent mutant copies has its own line, so all of them die out with probability $q^k$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Family With a Recessive Trait

**Problem.** Two parents are carriers ($Aa$) of a recessive trait: a child shows the trait only if it is $aa$. They have 4 children. Find the probability that at least one child shows the trait, and the probability that exactly one does.

**Solution.**

1. Each child is $aa$ with probability $\frac{1}{4}$, independently. So $N_{aa} \sim \text{Bin}(4, \frac{1}{4})$.
2. $P(N_{aa} = 0) = \left( \frac{3}{4} \right)^4 = \frac{81}{256} \approx 0.3164$. So $P(N_{aa} \ge 1) = 1 - 0.3164 = 0.6836$.
3. $P(N_{aa} = 1) = \binom{4}{1} \cdot \frac{1}{4} \cdot \left( \frac{3}{4} \right)^3 = 4 \times 0.25 \times 0.421875 = 0.421875$.

**Answer.** $P(\text{at least one}) = 0.6836$ and $P(\text{exactly one}) = 0.4219$.

**Check.** The expected number is $4 \times \frac{1}{4} = 1$ child, and "at least one" has a probability of about 68%, not 100%: carriers can have 4 children without the trait. The simulation below gives $0.6836$ and $0.4218$.

### Example 2: Carriers and Counts in a Population

**Problem.** (a) A recessive condition affects $q^2 = \frac{1}{10{,}000}$ of the people (made-up). Assuming Hardy-Weinberg proportions, find the fraction of carriers. (b) In a sample of 1,000 people there are 370 $AA$, 460 $Aa$ and 170 $aa$. Estimate $p$ and the counts expected under Hardy-Weinberg.

**Solution.**

1. (a) $q = \sqrt{0.0001} = 0.01$ and $p = 0.99$. The carriers are $Aa$: $2pq = 2 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 = 0.0198$.
2. (a) The ratio of carriers to affected people is $\frac{0.0198}{0.0001} = 198$.
3. (b) Count the copies of $A$: each $AA$ has 2, each $Aa$ has 1. There are $2 \times 370 + 460 = 1200$ copies of $A$ among $2000$ copies, so $\hat{p} = 0.6$ and $\hat{q} = 0.4$.
4. (b) Expected counts: $AA$: $1000 \times 0.36 = 360$, $Aa$: $1000 \times 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 480$, $aa$: $1000 \times 0.16 = 160$.

**Answer.** (a) About 2% of the people (about 1 in 50) are carriers, 198 carriers for each affected person. (b) $\hat{p} = 0.6$ and the expected counts are $360$, $480$ and $160$, close to the observed $370$, $460$ and $170$.

**Check.** The expected counts add to $1000$. Most copies of a rare recessive allele are hidden in carriers (there are 198 carriers with one copy each for every affected person with two copies). How close is "close" is a question for a statistical test ([21.2](21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)), which we do not do here.

### Example 3: Drift in a Small Population

**Problem.** A population has $N = 50$ individuals (100 gene copies), and the allele $A$ has frequency $0.3$. Neither allele has an advantage. Find the standard deviation of the frequency of $A$ in the next generation, and the probability that $A$ eventually takes over.

**Solution.**

1. The number of copies of $A$ next generation is $\text{Bin}(100, 0.3)$, with mean 30 and variance $100 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 21$.
2. The frequency is this count divided by 100: mean $0.3$ and variance $\frac{21}{10{,}000} = 0.0021$, which is $\frac{p(1 - p)}{2N}$. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.0021} \approx 0.0458$.
3. The frequency is a martingale that ends at 0 or 1, so $P(\text{fixation}) = 0.3$.

**Answer.** The frequency changes by about $\pm 0.046$ per generation, and $A$ takes over with probability $0.3$ (and is lost with probability $0.7$).

**Check.** With $N = 500$ the standard deviation would be $\sqrt{\frac{0.21}{1000}} = 0.0145$, three times smaller: drift is much weaker in a large population. The simulation below gives a fixation fraction of $0.2969$ (the sampling error with 20,000 runs is about $\pm 0.003$).

### Example 4: Will a New Mutation Survive?

**Problem.** A new mutant copy leaves a $\text{Pois}(m)$ number of copies in the next generation. Find the probability that the line dies out for $m = 1.1$. If 10 independent new mutations of this kind appear, what is the chance that at least one survives?

**Solution.**

1. The equation is $q = e^{1.1(q - 1)}$. Iterating from $q = 0$ gives $0.3329$, $0.4801$, $0.5644$, $0.6193$, $0.6579, \dots$, and the values converge to $q \approx 0.8239$.
2. Check: $e^{1.1 \times (0.8239 - 1)} = e^{-0.19371} \approx 0.8239$. It satisfies the equation.
3. So the survival probability is $1 - 0.8239 = 0.1761$.
4. For 10 independent mutations, all die out with probability $0.8239^{10} \approx 0.1441$. So at least one survives with probability $1 - 0.1441 = 0.8559$.

**Answer.** A single mutation with $m = 1.1$ survives with probability only $0.176$, even though it is advantageous on average. With 10 mutations, at least one survives with probability about $0.856$.

**Check.** With $m = 1$ the equation has only the root 1, and the line dies out for sure. With $m = 0.9$ it dies out too. The rough rule $2(m - 1) = 0.2$ is close to $0.176$. The simulation below gives $0.1759$ for the share of lines alive after 100 generations.

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks Hardy-Weinberg and the drift model of Example 3. Part 2 checks the family of Example 1 and the survival of a mutation (Example 4).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Hardy-Weinberg: each child gets one allele from each parent, drawn from the gene pool (A has frequency 0.3).
p, n_children = 0.3, 1_000_000
allele_1 = rng.random(n_children) < p                  # True means allele A
allele_2 = rng.random(n_children) < p
n_A = allele_1.astype(int) + allele_2
for name, k, exact in [("AA", 2, p ** 2), ("Aa", 1, 2 * p * (1 - p)), ("aa", 0, (1 - p) ** 2)]:
    print(f"{name}: simulation {np.mean(n_A == k):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"Frequency of A among the children's alleles: {n_A.mean() / 2:.4f}   (still {p})")

# Genetic drift: 50 individuals = 100 allele copies. Each generation, 100 copies are drawn from the previous one.
n_copies, n_runs = 100, 20_000
count = np.full(n_runs, 30)                             # start with frequency 0.3
generations = np.zeros(n_runs)
running = np.ones(n_runs, dtype=bool)
while running.any():
    count[running] = rng.binomial(n_copies, count[running] / n_copies)
    generations[running] += 1
    running = (count > 0) & (count < n_copies)
print(f"A takes over the population in {np.mean(count == n_copies):.4f} of the runs (exact 0.3000)")
print(f"Mean number of generations until one allele is lost: {generations.mean():.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
AA: simulation 0.0899, exact 0.0900
Aa: simulation 0.4203, exact 0.4200
aa: simulation 0.4898, exact 0.4900
Frequency of A among the children's alleles: 0.3000   (still 0.3)
A takes over the population in 0.2969 of the runs (exact 0.3000)
Mean number of generations until one allele is lost: 119.9
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Mendel: two Aa parents. Each child is aa with probability 1/4, independently. Family of 4 children.
n_families = 1_000_000
n_aa = rng.binomial(4, 0.25, size=n_families)
print(f"P(at least one aa child): simulation {np.mean(n_aa >= 1):.4f}, exact {1 - 0.75 ** 4:.4f}")
print(f"P(exactly one aa child):  simulation {np.mean(n_aa == 1):.4f}, exact {4 * 0.25 * 0.75 ** 3:.4f}")

# A new mutant gene copy: each copy leaves a Poisson(m) number of copies in the next generation.
n_lines, n_generations = 200_000, 100
for m in [0.9, 1.0, 1.1]:
    size = np.ones(n_lines, dtype=np.int64)
    for _ in range(n_generations):
        size = rng.poisson(m * size)                    # a sum of Z Poisson(m) variables is Poisson(m Z)
    q = 0.0
    for _ in range(200_000):
        q = np.exp(m * (q - 1))                         # extinction probability: smallest root of q = G(q)
    print(f"m = {m}: alive after {n_generations} generations {np.mean(size > 0):.4f}, survival forever {1 - q:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(at least one aa child): simulation 0.6836, exact 0.6836
P(exactly one aa child):  simulation 0.4218, exact 0.4219
m = 0.9: alive after 100 generations 0.0000, survival forever 0.0000
m = 1.0: alive after 100 generations 0.0193, survival forever 0.0000
m = 1.1: alive after 100 generations 0.1759, survival forever 0.1761
```

The genotype frequencies are $p^2$, $2pq$ and $q^2$, and the allele frequency stays at $0.3$. In the drift model, $A$ takes over in about 30% of the runs, equal to its starting frequency. The critical case $m = 1$ is interesting: 1.9% of the lines are still alive after 100 generations, but they all die out in the end.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that a dominant allele must be common.** "Dominant" says which allele shows. It says nothing about frequency. The Hardy-Weinberg proportions depend only on $p$.
- **Forgetting the factor 2 in $2pq$.** The heterozygote $Aa$ can come as $A$ then $a$, or $a$ then $A$. Without the 2, the three frequencies do not add up to 1.
- **Expecting a rare recessive allele to disappear.** Most copies of it are in carriers, who show no sign. The share of all copies that sit in carriers is $\frac{2pq}{2pq + 2q^2} = p$, which is 99% when $q = 0.01$.
- **Reading the Hardy-Weinberg law as a law of nature.** It holds under its assumptions (random mating, large population, no selection, no mutation, no migration). Real populations break them, and a departure from the proportions is a clue that one of them fails.
- **Expecting drift to push the frequency toward 50%.** Drift is a fair random walk with no pull to any value. It ends at 0 or 1, and the probability to end at 1 equals the starting frequency.
- **Believing that an advantageous mutation always spreads.** With $m = 1.1$, the new mutant line dies out with probability $0.824$. Most good new mutations are lost at the start.

## Where It Is Used

- **Genetics:** risks for families (carrier couples, Example 1), the share of carriers in a population (Example 2), and a test of whether a population follows the Hardy-Weinberg proportions.
- **Population biology:** drift and the chance that a new variant takes over a population; the spread of an infection that starts from one case, as in [18.6 Branching Processes](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.6-Branching-Processes.md) (an average of more than one new case per case means that an outbreak can grow).
- **AI and machine learning:** genetic algorithms mutate and recombine candidate solutions, and the survival of a good variant follows the same branching and drift arguments.
- **Computer science and statistics:** a random sample drawn with replacement from a set, as in the drift model, is the resampling step of the bootstrap and of particle methods.
- **Everyday life:** why a surname (or a family joke) can disappear from a small town even when each family has on average one child to carry the name on.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A parent $Aa$ and a parent $aa$ have a child. What is the probability that the child is $aa$? What are the possible genotypes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The $Aa$ parent passes on $a$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and the $aa$ parent always passes on $a$. So:

$$P(aa) = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(Aa) = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(AA) = 0$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** The allele $A$ has frequency $p = 0.6$. Find the Hardy-Weinberg frequencies of $AA$, $Aa$ and $aa$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $q = 0.4$:

$$P(AA) = 0.36, \qquad P(Aa) = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.48, \qquad P(aa) = 0.16$$

They add up to $1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A recessive condition affects 1 in 2,500 people (made-up). Assuming Hardy-Weinberg proportions, find the fraction of carriers.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$q^2 = \frac{1}{2500} = 0.0004$, so $q = 0.02$ and $p = 0.98$. The carrier fraction is:

$$2pq = 2 \times 0.98 \times 0.02 = 0.0392$$

About 3.9%, roughly 1 person in 26.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In the drift model with $N = 50$ individuals and present frequency $0.3$, find the mean and the variance of the number of copies of $A$ in the next generation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The number of copies is $\text{Bin}(100, 0.3)$, so:

$$E = 100 \times 0.3 = 30, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = 100 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 21$$

The standard deviation is $\sqrt{21} \approx 4.58$ copies, which is $0.0458$ in frequency.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A population starts with genotype frequencies $x$ ($AA$), $y$ ($Aa$) and $z$ ($aa$) that do not follow Hardy-Weinberg. Show that after one generation of random mating the frequencies are $p^2$, $2pq$ and $q^2$ with $p = x + \frac{y}{2}$, and that they stay there.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The frequency of $A$ among all gene copies is $p = x + \frac{y}{2}$ ($AA$ carries two copies of $A$ and $Aa$ one, out of two copies per person). Under random mating, each child receives two independent alleles from the gene pool, each $A$ with probability $p$. So the child's genotype probabilities are $p^2$, $2pq$ and $q^2$, whatever $x$, $y$, $z$ were. The new allele frequency is

$$p^2 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2pq = p(p + q) = p$$

so the next generation again draws from a pool with frequency $p$, and the proportions stay the same.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the branching process with $\text{Pois}(1.2)$ offspring, started from one copy, for 80 generations, and compare the survival fraction with the smallest root of $q = e^{1.2(q-1)}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
m, n_lines, n_generations = 1.2, 200_000, 80

# Each gene copy leaves a Poisson(1.2) number of copies. Follow 200,000 new mutants.
size = np.ones(n_lines, dtype=np.int64)
for _ in range(n_generations):
    size = rng.poisson(m * size)

q = 0.0
for _ in range(1000):
    q = np.exp(m * (q - 1))                              # smallest root of q = exp(m (q - 1))
print(f"Survival: simulation {np.mean(size > 0):.4f}, exact {1 - q:.4f}   (q = {q:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
Survival: simulation 0.3137, exact 0.3137   (q = 0.6863)
```

Even with 20% more copies per generation on average, two lines out of three die out.

</details>

## Summary

- Each parent passes on one of its two alleles at random, so a cross $Aa \times Aa$ gives $AA$, $Aa$, $aa$ with probabilities $\frac{1}{4}$, $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{4}$, and the number of $aa$ among $n$ children is $\text{Bin}(n, \frac{1}{4})$.
- With random mating and allele frequencies $p$ and $q = 1 - p$, the genotypes follow the Hardy-Weinberg proportions $p^2$, $2pq$, $q^2$ in one generation, and the allele frequency does not change.
- In a finite population the next generation is a binomial sample of the present one. The frequency is a martingale with variance $\frac{p(1-p)}{2N}$ per generation, and an allele with frequency $p_0$ takes over with probability $p_0$.
- A new mutant copy is a branching process: it dies out with the smallest root of $s = G(s)$. With a mean offspring number $m = 1.1$, it dies out with probability $0.824$, though it is advantageous.
- These are simplified models, and their assumptions matter.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(AA) = p^2, \quad P(Aa) = 2pq, \quad P(aa) = q^2 \qquad (q = 1 - p)}
$$
