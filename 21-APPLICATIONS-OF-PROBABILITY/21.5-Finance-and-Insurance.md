> **Big idea:** Insurance and investing are bets managed with expectation and variance: pooling many independent risks makes the average loss steady (so a small loading on the premium is enough), diversifying over assets removes only the part of the risk that is not shared, and prices that move by random percentage steps follow a log-normal law.

---

## Why It Matters

Money problems are probability problems. An insurance company must set a premium: high enough to pay the claims, low enough to sell. A saver must decide how to split money between assets whose returns are random and linked. A price that goes up and down each day needs a model before we can say anything about next year. This file shows the three basic models: the pooling of claims, the variance of a portfolio, and the random-walk model of a price. They use the expected value, the Central Limit Theorem, covariance and the log-normal distribution.

> **Warning:** These are simplified models for learning. They are not advice about money, insurance or investing. Real markets have changing risks, dependence in a crisis and rules that these models leave out. All numbers here are made up.

## Intuition

One house has a 5% chance of a fire this year that costs 1,000 dollars to repair. For the owner this is a nasty gamble. For an insurer with 10,000 such houses, the number of fires is close to $10{,}000 \times 0.05 = 500$, and the total cost is close to 500,000 dollars, with little surprise. The cost per house is about 50 dollars on average, and the insurer can charge 55 dollars. The few extra dollars cover the random part. Pooling turns a big risk for one person into a steady cost for many.

Investing works the opposite way: a lot of risk is removed by spreading money over several assets, but not all. If two assets move up and down together, holding both is almost the same as holding one. If they move in opposite ways, the ups of one cancel the downs of the other. The **covariance** of the two returns decides how much risk is left.

For a price, think of a number that is multiplied each day by a random factor near 1, such as $1.01$ or $0.99$. Over a year, the factors multiply, so the logarithm of the price is a sum of many small random steps. A sum of many random steps is a random walk, and by the Central Limit Theorem it is approximately normal.

## Definition

**Pooling insurance claims.** A policy produces a random claim $X_i \ge 0$ with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ (most policies have claim 0). The claims of $n$ independent policies add up to $S = X_1 + \dots + X_n$ with

$$
E[S] = n \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S) = n \sigma^2, \qquad \frac{\text{sd}(S)}{E[S]} = \frac{\sigma}{\mu \sqrt{n}}
$$

The **pure premium** is $\mu$, and the **loading** $\theta \gt 0$ is the extra fraction, so the charged premium is $(1 + \theta) \mu$ per policy. For large $n$ the Central Limit Theorem gives:

$$
P(\text{the insurer loses money}) = P\big(S \gt (1 + \theta) n \mu\big) \approx 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\theta \mu \sqrt{n}}{\sigma} \right)
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF.

**In words:** the relative spread of the total claim shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, so the chance of a loss falls quickly as the pool grows.

**Ruin.** An insurer has capital $u$ (a whole number of units). Each year the capital goes up by 1 with probability $p$ and down by 1 with probability $q = 1 - p$. For example, the premium of 1 unit comes in, and with probability $q$ a claim of 2 units is paid. With $p \gt q$, the probability that the capital ever reaches 0 (**ruin**) is:

$$
P(\text{ruin} \mid \text{start } u) = \left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^{u}
$$

**In words:** with a positive average gain the chance of ruin is not 0, but it falls geometrically with the starting capital. With $p = q$ (no gain) ruin is certain.

**Portfolio risk.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be the returns of two assets over a year, with variances $\sigma_X^2$, $\sigma_Y^2$. A portfolio holds a fraction $a$ in the first and $b$ in the second. Its variance is:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2 \sigma_X^2 + b^2 \sigma_Y^2 + 2ab \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)
$$

where $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \rho \sigma_X \sigma_Y$ and $\rho$ is the correlation. With $n$ assets that all have variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, held in equal parts $\frac{1}{n}$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\text{portfolio}) = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \left( 1 - \frac{1}{n} \right) \rho \right) \to \rho \thinspace \sigma^2 \quad (n \to \infty)
$$

**In words:** the variance of each asset's own part is averaged away, and the part that all assets share (the covariance) stays.

**The random-walk price.** Let $S_0$ be today's price, and let the daily **log-returns** $R_1, R_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The price after $n$ days is $S_n = S_0 \exp(R_1 + \dots + R_n)$, so

$$
\ln S_n \sim \mathcal{N}\big( \ln S_0 + n \mu, \thinspace n \sigma^2 \big), \qquad \text{median}(S_n) = S_0 e^{n \mu}, \qquad E[S_n] = S_0 e^{n \mu + n \sigma^2 / 2}
$$

**In words:** the log of the price is a random walk with drift $\mu$ and spread $\sigma \sqrt{n}$. The price itself is log-normal ([9.8](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.8-Log-Normal.md)), and its mean is above its median.

## Why It Is True

**The pool.** The claims are independent, so the variances add ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)): $\operatorname{Var}(S) = n \sigma^2$, and the means add by linearity of expectation. The ratio $\frac{\text{sd}(S)}{E[S]} = \frac{\sigma \sqrt{n}}{n \mu} = \frac{\sigma}{\mu \sqrt{n}}$. For the loss probability, standardize $S$: by the Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)), $\frac{S - n \mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}}$ is close to a standard normal $Z$. The insurer loses when $S \gt (1 + \theta) n \mu$, which means $\frac{S - n \mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} \gt \frac{\theta n \mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} = \frac{\theta \mu \sqrt{n}}{\sigma}$. So the probability is about $1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\theta \mu \sqrt{n}}{\sigma} \right)$.

**Ruin.** Let $r_u$ be the probability of ruin from capital $u$. After one year the capital is $u + 1$ with probability $p$ and $u - 1$ with probability $q$, so by the law of total probability

$$
r_u = p \thinspace r_{u+1} + q \thinspace r_{u-1}, \qquad r_0 = 1, \qquad r_u \to 0 \text{ as } u \to \infty
$$

(the last condition holds because the capital drifts upward). Try $r_u = a^u$. Then $a^u = p a^{u+1} + q a^{u-1}$, which gives $p a^2 - a + q = 0$. Its roots are $a = 1$ and $a = \frac{q}{p}$ (check: $p \frac{q^2}{p^2} - \frac{q}{p} + q = \frac{q^2}{p} - \frac{q}{p} + q = \frac{q(q - 1 + p)}{p} = 0$). The root $a = 1$ gives $r_u = 1$, which does not go to 0. So $r_u = \left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^u$. This is the gambler's-ruin result of [18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md) against unlimited money. When $p = q$ the root $a = 1$ is double and the capital has no drift, so ruin is certain.

**The portfolio.** The formula for $\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY)$ is the variance of a sum with weights ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)): $a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) + b^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2ab \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$. For $n$ equal-weight assets there are $n$ variance terms and $n(n - 1)$ ordered covariance terms, each equal to $\rho \sigma^2$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \left( n \sigma^2 + n(n - 1) \rho \sigma^2 \right) = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{n - 1}{n} \rho \right)
$$

**The price.** The log of the price is a sum of independent normal variables, so it is normal with mean $\ln S_0 + n \mu$ and variance $n \sigma^2$ ([13.5](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md)). The exponential of a normal variable $\mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$ is log-normal with median $e^{m}$ and mean $e^{m + s^2/2}$ ([9.8](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.8-Log-Normal.md)). Put $m = \ln S_0 + n \mu$ and $s^2 = n \sigma^2$. In the limit of very many, very small steps, this walk becomes Brownian motion ([18.8](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.8-Brownian-Motion.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Pooling Claims

**Problem.** Each policy has a claim of 1,000 dollars with probability $0.05$, and no claim otherwise. The premium is 55 dollars per policy (a 10% loading on the pure premium). Find the probability that the insurer loses money with $n = 100$ policies and with $n = 10{,}000$ policies.

**Solution.**

1. Per policy: $\mu = 0.05 \times 1000 = 50$. The second moment is $E[X^2] = 0.05 \times 1000^2 = 50{,}000$, so $\sigma^2 = 50{,}000 - 50^2 = 47{,}500$ and $\sigma \approx 217.9$.
2. For $n = 10{,}000$: $E[S] = 500{,}000$ and $\text{sd}(S) = 217.9 \times 100 = 21{,}794$. The premium income is $550{,}000$. The insurer loses when $S \gt 550{,}000$:

$$
z = \frac{550{,}000 - 500{,}000}{21{,}794} \approx 2.294, \qquad P \approx 1 - \Phi(2.294) \approx 0.0109
$$

3. For $n = 100$: the number of claims $N$ is $\text{Bin}(100, 0.05)$ with mean 5, and $S = 1000 N$. The premium income is $5{,}500$, so the insurer loses when $N \ge 6$. Exactly, $P(N \ge 6) = 1 - P(N \le 5) = 0.3840$.

**Answer.** About 38% with 100 policies and about 1.1% with 10,000 policies.

**Check.** The relative spread $\frac{\sigma}{\mu \sqrt{n}}$ is $\frac{217.9}{50 \times 10} = 0.436$ for $n = 100$ and $0.0436$ for $n = 10{,}000$, ten times smaller. The exact binomial value for $n = 10{,}000$ is $0.0111$, in line with the normal approximation. The simulation below gives $0.3840$ and $0.0111$.

### Example 2: The Chance of Ruin

**Problem.** An insurer receives 1 unit of premium each year. With probability $0.45$ it pays a claim of 2 units that year (otherwise nothing). So the capital goes up by 1 with probability $0.55$ and down by 1 with probability $0.45$. Find the probability of ever reaching 0 from the capital 10 and from the capital 20.

**Solution.**

1. The mean claim is $0.45 \times 2 = 0.9$ per year, below the premium of 1, so the mean gain is $0.1$ per year.
2. Here $p = 0.55$, $q = 0.45$, and $\frac{q}{p} = \frac{0.45}{0.55} = \frac{9}{11} \approx 0.8182$.
3. From 10 units: $(0.8182)^{10} \approx 0.1344$.
4. From 20 units: $(0.8182)^{20} \approx 0.0181$.

**Answer.** About 13.4% from 10 units and about 1.8% from 20 units.

**Check.** Doubling the capital squares the ruin probability: $0.1344^2 = 0.0181$. A positive expected profit does not remove the risk of ruin: a run of bad years early on can end the business. Without the gain ($p = q = 0.5$), ruin is certain. The simulation below gives $0.1316$ from 10 units (the sampling error is about $\pm 0.002$).

### Example 3: Two Assets

**Problem.** Two assets each have a yearly return with standard deviation $0.20$, and mean $0.06$ and $0.08$ (made-up numbers). Their correlation is $\rho = 0.3$. Find the mean and the standard deviation of a portfolio that holds half in each. Compare with $\rho = 1$ and $\rho = -1$, and with 10 assets.

**Solution.**

1. The mean is $0.5 \times 0.06 + 0.5 \times 0.08 = 0.07$.
2. With $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y = 0.2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0.3 \times 0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.012$:

$$
\operatorname{Var} = 0.25 \times 0.04 + 0.25 \times 0.04 + 2 \times 0.25 \times 0.012 = 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.006 = 0.026
$$

and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.026} \approx 0.1612$.
3. For $\rho = 1$: $\operatorname{Cov} = 0.04$, $\operatorname{Var} = 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.04$, standard deviation $0.2$ (no gain from diversification). For $\rho = -1$: $\operatorname{Cov} = -0.04$, $\operatorname{Var} = 0.01 + 0.01 - 0.02 = 0$ (the risks cancel completely).
4. For 10 equal assets with $\rho = 0.3$: $\operatorname{Var} = 0.04 \left( \frac{1}{10} + \frac{9}{10} \times 0.3 \right) = 0.04 \times 0.37 = 0.0148$, standard deviation $0.1217$.

**Answer.** Mean $0.07$ and standard deviation $0.161$ for two assets (against $0.2$ for one). With 10 assets the standard deviation is $0.122$, and it can never go below $0.2 \sqrt{0.3} \approx 0.110$.

**Check.** The mean of the portfolio is the average of the means, but the risk is lower than the average risk, as long as $\rho \lt 1$. The floor $0.110$ comes from the part that all assets share. The simulation below gives $0.1613$ and $0.2001$ for $\rho = 0.3$ and $\rho = 1$.

### Example 4: A Random-Walk Price

**Problem.** A price starts at 1. Each day the log-return is $\mathcal{N}(0.0004, 0.01^2)$, independently, for 250 days. Find the probability that the price is below 1 after 250 days, the median price and the mean price.

**Solution.**

1. After 250 days, $\ln S_{250} \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$ with $m = 250 \times 0.0004 = 0.1$ and $s^2 = 250 \times 0.0001 = 0.025$, so $s = \sqrt{0.025} \approx 0.1581$.
2. The price is below 1 when $\ln S_{250} \lt 0$:

$$
P(S_{250} \lt 1) = \Phi\left( \frac{0 - 0.1}{0.1581} \right) = \Phi(-0.632) \approx 0.2635
$$

3. The median is $e^{0.1} \approx 1.1052$.
4. The mean is $e^{0.1 + 0.025/2} = e^{0.1125} \approx 1.1191$.

**Answer.** About a 26% chance that the price ends lower than where it began, a median of $1.105$ and a mean of $1.119$.

**Check.** The mean is above the median, as for every log-normal variable, because a few very high prices pull the mean up. The simulation below gives $0.2619$, $1.1043$ and $1.1192$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks the pooling (Example 1) and the ruin probability (Example 2). Part 2 checks the price walk (Example 4) and the two-asset portfolio (Example 3).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Pooling: each policy has a claim of 1000 dollars with probability 0.05 (mean 50). Premium: 55 dollars.
for n_policies in [100, 10_000]:
    n_claims = rng.binomial(n_policies, 0.05, size=200_000)
    total_claims = 1000 * n_claims
    total_premium = 55 * n_policies
    loss = np.mean(total_claims > total_premium)
    exact = binom.sf(total_premium // 1000, n_policies, 0.05)          # P(claims > premium)
    print(f"{n_policies:>6} policies: P(insurer loses money) simulation {loss:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

# Ruin: capital starts at 10 units. Each year: premium +1, and with probability 0.45 a claim of 2.
n_paths, n_years, start = 40_000, 400, 10
steps = np.where(rng.random((n_paths, n_years)) < 0.55, 1, -1).astype(np.int16)       # net change +1 or -1
capital = start + steps.cumsum(axis=1)
ruined = (capital.min(axis=1) <= 0)
print(f"P(ruin), start {start}: simulation {ruined.mean():.4f}, exact {(0.45 / 0.55) ** start:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
   100 policies: P(insurer loses money) simulation 0.3840, exact 0.3840
 10000 policies: P(insurer loses money) simulation 0.0111, exact 0.0111
P(ruin), start 10: simulation 0.1316, exact 0.1344
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Price model: 250 days, daily log-returns i.i.d. N(0.0004, 0.01^2). Start at price 1.
mu, sigma, n_days, n_paths = 0.0004, 0.01, 250, 40_000
log_returns = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=(n_paths, n_days))
final_price = np.exp(log_returns.sum(axis=1))
m, s = n_days * mu, sigma * np.sqrt(n_days)
print(f"P(price below start): simulation {np.mean(final_price < 1):.4f}, exact {norm.cdf(-m / s):.4f}")
print(f"Median price:  simulation {np.median(final_price):.4f}, exact {np.exp(m):.4f}")
print(f"Mean price:    simulation {final_price.mean():.4f}, exact {np.exp(m + s ** 2 / 2):.4f}")

# Two assets, each with standard deviation 0.20 per year, correlation 0.3. Hold half of each.
for rho in [0.3, 1.0, -1.0]:
    cov = 0.2 ** 2 * np.array([[1, rho], [rho, 1]])
    returns = rng.multivariate_normal([0.06, 0.08], cov, size=500_000)
    portfolio = returns @ np.array([0.5, 0.5])
    exact_sd = np.sqrt(0.25 * 0.04 + 0.25 * 0.04 + 2 * 0.25 * rho * 0.04)
    print(f"rho = {rho:4.1f}: portfolio sd simulation {portfolio.std():.4f}, exact {exact_sd:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(price below start): simulation 0.2619, exact 0.2635
Median price:  simulation 1.1043, exact 1.1052
Mean price:    simulation 1.1192, exact 1.1191
rho =  0.3: portfolio sd simulation 0.1613, exact 0.1612
rho =  1.0: portfolio sd simulation 0.2001, exact 0.2000
rho = -1.0: portfolio sd simulation 0.0000, exact 0.0000
```

The pooling shows the key effect: the same mean claim per policy (50 dollars) gives a 38% chance of a loss with 100 policies and 1.1% with 10,000. The ruin simulation is within its sampling noise of the exact value. The price walk has a mean above its median, and the portfolio standard deviation follows the covariance formula: $0.161$ for $\rho = 0.3$, $0.2$ when the assets move together, and $0$ when they move opposite.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that a positive expected profit means no risk.** The insurer with 10 units of capital and a positive mean gain still has a 13% chance of ruin. A small pool of policies can lose money in 38% of the years.
- **Assuming that claims are independent when they are not.** A flood or a storm hits many policies at once. Then the variance of $S$ is larger than $n \sigma^2$ (the covariance terms are positive), and the pool is much less steady than $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$.
- **Believing that more assets always remove the risk.** Diversification removes only the own part of each asset. The shared part stays, with the floor $\rho \sigma^2$ in the variance. In a crisis, correlations can go up.
- **Mixing mean and median of a price.** The mean price $e^{n \mu + n \sigma^2 / 2}$ is above the typical (median) price $e^{n \mu}$. The average of many people's final wealth is not what a typical person gets.
- **Using the arithmetic mean of percent returns for growth.** A gain of 50% followed by a loss of 50% gives a factor $1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$, a 25% loss. Returns multiply, so we average their logarithms.
- **Treating the model as a forecast or as advice.** Past returns are not a law. The numbers here are made up, and the model is a teaching tool.

## Where It Is Used

- **Insurance:** premiums from the mean and variance of claims, the size of the capital reserve from the ruin probability, and the benefit of a larger pool.
- **Finance:** portfolio variance and diversification; the log-normal price model is the starting point of option pricing, which uses Brownian motion ([18.8](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.8-Brownian-Motion.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** the covariance matrix of returns is the same object as in [11.5](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.5-Random-Vectors-and-Covariance-Matrix.md), and averaging many correlated models (an ensemble) has the same variance floor as averaging assets.
- **Computer science:** the capital of a system under random load, such as the free memory or credit of a server, is a random walk with a risk of reaching zero.
- **Everyday life:** why a group of friends can share risks (a trip, a repair) and why one person cannot; and why a lottery ticket has a negative mean ([21.10](21.10-Games-Gambling-and-Sports.md)).

**2. (Easy)** Two assets have standard deviations $0.10$ each and are uncorrelated. Find the standard deviation of a 50/50 portfolio.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With $\operatorname{Cov} = 0$:

$$\operatorname{Var} = 0.25 \times 0.01 + 0.25 \times 0.01 = 0.005, \qquad \text{sd} = \sqrt{0.005} \approx 0.0707$$

This is $\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{2}}$, the same as the standard error of an average of 2 independent values.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The capital moves up 1 with probability $0.6$ and down 1 with probability $0.4$. Find the probability of ruin from capital 5.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Here $\frac{q}{p} = \frac{0.4}{0.6} = \frac{2}{3}$, so:

$$P(\text{ruin}) = \left( \tfrac{2}{3} \right)^5 = \frac{32}{243} \approx 0.1317$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A yearly log-return is $\mathcal{N}(0.05, 0.2^2)$. Find the probability that the price after one year is below the start, and the median and mean of the price multiplier. ($\Phi(-0.25) \approx 0.4013$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The price is below the start when the log-return is negative:

$$P = \Phi\left( \frac{0 - 0.05}{0.2} \right) = \Phi(-0.25) \approx 0.4013$$

The median multiplier is $e^{0.05} \approx 1.0513$. The mean multiplier is $e^{0.05 + 0.04/2} = e^{0.07} \approx 1.0725$. So even with a positive mean, the price falls in about 40% of the years.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For $n$ assets with equal variance $\sigma^2$ and equal pairwise correlation $\rho$, prove that the equal-weight portfolio has variance $\sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n} \rho \right)$. What happens when $\rho = 0$, and when $n \to \infty$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the variance of a sum, $\operatorname{Var}\left( \sum_i X_i \right) = \sum_i \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + \sum_{i \ne j} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$, with $n$ terms $\sigma^2$ and $n(n - 1)$ terms $\rho \sigma^2$. Dividing by $n^2$ for the weights $\frac{1}{n}$:

$$\operatorname{Var} = \frac{n \sigma^2 + n(n - 1) \rho \sigma^2}{n^2} = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{n - 1}{n} \rho \right)$$

For $\rho = 0$ this is $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, the variance of an average of independent values. For $n \to \infty$ it tends to $\rho \sigma^2$: the shared part cannot be averaged away.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the equal-weight portfolio of 10 assets with $\sigma = 0.2$ and $\rho = 0.3$ (each asset is a shared "market" part plus its own part), and compare its standard deviation with the formula.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_assets, n_years, sigma, rho = 10, 500_000, 0.2, 0.3

# Each asset = common market part + own part, so any two assets have correlation rho.
market = rng.normal(size=(n_years, 1))
own = rng.normal(size=(n_years, n_assets))
returns = sigma * (np.sqrt(rho) * market + np.sqrt(1 - rho) * own)
portfolio = returns.mean(axis=1)                      # equal weights 1/10

exact = sigma * np.sqrt(1 / n_assets + (1 - 1 / n_assets) * rho)
print(f"Portfolio sd: simulation {portfolio.std():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"Limit as n grows: sigma * sqrt(rho) = {sigma * np.sqrt(rho):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Portfolio sd: simulation 0.1217, exact 0.1217
Limit as n grows: sigma * sqrt(rho) = 0.1095
```

</details>

## Summary

- Pooling $n$ independent claims gives a total with mean $n \mu$, standard deviation $\sigma \sqrt{n}$ and relative spread $\frac{\sigma}{\mu \sqrt{n}}$. A loading $\theta$ on the premium makes the loss probability about $1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\theta \mu \sqrt{n}}{\sigma} \right)$.
- With a positive average gain, the probability of ruin from capital $u$ is $\left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^u$. It is smaller for larger capital but never 0.
- Portfolio variance is $a^2 \sigma_X^2 + b^2 \sigma_Y^2 + 2ab \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$. Diversification removes the own risk of the assets and leaves the shared part, with the floor $\rho \sigma^2$.
- A price with independent normal log-returns is log-normal. Its median is $S_0 e^{n \mu}$ and its mean $S_0 e^{n \mu + n \sigma^2 / 2}$ is higher.
- All of this is a simplified model with made-up numbers, not advice.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2 \sigma_X^2 + b^2 \sigma_Y^2 + 2ab \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}
$$
