> **Big idea:** What a positive test result means depends on three numbers, not two: the sensitivity and specificity of the test, and the base rate of the disease in the people tested, and Bayes' theorem (in odds form: posterior odds equal prior odds times the likelihood ratio) combines them, also for repeated tests and for a choice of threshold.

---

## Why It Matters

A test comes back positive. How worried should you be? Most people (and many doctors) answer with the accuracy of the test, such as "the test is 95% right". This answer is often badly wrong, because it forgets how rare the disease is. The same tools are used in screening programs, in spam filters ([21.1.1](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.1-Probabilistic-Classifiers-and-Naive-Bayes.md)) and in security alarms ([21.4](21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md)). Module 04 introduced the idea in [4.11.2 Medical Testing](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.2-Medical-Testing.md). Here we go further: likelihood ratios, a second test, and the choice of a threshold.

> **Warning:** This file uses made-up numbers only, to show the logic. It is not medical advice. Real tests, real diseases and real decisions need a doctor and real data.

## Intuition

Take 10,000 people. Suppose 100 of them (1%) have a disease. A test finds 90 of these 100 (it misses 10). Among the 9,900 healthy people, the test gives a false alarm to 5%, which is 495 people. Now count the positive tests: $90 + 495 = 585$. Only 90 of these 585 people are really sick. So after a positive test, the chance of being sick is $\frac{90}{585}$, about 15%, not 90% and not 95%.

The reason is that false alarms from a large healthy group (495) are more numerous than true alarms from a small sick group (90). If the disease were common (say half of the people tested), the same test would be much more useful. So the same test means different things for different groups of people: a person with symptoms is not the same as a person chosen at random.

## Picture

```text
  10,000 people
     |
     +-- 100 sick (1%) ----------+-- 90 test positive     sensitivity 0.90
     |                           +-- 10 test negative
     |
     +-- 9,900 healthy (99%) ----+-- 495 test positive    false alarm 0.05
                                 +-- 9,405 test negative  specificity 0.95

  positive tests: 90 + 495 = 585, and 90 of them are sick:  90 / 585 = 0.154
  negative tests: 10 + 9,405 = 9,415; 9,405 healthy: 9,405 / 9,415 = 0.9989
```

Counting people instead of probabilities (all numbers are made up). A positive result is far from a sure diagnosis, but a negative result is very reassuring.

## Definition

Let $D$ be the event "the person has the disease", and $+$ and $-$ the test results. The **prevalence** $\pi = P(D)$ is the fraction of the tested group that has the disease (the **base rate**). Two numbers describe the test:

$$
\text{sensitivity} = P(+ \mid D), \qquad \text{specificity} = P(- \mid D^c)
$$

**In words:** the sensitivity is the fraction of sick people that the test finds. The specificity is the fraction of healthy people that the test clears. So $1 - \text{specificity} = P(+ \mid D^c)$ is the false alarm rate.

Two other numbers describe what a result means for the person: the **positive predictive value** (PPV) and the **negative predictive value** (NPV):

$$
\text{PPV} = P(D \mid +) = \frac{\text{sens} \cdot \pi}{\text{sens} \cdot \pi + (1 - \text{spec})(1 - \pi)}, \qquad \text{NPV} = P(D^c \mid -) = \frac{\text{spec} \cdot (1 - \pi)}{\text{spec} \cdot (1 - \pi) + (1 - \text{sens}) \pi}
$$

**In words:** the PPV is the fraction of positive results that are true. The NPV is the fraction of negative results that are true.

In odds form ([4.5](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.5-Odds-Form-of-Bayes.md)), a result multiplies the odds of disease by its **likelihood ratio**:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{P(D \mid +)}{P(D^c \mid +)}}_{\text{odds after}} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{sens}}{1 - \text{spec}}}_{\text{LR}^{+}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}}_{\text{odds before}}, \qquad \text{LR}^{-} = \frac{1 - \text{sens}}{\text{spec}}
$$

**In words:** a positive result multiplies the odds by $\text{LR}^{+}$ (a large number for a good test), and a negative result multiplies them by $\text{LR}^{-}$ (a number below 1).

**A threshold model.** Many tests measure a number (a score) and call the result positive when the score is above a **threshold** $t$. Suppose the score is $\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma^2)$ for healthy people and $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2)$ for sick people. Then:

$$
\text{sens}(t) = P(\text{score} \gt t \mid D), \qquad \text{spec}(t) = P(\text{score} \le t \mid D^c)
$$

**In words:** raising the threshold clears more healthy people (higher specificity) but misses more sick people (lower sensitivity).

## Why It Is True

**The predictive values.** Bayes' theorem ([4.4](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.4-Bayes-Theorem.md)) with the law of total probability for the denominator ([4.3](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.3-Law-of-Total-Probability.md)):

$$
P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D) P(D)}{P(+ \mid D) P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c) P(D^c)}
$$

and $P(+ \mid D) = \text{sens}$, $P(+ \mid D^c) = 1 - \text{spec}$, $P(D) = \pi$. The NPV is the same computation with $-$ in place of $+$.

**The odds form.** Write Bayes' theorem for $D$ and for $D^c$ and divide. The common denominator $P(+)$ cancels:

$$
\frac{P(D \mid +)}{P(D^c \mid +)} = \frac{P(+ \mid D) P(D)}{P(+ \mid D^c) P(D^c)} = \frac{\text{sens}}{1 - \text{spec}} \cdot \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}
$$

For a negative result the same steps give $\text{LR}^{-} = \frac{P(- \mid D)}{P(- \mid D^c)} = \frac{1 - \text{sens}}{\text{spec}}$.

**A second test.** Suppose a second test is done, and the two results are **conditionally independent given the disease status** ([4.9](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.9-Conditional-Independence.md)): once we know whether the person is sick, the first result tells us nothing new about the second. Then the likelihood ratios multiply, and the odds after the first test are the "odds before" for the second (sequential updating, [4.6](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.6-Sequential-Bayesian-Updating.md)):

$$
\text{odds after two results} = \text{LR}_1 \times \text{LR}_2 \times \text{odds before}
$$

Repeating a test on the same sample does not always satisfy this assumption, because the same source of error can affect both results. Then the second result carries less new information than the formula says.

**The base rate effect.** Divide the top and bottom of the PPV by $\text{sens} \cdot \pi$:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{1 - \text{spec}}{\text{sens}} \cdot \dfrac{1 - \pi}{\pi}}
$$

When $\pi$ is small, $\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}$ is large, so the PPV is small. A test is useful only if the positive result changes the odds, which needs $\text{LR}^{+} \gt 1$. This is equivalent to $\text{sens} \gt 1 - \text{spec}$ (problem 5).

**The threshold.** If the scores are normal, $\text{sens}(t) = 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{t - \mu_1}{\sigma} \right)$ and $\text{spec}(t) = \Phi\left( \frac{t - \mu_0}{\sigma} \right)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. As $t$ rises, the first falls and the second rises. No threshold gives both near 1 unless the two distributions are far apart.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Predictive Values

**Problem.** A disease has prevalence $\pi = 0.01$. A test has sensitivity $0.90$ and specificity $0.95$. Find the PPV and the NPV.

**Solution.**

1. The probability of a positive result for a sick person is $0.90$, and for a healthy person is $1 - 0.95 = 0.05$.
2. The probability of a positive test is $P(+) = 0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585$.
3. PPV $= \frac{0.009}{0.0585} \approx 0.1538$.
4. The probability of a negative test is $P(-) = 0.10 \times 0.01 + 0.95 \times 0.99 = 0.001 + 0.9405 = 0.9415$.
5. NPV $= \frac{0.9405}{0.9415} \approx 0.99894$.

**Answer.** PPV is about 15.4% and NPV is about 99.9%.

**Check.** The counts of the Picture give the same: $\frac{90}{585} = 0.1538$ and $\frac{9405}{9415} = 0.99894$. The simulation below gives $0.1525$ and $0.99893$.

### Example 2: The Same Test in Different Groups

**Problem.** Use the test of Example 1 ($\text{sens} = 0.90$, $\text{spec} = 0.95$). Find the PPV when the prevalence is $0.001$, $0.01$, $0.1$ and $0.5$, with the odds form.

**Solution.**

1. The likelihood ratio is $\text{LR}^{+} = \frac{0.90}{0.05} = 18$.
2. For $\pi = 0.001$: odds before $= \frac{0.001}{0.999} \approx 0.001001$. Odds after $= 18 \times 0.001001 = 0.018018$, so $\text{PPV} = \frac{0.018018}{1.018018} \approx 0.0177$.
3. For $\pi = 0.01$: odds before $= \frac{1}{99}$, odds after $= \frac{18}{99} = 0.1818$, so $\text{PPV} = \frac{0.1818}{1.1818} \approx 0.1538$.
4. For $\pi = 0.1$: odds before $= \frac{1}{9}$, odds after $= 2$, so $\text{PPV} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667$.
5. For $\pi = 0.5$: odds before $= 1$, odds after $= 18$, so $\text{PPV} = \frac{18}{19} \approx 0.9474$.

**Answer.** The PPV is 1.8%, 15.4%, 66.7% and 94.7%.

**Check.** With the direct formula for $\pi = 0.1$: $\frac{0.09}{0.09 + 0.05 \times 0.9} = \frac{0.09}{0.135} = 0.6667$. The test did not change, only the group did. This is why a test is used on people with symptoms (a higher prevalence) more than on everybody.

### Example 3: A Second Test

**Problem.** Take the test of Example 1 and prevalence $0.01$. A person tests positive, and then a second, independent run of the test is done. What is the probability of disease after two positives? And after a positive followed by a negative?

**Solution.**

1. Prior odds: $\frac{0.01}{0.99} = 0.010101$. The likelihood ratios are $\text{LR}^{+} = 18$ and $\text{LR}^{-} = \frac{1 - 0.90}{0.95} = 0.10526$.
2. After the first positive: odds $= 18 \times 0.010101 = 0.18182$ (probability $0.1538$).
3. After a second positive: odds $= 18 \times 0.18182 = 3.2727$, and the probability is $\frac{3.2727}{4.2727} \approx 0.7660$.
4. After a positive followed by a negative: odds $= 0.10526 \times 0.18182 = 0.019139$, and the probability is $\frac{0.019139}{1.019139} \approx 0.0188$.

**Answer.** After two positives: about 76.6%. After a positive and then a negative: about 1.9%.

**Check.** A second positive moves the probability from 15% to 77%, so a second test helps a lot. The simulation below gives $0.7644$ for two positives. This holds only if the two results are conditionally independent given the disease status. If the second run repeats the same error of the first, the real probability is lower.

### Example 4: Choosing a Threshold

**Problem.** A test gives a score. Healthy people have a score $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and sick people $\mathcal{N}(2, 1)$ (made-up numbers). The prevalence is $0.02$. For the thresholds $t = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2$, find the sensitivity, the specificity and the PPV.

**Solution.**

1. For each $t$: $\text{sens} = P(N(2, 1) \gt t) = \Phi(2 - t)$ and $\text{spec} = P(N(0, 1) \le t) = \Phi(t)$.
2. For $t = 1$: $\text{sens} = \Phi(1) = 0.8413$ and $\text{spec} = \Phi(1) = 0.8413$. The PPV is $\frac{0.02 \times 0.8413}{0.02 \times 0.8413 + 0.98 \times 0.1587} = \frac{0.016826}{0.016826 + 0.155526} = \frac{0.016826}{0.172352} \approx 0.0976$.
3. The other thresholds are found in the same way:

| Threshold $t$ | Sensitivity | Specificity | PPV |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0.5$ | $0.9332$ | $0.6915$ | $0.0581$ |
| $1.0$ | $0.8413$ | $0.8413$ | $0.0977$ |
| $1.5$ | $0.6915$ | $0.9332$ | $0.1744$ |
| $2.0$ | $0.5000$ | $0.9772$ | $0.3096$ |

**Answer.** A higher threshold lowers the sensitivity (from 93% to 50%) and raises both the specificity and the PPV (from 5.8% to 31%).

**Check.** The PPV improves because fewer healthy people are called positive, and these are the large group. The price is that half of the sick people are missed at $t = 2$. Which threshold is best depends on the cost of a missed case against the cost of a false alarm, and that is a decision for people, not only a calculation. The simulation below at $t = 1$ gives a PPV of $0.0975$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks Examples 1 to 3 on a population of 2 million people. Part 2 checks Example 4 for $t = 1$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people = 2_000_000
prevalence, sens, spec = 0.01, 0.90, 0.95          # made-up numbers

# Each person is sick with probability 0.01. The test is positive with probability sens if sick, 1 - spec if healthy.
is_sick = rng.random(n_people) < prevalence
positive = rng.random(n_people) < np.where(is_sick, sens, 1 - spec)

ppv = is_sick[positive].mean()                      # P(sick | positive)
npv = (~is_sick[~positive]).mean()                  # P(healthy | negative)
exact_ppv = prevalence * sens / (prevalence * sens + (1 - prevalence) * (1 - spec))
exact_npv = (1 - prevalence) * spec / ((1 - prevalence) * spec + prevalence * (1 - sens))
print(f"PPV: simulation {ppv:.4f}, exact {exact_ppv:.4f}")
print(f"NPV: simulation {npv:.5f}, exact {exact_npv:.5f}")

# Two independent tests (given the true state): how sure are we after two positives?
positive_2 = rng.random(n_people) < np.where(is_sick, sens, 1 - spec)
both = positive & positive_2
odds = exact_ppv / (1 - exact_ppv) * (sens / (1 - spec))
print(f"After two positives: simulation {is_sick[both].mean():.4f}, exact {odds / (1 + odds):.4f}")

# The same test at different prevalences.
for p in [0.001, 0.01, 0.10, 0.50]:
    print(f"prevalence {p:5.3f}: PPV = {p * sens / (p * sens + (1 - p) * (1 - spec)):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
PPV: simulation 0.1525, exact 0.1538
NPV: simulation 0.99893, exact 0.99894
After two positives: simulation 0.7644, exact 0.7660
prevalence 0.001: PPV = 0.0177
prevalence 0.010: PPV = 0.1538
prevalence 0.100: PPV = 0.6667
prevalence 0.500: PPV = 0.9474
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_people, prevalence, threshold = 2_000_000, 0.02, 1.0       # made-up numbers

# Test score: N(2, 1) for sick people and N(0, 1) for healthy people. Positive means score > threshold.
is_sick = rng.random(n_people) < prevalence
score = rng.normal(np.where(is_sick, 2.0, 0.0), 1.0)
positive = score > threshold

print(f"Sensitivity: {positive[is_sick].mean():.4f}   (exact 0.8413)")
print(f"Specificity: {(~positive[~is_sick]).mean():.4f}   (exact 0.8413)")
print(f"PPV:         {is_sick[positive].mean():.4f}   (exact 0.0977)")
```

Output:

```text
Sensitivity: 0.8434   (exact 0.8413)
Specificity: 0.8413   (exact 0.8413)
PPV:         0.0975   (exact 0.0977)
```

The PPV of a good test is only 15% at a prevalence of 1%, and it rises to 95% at a prevalence of 50%. The simulated two-positive probability is close to the odds-form value. In Part 2, the simulated values are within sampling noise of the exact ones (only about 40,000 of the 2 million people are sick).

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading the sensitivity as the chance of disease after a positive result.** The sensitivity is $P(+ \mid D)$. The PPV is $P(D \mid +)$. They are different, and swapping them is the error of [4.11.3 Prosecutor's Fallacy](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.3-Prosecutors-Fallacy.md).
- **Forgetting the base rate.** A test with 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity gives a PPV of 15% at a prevalence of 1%. Count people (as in the Picture) to see why.
- **Using the accuracy of a test as one number.** A test that calls everyone healthy is right for 99% of the people when the prevalence is 1%, and finds no one. Sensitivity and specificity must be reported separately.
- **Assuming that a repeated test is independent.** The multiplication of likelihood ratios needs conditional independence given the true state. Repeating a test on the same sample can repeat the same error.
- **Treating the choice of a threshold as a pure calculation.** The best threshold depends on the cost of a missed case and of a false alarm, as well as on the numbers.
- **Using a model as advice.** The numbers here are made up. A real decision needs real data and a professional.

## Where It Is Used

- **Medicine and public health:** screening programs, confirmatory tests after a positive screening test, and the reading of a test result with the patient's symptoms (a higher prior).
- **AI and machine learning:** the same quantities are called recall (sensitivity), precision (PPV) and the false positive rate for a classifier. A classifier threshold moves along the same trade-off. A risk score is useful if it is calibrated ([21.1.6](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)).
- **Computer science and security:** spam filters, fraud alerts and intrusion alarms with very small base rates ([21.4](21.4-Cryptography-and-Security.md)).
- **Statistics:** a hypothesis test is a diagnostic test: the significance level is $1 - \text{spec}$ and the power is the sensitivity ([21.2](21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **Everyday life:** before you trust a rare alert (a smoke detector in a kitchen, a message that says you won a prize), ask how often the alert is true among all alerts.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A test has sensitivity $0.8$ and specificity $0.9$. The prevalence is $0.1$. Find the PPV.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{0.8 \times 0.1}{0.8 \times 0.1 + 0.1 \times 0.9} = \frac{0.08}{0.08 + 0.09} = \frac{0.08}{0.17} \approx 0.4706$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Of 1,000 people, 2% are sick. A test has sensitivity $0.95$ and specificity $0.90$. Count how many sick and healthy people test positive, and find the PPV.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There are 20 sick people and 980 healthy people. Positives among the sick: $20 \times 0.95 = 19$. Positives among the healthy: $980 \times 0.10 = 98$. So:

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{19}{19 + 98} = \frac{19}{117} \approx 0.1624$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** In problem 2, find the NPV.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Negatives among the healthy: $980 \times 0.90 = 882$. Negatives among the sick: $20 \times 0.05 = 1$. So:

$$\text{NPV} = \frac{882}{882 + 1} = \frac{882}{883} \approx 0.99887$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A test has sensitivity $0.90$ and specificity $0.95$, and the prevalence is $0.01$. Find $\text{LR}^{-}$, and the probability of disease after a negative result. Compare with $1 - \text{NPV}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\text{LR}^{-} = \frac{1 - 0.90}{0.95} = 0.10526$. The prior odds are $\frac{0.01}{0.99} = 0.010101$, so the odds after a negative result are $0.10526 \times 0.010101 = 0.0010633$ and the probability is

$$\frac{0.0010633}{1.0010633} \approx 0.001062$$

This agrees with $1 - \text{NPV} = 1 - 0.99894 = 0.00106$. A negative result lowers the risk from 1% to about 0.1%.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $P(D \mid +) \gt P(D)$ exactly when $\text{sens} \gt 1 - \text{spec}$. What does it say about a test with $\text{sens} + \text{spec} = 1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $\pi = P(D)$, $a = \text{sens}$ and $b = 1 - \text{spec}$. Then $\text{PPV} = \frac{\pi a}{\pi a + (1 - \pi) b}$, and PPV $\gt \pi$ means $a \gt \pi a + (1 - \pi) b$ (multiply by the positive denominator and divide by $\pi$). Move $\pi a$ to the left:

$$(1 - \pi) a \gt (1 - \pi) b \iff a \gt b$$

So a positive result raises the probability of disease exactly when $\text{sens} \gt 1 - \text{spec}$. If $\text{sens} + \text{spec} = 1$ then $a = b$, the PPV equals $\pi$, and the test result is independent of the disease: the test is useless.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Example 4 with the threshold $t = 1.5$ and prevalence $0.02$, and compare the PPV with $0.1744$.

<details>

## Summary

- Sensitivity $P(+ \mid D)$ and specificity $P(- \mid D^c)$ describe the test. The predictive values $P(D \mid +)$ and $P(D^c \mid -)$ describe the person who got the result, and they depend on the prevalence.
- For a rare disease, a positive result from a good test is still more likely to be a false alarm: the false alarms of the large healthy group outnumber the true alarms of the small sick group.
- In odds form, a result multiplies the odds of disease by $\text{LR}^{+} = \frac{\text{sens}}{1 - \text{spec}}$ or $\text{LR}^{-} = \frac{1 - \text{sens}}{\text{spec}}$. For results that are conditionally independent, the likelihood ratios multiply, so a second test can change the picture a lot.
- Moving the threshold of a score trades sensitivity for specificity. The right choice depends on costs.
- The numbers here are made up, and this is a model, not advice.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{PPV} = P(D \mid +) = \frac{\text{sens} \cdot \pi}{\text{sens} \cdot \pi + (1 - \text{spec})(1 - \pi)}}
$$
