> **Big idea:** A game with a negative expected profit per bet loses money in the long run for sure, even though any short run can be won: the Law of Large Numbers says that the average profit tends to the expected value, while the Central Limit Theorem says that the chance of being ahead shrinks like the normal tail when the number of bets grows.

---

## Why It Matters

Games of chance are the place where probability began, and they are still the clearest place to see expectation and the Law of Large Numbers at work. A casino can lose on one night and still be sure to win over a year. A sports series can be won by the weaker team more often than fans think. This file explains why: the casino's edge, the swings that hide it in the short run, the chance of being ahead after $n$ bets, and the chance that the better team wins a series. It uses counting (Module 02), expectation (Module 06) and the limit theorems (Module 17).

> **Note:** This file is about understanding, not about beating the house. Nothing here is a system for winning. Every bet in this file has a negative expected value for the player, and that is the point.

## Intuition

A roulette wheel has 37 pockets, numbered 0 to 36. You bet 1 unit (say 1 dollar) on one number. If the ball stops there, you win 35 units and keep your bet. If not, you lose your bet. A fair game would pay 36 to 1, since there are 37 pockets and you have 1 chance in 37, so the payout is a bit smaller than fair: the house keeps $\frac{1}{37}$ of each unit on average, about 2.7 cents per dollar.

On a single spin, you are a big winner (35 units) or a small loser (1 unit). Over 100 spins, you win about 2.7 times on average, which means a loss of about 2.7 units, but in about half of all 100-spin sessions the player is ahead, since 3 wins are enough. Over 10,000 spins, the mean loss is 270 units, but the swings are about 580 units, so one player in three is still ahead. Over a million spins the mean loss is 27,000 units and the swings are about 5,800, so being ahead is a 4.6-standard-deviation event: almost never. The casino plays millions of spins. For the casino, the swings average out.

In sports, a similar story: a team that wins each game with probability $0.6$ is clearly the better team, but over a best-of-7 series it loses about 29% of the time, since 7 games are not many.

## Definition

A bet has a **profit** $X$ (a random variable, negative for a loss). Its **expected profit** is

$$
E[X] = \sum_{x} x \thinspace P(X = x)
$$

and the **house edge** is $-E[X]$ per unit staked.

**In words:** the expected profit is the sum of prize times probability, and the house edge is the average loss of the player per unit bet.

**Roulette with one zero.** There are 37 equally likely pockets. A straight-up bet (one number, payout 35 to 1) and an even-money bet (red, payout 1 to 1; 18 red pockets) have:

$$
E[X_{\text{straight}}] = 35 \cdot \frac{1}{37} - 1 \cdot \frac{36}{37} = -\frac{1}{37}, \qquad E[X_{\text{red}}] = 1 \cdot \frac{18}{37} - 1 \cdot \frac{19}{37} = -\frac{1}{37}
$$

**In words:** both bets lose $\frac{1}{37} \approx 0.027$ of the stake (2.7%) on average, but the straight-up bet has much bigger swings.

**Many bets.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be the profits of independent bets with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$ be the total profit. Then

$$
\frac{S_n}{n} \to \mu \quad \text{(almost surely)}, \qquad S_n \approx \mathcal{N}(n \mu, n \sigma^2) \quad \text{(for large } n \text{)}
$$

so, for $\mu \lt 0$, the probability of being ahead is about

$$
P(S_n \gt 0) \approx 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\sqrt{n} \thinspace \lvert \mu \rvert}{\sigma} \right)
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF.

**In words:** the average profit settles at $\mu$ (the Law of Large Numbers), the total drifts away like $n \mu$ while its swings grow only like $\sqrt{n}$, and so the chance of being ahead goes to 0.

**A best-of series.** A team wins each game with probability $p$, independently. In a series of at most $2k - 1$ games (best of $2k - 1$), the first team with $k$ wins takes the series, so:

$$
P(\text{the team wins the series}) = P\big( \text{Bin}(2k - 1, p) \ge k \big)
$$

**In words:** it is the same as playing all $2k - 1$ games and counting wins, because the extra games after the series is decided do not change the winner.

**Betting to a target.** A player with $i$ units makes 1-unit bets that win with probability $p$ (and lose with probability $q = 1 - p$) until reaching $N$ units or 0. For $p \ne q$, with $r = \frac{q}{p}$:

$$
P(\text{reach } N \text{ before } 0) = \frac{1 - r^{i}}{1 - r^{N}}
$$

## Why It Is True

**The roulette numbers.** The expected profits follow directly from the definition, with probabilities $\frac{1}{37}$ and $\frac{36}{37}$ (counting equally likely pockets, [3.7](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.7-Equally-Likely-Outcomes.md)). For the variance of the straight-up bet, $E[X^2] = 35^2 \cdot \frac{1}{37} + 1 \cdot \frac{36}{37} = \frac{1225 + 36}{37} = \frac{1261}{37} \approx 34.081$, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = 34.081 - \left( \frac{1}{37} \right)^2 \approx 34.080, \qquad \sigma \approx 5.84
$$

For the red bet, $X = \pm 1$, so $E[X^2] = 1$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 1 - \frac{1}{37^2} \approx 0.9993$, $\sigma \approx 1$.

**Many bets.** The Strong Law of Large Numbers ([17.4](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.4-Strong-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)) says that the average of independent identically distributed variables tends to the mean, with probability 1. So in the long run the player loses $\frac{1}{37}$ of each unit bet, for sure. By linearity and independence, $E[S_n] = n \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n \sigma^2$, and the Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) gives the normal shape. Then:

$$
P(S_n \gt 0) = P\left( \frac{S_n - n \mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} \gt \frac{-n \mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}} \right) \approx 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\sqrt{n} \thinspace \lvert \mu \rvert}{\sigma} \right)
$$

The argument of $\Phi$ grows like $\sqrt{n}$, so the probability goes to 0. It needs $n$ of the order of $\left( \frac{\sigma}{\mu} \right)^2$ bets to become small: about $37^2 \approx 1369$ bets for the red bet, and about $37^2 \times 34 \approx 46{,}000$ for the straight-up bet. For small $n$ the normal approximation is poor for the straight-up bet, because the profit is very skewed (the exact value for $n = 100$ is $0.509$, from the binomial).

**The series.** If the series is stopped early, the winner is the first team to reach $k$ wins. If we imagine that all $2k - 1$ games are played, exactly one team has at least $k$ wins, and it is the same team. So the series winner is the team with at least $k$ wins among $2k - 1$ games: binomial ([7.2](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.2-Binomial.md)). For $k = 4$ and $p = 0.6$:

$$
P(\text{win}) = \sum_{j=4}^{7} \binom{7}{j} 0.6^j \thinspace 0.4^{7-j} \approx 0.7102
$$

**Betting to a target.** Let $h_i$ be the probability of reaching $N$ before 0 from $i$ units. By conditioning on the first bet, $h_i = p \thinspace h_{i+1} + q \thinspace h_{i-1}$, with $h_0 = 0$ and $h_N = 1$. The sequences $1$ and $r^i$ with $r = \frac{q}{p}$ both satisfy the recursion (the equation $p r^2 - r + q = 0$ has the roots $1$ and $\frac{q}{p}$). Fit $h_i = A + B r^i$ to the two boundary conditions: $A = -B$ from $h_0 = 0$, and $B(r^N - 1) = 1$ from $h_N = 1$. So $h_i = \frac{r^i - 1}{r^N - 1} = \frac{1 - r^i}{1 - r^N}$ (see [18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md)). For a fair game ($p = q$) the answer is $\frac{i}{N}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The House Edge

**Problem.** A player makes 1,000 straight-up bets of 1 unit on a single-zero wheel. Find the expected total profit and the standard deviation of the total.

**Solution.**

1. The expected profit per bet is $\mu = -\frac{1}{37} \approx -0.0270$. For 1,000 bets, $E[S_{1000}] = 1000 \times \left( -\frac{1}{37} \right) = -27.03$.
2. The variance of one bet is $\sigma^2 \approx 34.08$. The bets are independent, so $\operatorname{Var}(S_{1000}) = 1000 \times 34.08 = 34{,}080$.
3. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{34{,}080} \approx 184.6$.

**Answer.** The expected total is $-27$ units, with a standard deviation of about $185$ units.

**Check.** The swing (185) is almost 7 times larger than the expected loss (27), so in a session of 1,000 bets the sign of the result is nearly a coin toss: the normal approximation gives $P(\text{ahead}) \approx 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{27.03}{184.6} \right) = 1 - \Phi(0.146) \approx 0.442$. The house edge is invisible in the short run.

### Example 2: How Likely Is It to Be Ahead?

**Problem.** A player makes $n$ straight-up bets of 1 unit. Find the probability of being ahead for $n = 100$, $n = 10{,}000$ and $n = 1{,}000{,}000$.

**Solution.**

1. A win pays $+35$ and a loss $-1$, so with $W$ wins the profit is $35 W - (n - W) = 36 W - n$. The player is ahead when $W \gt \frac{n}{36}$, and $W \sim \text{Bin}(n, \frac{1}{37})$.
2. For $n = 100$: $W \gt 2.78$, so $W \ge 3$. The mean number of wins is $2.70$, so $P(W \ge 3) = 1 - P(W \le 2) = 0.5094$ (exact binomial).
3. For $n = 10{,}000$: $W \ge 278$, with mean $270.3$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{10{,}000 \times \frac{1}{37} \times \frac{36}{37}} \approx 16.2$. The normal approximation from the Definition gives $1 - \Phi\left( \frac{100}{37 \times 5.838} \right) = 1 - \Phi(0.463) \approx 0.322$. The exact binomial value is $0.3251$.
4. For $n = 1{,}000{,}000$: $z = \frac{1000}{37 \times 5.838} = 4.63$, and $1 - \Phi(4.63) \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-6}$. The exact binomial value is $2.0 \times 10^{-6}$.

**Answer.** About 51% for 100 bets, 32.5% for 10,000 bets, and about 2 in a million for a million bets.

**Check.** The probability of being ahead falls from more than half to almost zero, while the expected loss per bet is the same $\frac{1}{37}$ in every row. For 100 bets the player is ahead more than half the time because a single lucky win (35 units) covers the typical losses: the profit is skewed. The simulation below gives $0.5098$, $0.3245$ and $0.000001$.

### Example 3: Best-of-7

**Problem.** Team A wins each game against Team B with probability $p = 0.6$, independently. Find the probability that A wins a best-of-7 series. How does this change for $p = 0.55$ and $p = 0.7$?

**Solution.**

1. A wins the series if it has at least 4 wins in 7 games: $P = \sum_{j=4}^{7} \binom{7}{j} 0.6^j 0.4^{7-j}$.
2. The terms: $j = 4$: $35 \times 0.1296 \times 0.064 = 0.29030$; $j = 5$: $21 \times 0.07776 \times 0.16 = 0.26127$; $j = 6$: $7 \times 0.046656 \times 0.4 = 0.13063$; $j = 7$: $0.6^7 = 0.02799$.
3. The sum is $0.29030 + 0.26127 + 0.13063 + 0.02799 = 0.7102$.
4. In the same way, $p = 0.55$ gives $0.6083$ and $p = 0.7$ gives $0.8740$.

**Answer.** $0.710$ for $p = 0.6$, $0.608$ for $p = 0.55$ and $0.874$ for $p = 0.7$.

**Check.** A team that is better on each game 60 to 40 loses the series 29% of the time. Two equally good teams ($p = 0.5$) give $0.5$, as expected. The longer the series, the more likely the better team wins, because the number of games plays the role of $n$ in the Law of Large Numbers.

### Example 4: Playing to a Target

**Problem.** A player has 10 units and bets 1 unit on red each spin (win probability $\frac{18}{37}$). The player will stop at 20 units or at 0. What is the chance of reaching 20? Compare with a fair game.

**Solution.**

1. Here $p = \frac{18}{37}$, $q = \frac{19}{37}$, and $r = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{19}{18} = 1.0556$.
2. $r^{10} = 1.7172$ and $r^{20} = 2.9486$.
3. $P(\text{reach } 20) = \frac{1 - 1.7172}{1 - 2.9486} = \frac{-0.7172}{-1.9486} = 0.3680$.
4. In a fair game the answer is $\frac{10}{20} = 0.5$.

**Answer.** About 36.8% in the roulette game, against 50% in a fair game.

**Check.** The expected profit of the whole plan is $0.368 \times 10 - 0.632 \times 10 = -2.64$ units: a negative expected value, as the house edge says (the expected number of bets is about 98, from the duration formula of [18.2.2](../18-STOCHASTIC-PROCESSES/18.2-Random-Walks/18.2.2-Gamblers-Ruin.md), and $\frac{98}{37} \approx 2.65$). Choosing when to stop does not change the sign of the expectation. The simulation below gives $0.3686$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 follows 10 million straight-up bets of one player (the Law of Large Numbers), and then compares many players. Part 2 simulates best-of-7 series and the betting-to-a-target game.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Roulette, single zero: 37 pockets. A straight-up bet of 1 unit on one number pays 35, otherwise loses 1.
n_spins = 10_000_000
hit = rng.integers(0, 37, size=n_spins) == 0               # our number comes up with probability 1/37
profit = np.where(hit, 35, -1)
running_mean = profit.cumsum() / np.arange(1, n_spins + 1)
for n in [100, 10_000, 1_000_000, 10_000_000]:
    print(f"After {n:>10,} spins: average profit per bet {running_mean[n - 1]:+.4f}   (exact {-1 / 37:+.4f})")
print(f"Variance of one bet: {profit.var():.2f}   (exact 34.08)")

# Many players, 100 spins each: how many are ahead at the end?
wins = rng.binomial(100, 1 / 37, size=1_000_000)         # number of wins in 100 spins
final = 36 * wins - 100                                  # each win pays +35, each loss -1
print(f"100 spins:   P(ahead) = {np.mean(final > 0):.4f}   (exact 0.5094),  mean profit {final.mean():+.2f}")
for n, exact_ahead in [(10_000, 0.325057), (1_000_000, 0.000002)]:
    final = 36 * rng.binomial(n, 1 / 37, size=1_000_000) - n
    print(f"{n:>9,} spins: P(ahead) = {np.mean(final > 0):.6f} (exact {exact_ahead:.6f}), mean profit {final.mean():+.1f}")
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_series = 1_000_000

# Best-of-7 series: the better team wins each game with probability p, independently. First to 4 wins.
for p in [0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.7]:
    wins = rng.binomial(7, p, size=n_series)            # playing all 7 games gives the same winner
    print(f"p = {p:.2f}: better team wins the series {np.mean(wins >= 4):.4f}   (exact {binom.sf(3, 7, p):.4f})")

# Gambler's ruin: bet 1 unit on red each spin (win probability 18/37). Start with 10, stop at 0 or 20.
n_players, p_win = 100_000, 18 / 37
money = np.full(n_players, 10)
active = np.ones(n_players, dtype=bool)
while active.any():
    step = np.where(rng.random(n_players) < p_win, 1, -1)
    money[active] += step[active]
    active = (money > 0) & (money < 20)
r = 19 / 18
print(f"P(reach 20 before 0): simulation {np.mean(money == 20):.4f}, exact {(1 - r ** 10) / (1 - r ** 20):.4f}   (fair game: 0.5)")
```

Output:

p = 0.50: better team wins the series 0.4998   (exact 0.5000)
p = 0.55: better team wins the series 0.6087   (exact 0.6083)
p = 0.60: better team wins the series 0.7099   (exact 0.7102)
p = 0.70: better team wins the series 0.8743   (exact 0.8740)
P(reach 20 before 0): simulation 0.3686, exact 0.3680   (fair game: 0.5)

The running average is wild after 100 spins (this player had no win at all, $-1.0000$) and comes close to $-\frac{1}{37} = -0.0270$ only after millions of bets. The fraction of players who are ahead falls as $n$ grows, and for a million spins nearly nobody is ahead. In the second part, a 60-40 team wins a best-of-7 series with probability $0.71$, and the roulette player reaches the target with probability $0.37$ instead of $0.5$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Believing in a streak.** After 10 reds in a row, the next spin is red with probability $\frac{18}{37}$ again. The spins are independent. The wheel has no memory, and a "due" number is a myth (compare [4.7 Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)).
- **Thinking that a win in the short run disproves the house edge.** The edge is an average over the long run. In 100 bets about half of the players are ahead. The Law of Large Numbers needs a large number of bets.
- **Expecting the Law of Large Numbers to repay losses.** The law says that the average tends to $\mu$, not that the total returns to 0. The total loss $S_n$ keeps growing like $n \mu$ (compare [17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)). A loss is not "made up" by later luck.
- **Believing that a betting system changes the expected value.** Changing the size of the bets or the time to stop does not turn a negative expected profit into a positive one. Example 4 shows that stopping at a target only changes the shape of the result.
- **Using the normal approximation for few bets of a long shot.** For $n = 100$ the normal approximation gives $0.48$, and the exact value is $0.509$. A skewed bet needs many more bets before the bell curve fits.
- **Mixing up the odds and the probability.** "35 to 1" is the payout. The probability of the number is 1 in 37, not 1 in 36 (that is the payout plus one, and the difference is the house edge).

## Where It Is Used

- **Casinos and lotteries:** the pay-out tables of games are set so that the expected profit of the player is negative. The house relies on the Law of Large Numbers across millions of bets.
- **Sports and ratings:** the chance that the better team wins a series, a tournament or a season, and how many games are needed to know which team is better (the standard error of a win rate is $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$, as in [21.2](21.2-Statistics-and-Data-Science.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** game-playing programs choose moves by expected value; a bandit algorithm balances trying a new option and using the best known one ([21.1.5](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md)); and a model that bets on its own probabilities loses money if it is overconfident ([21.1.6](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.6-Uncertainty-and-Calibration.md)).
- **Finance and insurance:** the same mathematics as the insurer who needs many independent policies, with the roles reversed ([21.5](21.5-Finance-and-Insurance.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a raffle ticket, an arcade game or a "spin to win" offer: ask for the prizes and probabilities, and compute the expected value before paying.

The expected prize is $1{,}000{,}000 \times 10^{-7} + 10 \times 0.01 = 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.20$ dollars. So:

$$E[\text{profit}] = 0.20 - 2 = -1.80 \text{ dollars}$$

On average, the player loses 90% of the price. Most of the value of the ticket comes from two very unlikely kinds of events.

**4. (Medium)** A team wins each game with probability $0.6$. Find the probability that it wins a best-of-3 series and a best-of-5 series.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P = \binom{5}{3} 0.6^3 0.4^2 + \binom{5}{4} 0.6^4 0.4 + 0.6^5 = 0.3456 + 0.2592 + 0.07776 = 0.68256$$

The longer the series, the better for the better team: $0.648$, $0.683$, $0.710$ for 3, 5 and 7 games.

**5. (Hard)** For the red bet ($\sigma \approx 1$, $\mu = -\frac{1}{37}$), use the normal approximation to estimate the probability of being ahead after $n = 10{,}000$ bets, and find $n$ for which this probability is $0.16$ (that is, $z = 1$).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The profit $S_n$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(-\frac{n}{37}, n \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{37^2} \approx 0.9993$. Then:

$$P(S_n \gt 0) \approx 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\sqrt{n}}{37 \sigma} \right)$$

For $n = 10{,}000$ the argument is $\frac{100}{37 \times 0.9996} = 2.70$, so $P \approx 1 - \Phi(2.70) \approx 0.0034$. The exact binomial value is $0.0033$. For $z = 1$ we need $\sqrt{n} = 37 \sigma$, so $n = 37^2 \times 0.9993 \approx 1368$.

## Summary

- The expected profit of a bet is the sum of prize times probability. On a single-zero wheel, both a straight-up bet and a red bet have the expected profit $-\frac{1}{37} \approx -0.027$ per unit staked (a house edge of 2.7%).
- By the Law of Large Numbers, the average profit tends to the expected profit. By the Central Limit Theorem, the chance of being ahead after $n$ bets is about $1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\sqrt{n} \lvert \mu \rvert}{\sigma} \right)$, which goes to 0.
- A short run says little: after 100 straight-up bets about half of the players are ahead, since the swings are much larger than the edge.
- The better team wins a series of at most $2k - 1$ games with probability $P(\text{Bin}(2k - 1, p) \ge k)$, so a 60-40 team loses a best-of-7 series 29% of the time.
- Stopping rules and betting systems do not change the sign of the expected profit.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[\text{profit per bet}] = \sum \text{prize} \times \text{probability} = -\frac{1}{37} \quad \text{(single-zero roulette, any bet)}}
$$
