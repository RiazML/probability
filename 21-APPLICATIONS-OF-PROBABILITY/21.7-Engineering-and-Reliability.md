> **Big idea:** The reliability of a machine is the probability that its lifetime lasts past a time $t$; for independent parts, a series system multiplies the reliabilities, a parallel system multiplies the failure probabilities, and the exponential and Weibull laws describe how the risk of failure stays constant or changes with age.

---

## Why It Matters

Engineers must say how long a machine will work: a hard drive, a bridge cable, a server, an aircraft engine. Parts fail at random times, so the lifetime is a random variable. A whole system is built from many parts, and its lifetime comes from the lifetimes of its parts. Two questions follow. How reliable is the system if we know the parts (series and parallel structures, redundancy)? And what law does a single part follow (exponential for "no ageing", Weibull for wear-out and early failures)? This file answers both with the tools of Modules 09 and 13.

## Intuition

A string of old Christmas lights, wired in a line, goes dark when any one bulb fails. If each of 50 bulbs works with probability $0.99$, the string works with probability $0.99^{50} \approx 0.605$. Many good parts in a chain make a poor chain. This is a **series** system.

Now put two bulbs side by side in each place, so that the light stays on if at least one of the two works. With each bulb at $0.99$, a place fails only if both fail: $0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0001$. This is a **parallel** system, or **redundancy**: it fails only when everything fails. Redundancy makes a system far more reliable, but it costs more parts.

For the lifetime of one part, ask whether it ages. A light bulb that is a month old and one that is a day old are different: the old one is nearer to the end. A part that never ages has a constant risk of failing in the next hour, whatever its age. This is the exponential law. Wear-out gives a rising risk, and early manufacturing defects give a falling risk. The Weibull law covers all three.

## Definition

Let $T \ge 0$ be the lifetime of a part. The **reliability** (or survival function) is

$$
R(t) = P(T \gt t)
$$

**In words:** the probability that the part is still working at time $t$. The **hazard rate** $h(t)$ is the risk of failing right after $t$, given that the part works at $t$, per unit of time. For a density $f$, $h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{R(t)}$.

**Two lifetime laws.** In the exponential law, $T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the **rate** (failures per hour) and the mean lifetime is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$. In the Weibull law, $T \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)$, where $k$ is the **shape** and $\lambda$ is a **scale** (a typical lifetime):

$$
R(t) = e^{-\lambda t} \quad \text{(exponential)}, \qquad R(t) = \exp\left( -\left( \frac{t}{\lambda} \right)^k \right) \quad \text{(Weibull)}
$$

The exponential hazard is the constant $\lambda$. The Weibull hazard is $\frac{k}{\lambda} \left( \frac{t}{\lambda} \right)^{k-1}$: it rises for $k \gt 1$ (wear-out), is constant for $k = 1$ and falls for $k \lt 1$ (early failures), see [9.11](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.11-Weibull.md).

**Systems of independent parts.** Let the parts $1, \dots, n$ have independent lifetimes $T_i$ with reliabilities $R_i(t)$. The system lifetime is $\min_i T_i$ for a **series** system (it fails at the first failure) and $\max_i T_i$ for a **parallel** system (it fails at the last failure). Then:

$$
R_{\text{series}}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} R_i(t), \qquad R_{\text{parallel}}(t) = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{n} \big( 1 - R_i(t) \big)
$$

**In words:** a series system works only if all parts work, and a parallel system works unless all parts fail.

## Why It Is True

**Series.** The system works at time $t$ exactly when every part works, which is $\min_i T_i \gt t$. By independence ([4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)), the probability of "all work" is the product $\prod_i P(T_i \gt t)$. This is $P(\min_i T_i \gt t)$ from [13.6](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.6-Max-and-Min.md).

**Parallel.** The system has failed at time $t$ exactly when every part has failed, which is $\max_i T_i \le t$. The probability is $\prod_i P(T_i \le t) = \prod_i (1 - R_i(t))$ by independence, and the reliability is its complement.

**Series of exponential parts.** If $T_i \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$ are independent, then $R_{\text{series}}(t) = \prod_i e^{-\lambda_i t} = e^{-(\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n) t}$. So the series system is exponential with rate $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n$: **failure rates add**, and the mean lifetime is $\frac{1}{\sum_i \lambda_i}$. For any independent parts the same holds for the hazard: the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms, so $h_{\text{series}}(t) = \sum_i h_i(t)$.

**Parallel of two exponential parts.** For $T_1, T_2$ i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$:

$$
R_{\text{parallel}}(t) = 1 - (1 - e^{-\lambda t})^2 = 2 e^{-\lambda t} - e^{-2 \lambda t}
$$

By the tail-sum formula ([6.9](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.9-Tail-Sum-Formula.md)), $E[T] = \int_0^\infty R(t) \thinspace dt$, so the mean lifetime is

$$
\int_0^{\infty} \left( 2 e^{-\lambda t} - e^{-2 \lambda t} \right) dt = \frac{2}{\lambda} - \frac{1}{2 \lambda} = \frac{3}{2 \lambda}
$$

Here is the same answer in words: the first failure comes after a mean time of $\frac{1}{2 \lambda}$ (the minimum of two exponentials has rate $2 \lambda$), and then, by the memoryless property ([9.3](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)), the other part has a fresh $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ life with mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$. The total is $\frac{1}{2 \lambda} + \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{3}{2 \lambda}$. Two parts give only 1.5 times the life, not 2 times.

**Ageing.** The chance that a part that is working at age $s$ works until age $s + t$ is $\frac{R(s + t)}{R(s)}$. For the exponential law this is $\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = R(t)$: an old part is as good as a new one (memoryless). For the Weibull law it is

$$
\frac{R(s + t)}{R(s)} = \exp\left( -\frac{(s + t)^k - s^k}{\lambda^k} \right)
$$

For $k \gt 1$ this is smaller than $R(t)$ (the part wears out), and for $k \lt 1$ it is larger (survivors are the strong ones).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Series or Parallel?

**Problem.** Every part works with probability $r = 0.9$, independently. Find the reliability of: (a) 3 parts in series, (b) 3 parts in parallel, (c) two parallel pairs connected in series, (d) two series pairs connected in parallel (the four-part structures of the Picture).

**Solution.**

1. (a) $0.9^3 = 0.729$.
2. (b) $1 - (1 - 0.9)^3 = 1 - 0.001 = 0.999$.
3. (c) Each parallel pair works with probability $1 - 0.1^2 = 0.99$. The two pairs are in series: $0.99^2 = 0.9801$.
4. (d) Each series pair works with probability $0.9^2 = 0.81$. The two pairs are in parallel: $1 - (1 - 0.81)^2 = 1 - 0.0361 = 0.9639$.

**Answer.** (a) $0.729$, (b) $0.999$, (c) $0.9801$, (d) $0.9639$.

**Check.** Series is worse than a single part ($0.729 \lt 0.9$), and parallel is better ($0.999 \gt 0.9$). For the same 4 parts, (c) beats (d): it is better to put the redundancy at the level of each part (a spare for every position) than at the level of the whole chain. The simulation below gives the same numbers.

### Example 2: Exponential Lifetimes in Series and in Parallel

**Problem.** A part has an exponential lifetime with mean 1,000 hours, so $\lambda = 0.001$ per hour (made-up). Find (a) the mean lifetime and $P(T \gt 100)$ for 3 parts in series, (b) the mean lifetime and $P(T \gt 1000)$ for 2 parts in parallel, compared with one part.

**Solution.**

1. (a) The rates add: $3 \times 0.001 = 0.003$. The mean is $\frac{1}{0.003} = 333.3$ hours. And $P(T \gt 100) = e^{-0.003 \times 100} = e^{-0.3} \approx 0.7408$.
2. (b) The mean is $\frac{3}{2 \lambda} = \frac{3}{2 \times 0.001} = 1500$ hours. And $P(T \gt 1000) = 2 e^{-1} - e^{-2} = 2 \times 0.3679 - 0.1353 = 0.6004$.
3. For one part: $P(T \gt 1000) = e^{-1} \approx 0.3679$.

**Answer.** (a) Mean 333.3 hours and reliability $0.741$ at 100 hours. (b) Mean 1,500 hours and reliability $0.600$ at 1,000 hours, against $0.368$ for one part.

**Check.** The series system lives only a third as long as one part, and the pair in parallel lives 1.5 times as long. The simulation below gives $333.7$, $1499.3$, and $0.7424$, $0.6002$, $0.3682$.

### Example 3: A Wearing-Out Part

**Problem.** A part has a Weibull lifetime with shape $k = 2$ and scale $\lambda = 1000$ hours (made-up). Find the reliability at 500 hours, the mean lifetime, and the probability that a part that has worked for 500 hours works for 500 hours more.

**Solution.**

1. $R(500) = \exp\left( -\left( \frac{500}{1000} \right)^2 \right) = e^{-0.25} \approx 0.7788$.
2. The mean is $\lambda \thinspace \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{1}{k} \right) = 1000 \times \Gamma(1.5) = 1000 \times 0.8862 = 886.2$ hours.
3. Use $\frac{R(1000)}{R(500)}$, with $R(1000) = e^{-1} = 0.3679$:

$$
\frac{0.3679}{0.7788} = e^{-1 + 0.25} = e^{-0.75} \approx 0.4724
$$

**Answer.** $R(500) = 0.779$, mean $886$ hours, and an old part (500 hours) has only a 47.2% chance of working 500 more hours, against 77.9% for a new part.

**Check.** For $k = 2$ the hazard rises with age, so age matters, and replacing an old part before it fails makes sense. For an exponential part, the same question would give the same answer for a new and an old part. The simulation below gives $0.4711$ against the exact $0.4724$.

## Simulate It

Part 1 simulates the lifetimes of Examples 2 and 3. Part 2 simulates the structures of Example 1, and a 2-out-of-3 system.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 500_000
mean_life = 1000.0                                   # hours, made-up; exponential rate 0.001 per hour

# Exponential parts: a series system fails at the MIN, a parallel pair fails at the MAX.
series3 = rng.exponential(mean_life, size=(n, 3)).min(axis=1)
parallel2 = rng.exponential(mean_life, size=(n, 2)).max(axis=1)
single = rng.exponential(mean_life, size=n)
print(f"Series of 3:   mean {series3.mean():6.1f} (exact {mean_life / 3:6.1f})")
print(f"               P(T > 100)  {np.mean(series3 > 100):.4f} (exact {np.exp(-0.3):.4f})")
print(f"Parallel of 2: mean {parallel2.mean():6.1f} (exact {1.5 * mean_life:6.1f})")
print(f"               P(T > 1000) {np.mean(parallel2 > 1000):.4f} (exact {2 * np.exp(-1) - np.exp(-2):.4f})")
print(f"One part:      P(T > 1000) {np.mean(single > 1000):.4f} (exact {np.exp(-1):.4f})")

# Weibull wear-out: shape 2, scale 1000 hours. An old part is worse than a new one.
w = 1000 * rng.weibull(2.0, size=n)
survivors = w[w > 500]
print(f"Weibull: P(T > 500)  {np.mean(w > 500):.4f} (exact {np.exp(-0.25):.4f}), mean {w.mean():.1f} (exact 886.2)")
print(f"Old part, P(T > 1000 | T > 500): {np.mean(survivors > 1000):.4f} (exact {np.exp(-0.75):.4f})")
print(f"New part, P(T > 500):            {np.mean(w > 500):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Series of 3:   mean  333.7 (exact  333.3)
               P(T > 100)  0.7424 (exact 0.7408)
Parallel of 2: mean 1499.3 (exact 1500.0)
               P(T > 1000) 0.6002 (exact 0.6004)
One part:      P(T > 1000) 0.3682 (exact 0.3679)
Weibull: P(T > 500)  0.7787 (exact 0.7788), mean 885.5 (exact 886.2)
Old part, P(T > 1000 | T > 500): 0.4711 (exact 0.4724)
New part, P(T > 500):            0.7787
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, r = 1_000_000, 0.9                                # each part works with probability 0.9
a, b, c, d = (rng.random(n) < r for _ in range(4))

print(f"3 in series:                 {np.mean(a & b & c):.4f}   (exact {r ** 3:.4f})")
print(f"3 in parallel:               {np.mean(a | b | c):.4f}   (exact {1 - (1 - r) ** 3:.4f})")
print(f"Two parallel pairs in series: {np.mean((a | b) & (c | d)):.4f}   (exact {(1 - (1 - r) ** 2) ** 2:.4f})")
print(f"Two series pairs in parallel: {np.mean((a & b) | (c & d)):.4f}   (exact {1 - (1 - r ** 2) ** 2:.4f})")
print(f"2 out of 3 must work:        {np.mean(a.astype(int) + b + c >= 2):.4f}   (exact {3 * r ** 2 * (1 - r) + r ** 3:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
3 in series:                 0.7290   (exact 0.7290)
3 in parallel:               0.9990   (exact 0.9990)
Two parallel pairs in series: 0.9800   (exact 0.9801)
Two series pairs in parallel: 0.9640   (exact 0.9639)

```

The series and parallel rules, the rate addition and the factor $1.5$ for two parallel parts are all confirmed. For the Weibull part with $k = 2$, a survivor of 500 hours is clearly worse than a new part.

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding reliabilities instead of multiplying.** For a series system with $R_1 = R_2 = 0.9$ the answer is $0.81$, not $1.8$ or $0.9$.
- **Assuming that two parts in parallel live twice as long.** The mean of the maximum of two exponentials is $\frac{3}{2 \lambda}$, which is 1.5 times the mean of one part, and only 0.75 of the sum $\frac{2}{\lambda}$.
- **Forgetting that independence is an assumption.** A shared power supply, a storm or a bad batch can fail all parts together. Then a parallel system is much less reliable than $1 - \prod (1 - R_i)$, and a series system a little more.
- **Using the exponential law for a part that wears out.** The exponential says that an old part is as good as a new one. For bearings, tyres and batteries, a Weibull with $k \gt 1$ fits better. The exponential also gives no reason to replace parts early.
- **Mixing the rate and the scale.** For $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ the parameter $\lambda$ is a rate (mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$). For the Weibull law, $\lambda$ is a scale, in the same units as the lifetime.
- **Reading the mean lifetime as a guarantee.** A part with a mean life of 1,000 hours fails before 105 hours with probability $1 - e^{-0.105} \approx 0.1$, if it is exponential. Look at $R(t)$, not only the mean.

## Where It Is Used

- **Engineering:** reliability of machines, bridges and aircraft; redundancy (spare parts, backup power, twin engines); planning of maintenance and replacement ages from the hazard rate.
- **Computer science:** the failure of disks and servers, and the redundancy of data copies. A system of $n$ servers that needs any 2 of 3 to answer is a 2-out-of-3 system (the last line of Part 2 above). Storage with copies uses parallel structures.
- **AI and machine learning:** survival analysis predicts the time until an event (a customer leaves, a machine fails) with the same functions $R(t)$ and $h(t)$.
- **Medicine:** the time until a patient relapses is also a lifetime, with a survival curve $R(t)$ ([21.6](21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)).
- **Everyday life:** a car with a spare tyre has redundancy (a parallel structure). A journey with 3 connections that all must work is a series system.

**1. (Easy)** Four independent parts, each with reliability $0.95$, are connected in series. Find the system reliability.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$R = 0.95^4 \approx 0.8145$$

Four good parts give a system that fails in about 19% of the cases.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Two independent parts, each with reliability $0.8$, are connected in parallel. Find the system reliability.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$R = 1 - (1 - 0.8)^2 = 1 - 0.04 = 0.96$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Two independent parts with exponential lifetimes of rate $0.002$ per hour are connected in parallel. Find the mean lifetime of the system and its reliability at 500 hours.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $\frac{3}{2 \lambda} = \frac{3}{0.004} = 750$ hours. The reliability at $t = 500$, with $\lambda t = 1$:

$$R(500) = 2 e^{-1} - e^{-2} \approx 0.6004$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A series system has independent exponential parts with rates $0.001$, $0.002$ and $0.003$ per hour. Find the mean lifetime of the system, and the probability that the part with rate $0.003$ is the one that fails first.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The rates add: $0.001 + 0.002 + 0.003 = 0.006$, so the mean lifetime is $\frac{1}{0.006} \approx 166.7$ hours. For competing exponential lifetimes, the probability that part $i$ fails first is its share of the total rate:

$$P(\text{part 3 fails first}) = \frac{0.003}{0.006} = 0.5$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For two independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ lifetimes, prove that $E[\max(T_1, T_2)] = \frac{3}{2 \lambda}$ in two ways: with the tail-sum formula and with the memoryless property.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the tail-sum formula, $E[\max] = \int_0^\infty P(\max \gt t) \thinspace dt$ and $P(\max \gt t) = 1 - (1 - e^{-\lambda t})^2 = 2 e^{-\lambda t} - e^{-2 \lambda t}$:

$$E[\max] = \frac{2}{\lambda} - \frac{1}{2 \lambda} = \frac{3}{2 \lambda}$$

With the memoryless property: $\min(T_1, T_2) \sim \text{Exp}(2 \lambda)$ has mean $\frac{1}{2 \lambda}$. After the first failure, the other part has a fresh $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ remaining life, with mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$. Then $\max = \min + \text{(remaining life)}$, so $E[\max] = \frac{1}{2 \lambda} + \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{3}{2 \lambda}$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** A Weibull part has shape $k = 0.5$ and scale 1000 hours (early failures). Simulate $P(T \gt 500)$ for a new part and $P(T \gt 1000 \mid T \gt 500)$ for a part that has already worked 500 hours, and compare with the exact values.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Weibull with shape 0.5 (early failures): a part that has survived 500 hours is BETTER than a new one.
w = 1000 * rng.weibull(0.5, size=n)
survivors = w[w > 500]
print(f"New part,  P(T > 500):            {np.mean(w > 500):.4f}   (exact {np.exp(-0.5 ** 0.5):.4f})")
print(f"Old part,  P(T > 1000 | T > 500): {np.mean(survivors > 1000):.4f}   (exact {np.exp(-1 + 0.5 ** 0.5):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
New part,  P(T > 500):            0.4928   (exact 0.4931)
Old part,  P(T > 1000 | T > 500): 0.7460   (exact 0.7461)
```

With $k \lt 1$ the hazard falls with age. The weak parts fail early, and the survivors are stronger, so an old part (74.6%) is better than a new part (49.3%). This is the reason for "burn-in" tests of new parts.

</details>

## Summary

- Reliability is $R(t) = P(T \gt t)$. The exponential law has a constant hazard and $R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ (no ageing). The Weibull law has $R(t) = \exp(-(t/\lambda)^k)$, with a rising hazard for $k \gt 1$ and a falling one for $k \lt 1$.
- For independent parts, a series system multiplies reliabilities, and the failure rates add for exponential parts. A parallel system multiplies the failure probabilities: $R = 1 - \prod (1 - R_i)$.
- Two exponential parts in parallel live $\frac{3}{2 \lambda}$ on average, not $\frac{2}{\lambda}$.
- The chance of surviving $t$ more units at age $s$ is $\frac{R(s + t)}{R(s)}$. It equals $R(t)$ only for the exponential law.
- All of this assumes independent failures. A common cause breaks the formulas.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{R_{\text{series}} = \prod_{i} R_i, \qquad R_{\text{parallel}} = 1 - \prod_{i} (1 - R_i), \qquad R_{\text{exp}}(t) = e^{-\lambda t}}
$$
