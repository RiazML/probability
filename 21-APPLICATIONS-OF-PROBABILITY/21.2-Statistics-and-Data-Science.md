> **Big idea:** Statistics treats the data as a random sample from a distribution, so every number computed from the data (an average, a rate, a test score) is itself a random variable with a distribution, and the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem say how wide that distribution is.

---

## Why It Matters

Data are always a **sample**, a small part of a larger world. A poll asks 1,000 people about millions of voters. An A/B test shows two versions of a web page to a few thousand visitors. A machine learning model is tested on 500 images, not on all images that will ever exist. In each case we want to learn about the whole world and to say **how sure** we are. Probability answers this. It tells us how much a number computed from a sample would change if we drew a new sample. This file shows the four standard tools: the standard error, the confidence interval, the p-value, and the warning about running many tests.

## Intuition

A teacher wants the average number of hours that students in a big school spend on homework. She asks 100 randomly chosen students and gets an average of 2.1 hours. If she asked another 100 students, she would get a different number, perhaps 2.3. The average of a sample is random. How random? That is the key question.

Two things make the sample average steady. First, it is an average, so the ups and downs of single students cancel. Second, the more students she asks, the steadier it is: the spread of the average shrinks like one over the square root of the sample size. Ask 4 times as many students and the spread is cut in half. The Central Limit Theorem adds that the shape of the average's distribution is a bell, even if homework hours are skewed. With the bell shape, we can say things like "the true average is probably within 0.2 hours of 2.1".

A test of an idea works in a similar way. Suppose a new page seems to bring more clicks. We ask: if the new page were no better, how surprising would a result this good be? If it would be very surprising, we doubt that the page is no better.

## Definition

The **statistical model** is this: the data $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent draws from one distribution with an unknown mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, that is $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F$ ([10.6](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.6-IID.md)). A **statistic** is any number computed from the data, such as the sample mean and the sample variance:

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \qquad s^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2
$$

Because the data are random, a statistic is a random variable, and its distribution is its **sampling distribution**. Its standard deviation is called the **standard error** (SE). The three main facts are:

$$
E[\bar{X}_n] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

**In words:** the sample average is right on average, and its spread shrinks like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$.

A **confidence interval** (CI) is an interval computed from the data. With the Central Limit Theorem, the standard 95% interval for the mean is:

$$
\bar{X}_n \pm 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

where $1.96$ is the number with $P(-1.96 \le Z \le 1.96) = 0.95$ for a standard normal $Z$, and $s$ stands in for the unknown $\sigma$. For a proportion $\hat{p}$ (the fraction of "yes" in $n$ trials), $s^2$ is replaced by $\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})$.

**In words:** the sample average, plus and minus about two standard errors.

A **hypothesis test** asks whether the data are surprising under a **null hypothesis** $H_0$ (for example, "the two pages have the same click rate"). A **test statistic** $T$ measures the difference, and the **p-value** is the probability, if $H_0$ is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one we saw:

$$
p\text{-value} = P(\text{a result at least as extreme as the observed one} \mid H_0)
$$

The test **rejects** $H_0$ when the p-value is below the **significance level** $\alpha$, often $0.05$. The chance of rejecting a true $H_0$ is the **Type I error**.

**In words:** a small p-value says "this data would be rare if nothing were going on". It does not say that $H_0$ is false with probability $1 - p$.

For comparing two click rates from independent groups of sizes $n_A$ and $n_B$, with $\hat{p}$ the pooled rate over both groups, the test statistic is:

$$
z = \frac{\hat{p}_B - \hat{p}_A}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})\left( \frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B} \right)}}
$$

**In words:** the difference of the two rates, measured in standard errors, where the standard error is computed as if both groups share one rate.

## Why It Is True

**The sample mean.** By linearity of expectation ([6.2](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)), and because the variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of the variances ([11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[\bar{X}_n] &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[X_i] = \frac{n \mu}{n} = \mu && \text{(linearity)} \\
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n \sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} && \text{(independence, } \operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For a proportion, $X_i \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ has variance $p(1 - p)$, so $\text{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}$. By Chebyshev's inequality ([16.2](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)) the spread $\frac{\sigma^2}{n} \to 0$ makes $\bar{X}_n$ close to $\mu$ for large $n$: this is the Law of Large Numbers ([17.3](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).

**Why the interval has 95% coverage.** The Central Limit Theorem ([17.5](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.5-Central-Limit-Theorem.md)) says that $\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$ is close to a standard normal for large $n$. Slutsky's theorem ([17.8](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.8-Slutsky-Theorem.md)) says we may replace $\sigma$ by $s$, because $s \to \sigma$. So:

$$
P\left( -1.96 \le \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{s / \sqrt{n}} \le 1.96 \right) \approx 0.95
$$

Rearrange the inequality to isolate $\mu$:

$$
P\left( \bar{X}_n - 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \le \mu \le \bar{X}_n + 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) \approx 0.95
$$

The random things in this statement are the two ends of the interval. The mean $\mu$ is a fixed number. So "95%" describes the **method**: in the long run, 95% of the intervals built this way contain $\mu$. For any one finished interval, $\mu$ is either inside it or not.

**The p-value is uniform when $H_0$ is true.** Let the test statistic $T$ have a continuous CDF $F$ under $H_0$, and say large values of $T$ are extreme. The (one-sided) p-value is $P_{\text{val}} = 1 - F(T)$. By the probability integral transform ([13.1](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.1-CDF-Method.md), [19.4](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md)), $F(T) \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, so $1 - F(T) \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ as well. For any $\alpha$:

$$
P(P_{\text{val}} \le \alpha \mid H_0) = \alpha
$$

**In words:** if there is no real effect, p-values are equally likely to fall anywhere between 0 and 1, and a test at level $\alpha$ rejects a true null exactly $\alpha$ of the time. (For a two-sided test the same holds; for discrete statistics it holds approximately.)

**Many tests.** Run $m$ independent tests of true null hypotheses, each at level $\alpha$. The chance that none rejects is $(1 - \alpha)^m$ (independence, [4.7](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)), so:

$$
P(\text{at least one false alarm}) = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m
$$

The union bound ([3.6](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.6-Union-Bound.md)) shows that using the level $\frac{\alpha}{m}$ for each test (the **Bonferroni correction**) keeps this chance at most $\alpha$, even without independence: $P(\text{at least one}) \le m \cdot \frac{\alpha}{m} = \alpha$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Poll

**Problem.** In a poll of $n = 1000$ voters, 540 prefer candidate A. Find a 95% confidence interval for the share $p$ of all voters who prefer A.

**Solution.**

1. The sample proportion is $\hat{p} = \frac{540}{1000} = 0.54$.
2. Its standard error, with $\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) = 0.54 \times 0.46 = 0.2484$:

$$
\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.2484}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.0002484} \approx 0.01576
$$

3. The margin is $1.96 \times 0.01576 \approx 0.0309$.
4. The interval is $0.54 \pm 0.0309$.

**Answer.** About $(0.509, 0.571)$, so 54% with a margin of error of about 3 percentage points.

**Check.** The whole interval is above 0.5, so the poll supports "A is ahead". But the margin shows the result could be 51% or 57%. The simulation in problem 6 shows when this formula works badly (rare events).

### Example 2: An A/B Test

**Problem.** Page A gets 200 clicks from 4,000 visitors. Page B gets 250 clicks from 4,000 visitors. Is the difference statistically significant at the 5% level?

**Solution.**

1. The rates are $\hat{p}_A = \frac{200}{4000} = 0.05$ and $\hat{p}_B = \frac{250}{4000} = 0.0625$. The difference is $0.0125$.
2. Under $H_0$ (same rate), pool the data: $\hat{p} = \frac{200 + 250}{8000} = 0.05625$.
3. The standard error:

$$
\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.05625 \times 0.94375 \times \left( \frac{1}{4000} + \frac{1}{4000} \right)} = \sqrt{0.053086 \times 0.0005} = \sqrt{0.00002654} \approx 0.005152
$$

4. The test statistic is $z = \frac{0.0125}{0.005152} \approx 2.426$.
5. The two-sided p-value is the probability that a standard normal is beyond $\pm 2.426$:

$$
p\text{-value} = 2 \times P(Z \gt 2.426) \approx 2 \times 0.00763 = 0.0153
$$

**Answer.** The p-value is about 0.015, below 0.05, so the difference is significant at the 5% level.

**Check.** If the two pages were really equal, we would see a gap this large in only about 1.5% of experiments. The simulation below, with no real difference, gives $P(\lvert z \rvert \ge 2.426) \approx 0.015$. This is a p-value, not the probability that B is better.

### Example 3: Twenty Tests

**Problem.** A data scientist tests 20 unrelated features for a link with the outcome, each at level 0.05. None of them truly has a link. What is the chance that at least one test is "significant"? What happens with the Bonferroni level?

**Solution.**

1. The chance that one test is not significant is $0.95$. For 20 independent tests, the chance that none is significant is $0.95^{20} \approx 0.3585$.
2. So $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - 0.3585 = 0.6415$.
3. With the Bonferroni level $\frac{0.05}{20} = 0.0025$: the chance that none is significant is $(1 - 0.0025)^{20} \approx 0.9512$, so $P(\text{at least one}) \approx 0.0488$.

**Answer.** About 64% without a correction, and about 4.9% (below 5%) with the Bonferroni correction.

**Check.** Using the union bound, $20 \times 0.0025 = 0.05$, and the true value $0.0488$ is just below it. The simulation agrees. The warning is practical: if you test enough things, something will look significant by chance.

## Simulate It

Part 1 checks that dividing by $n - 1$ gives the right variance, and measures the coverage of the 95% interval for skewed data (Exponential with mean 1) at two sample sizes. Part 2 simulates the A/B test of Example 2 with no real difference, to see the p-values, and then the 20-test study of Example 3.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_reps = 100_000

# Sample variance: dividing by n - 1 is unbiased, dividing by n is too small.
data = rng.normal(0, 2, size=(n_reps, 5))               # true variance 4, samples of size 5
print(f"Mean of s^2 (divide by n-1): {data.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean():.3f}   exact 4.000")
print(f"Mean of s^2 (divide by n):   {data.var(axis=1, ddof=0).mean():.3f}   exact 3.200")

# Coverage of the 95% interval xbar +- 1.96 s / sqrt(n) for skewed data: Exp(1), true mean 1.
for n in [10, 100]:
    x = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_reps, n))
    xbar, s = x.mean(axis=1), x.std(axis=1, ddof=1)
    covered = np.abs(xbar - 1.0) <= 1.96 * s / np.sqrt(n)    # does the interval contain the mean?
    print(f"n = {n:>3}: the interval contains the true mean in {covered.mean():.4f} of the samples")
```

Output:

```text
Mean of s^2 (divide by n-1): 4.004   exact 4.000
Mean of s^2 (divide by n):   3.203   exact 3.200
n =  10: the interval contains the true mean in 0.8697 of the samples
n = 100: the interval contains the true mean in 0.9388 of the samples
```

Part 2:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# A/B test when there is NO real difference: both pages have a click rate of 0.05625.
n_tests, n_per_group, rate = 200_000, 4_000, 0.05625
clicks_a = rng.binomial(n_per_group, rate, size=n_tests)
clicks_b = rng.binomial(n_per_group, rate, size=n_tests)
pooled = (clicks_a + clicks_b) / (2 * n_per_group)
se = np.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * 2 / n_per_group)
z = (clicks_b - clicks_a) / n_per_group / se
p_values = 2 * (1 - norm.cdf(np.abs(z)))
print(f"P(p-value < 0.05) when nothing is going on: {np.mean(p_values < 0.05):.4f}")
print(f"P(p-value < 0.50): {np.mean(p_values < 0.50):.4f}")
print(f"P(|z| >= 2.426): {np.mean(np.abs(z) >= 2.426):.4f}")

# 20 tests of true nulls in one study: how often is at least one "significant"?
p_study = 2 * (1 - norm.cdf(np.abs(rng.normal(size=(100_000, 20)))))
print(f"At least one p < 0.05 in 20 tests: {np.mean((p_study < 0.05).any(axis=1)):.4f}, exact {1 - 0.95**20:.4f}")
print(f"With the cut-off 0.05 / 20:        {np.mean((p_study < 0.0025).any(axis=1)):.4f}, exact {1 - (1 - 0.0025)**20:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(p-value < 0.05) when nothing is going on: 0.0494
P(p-value < 0.50): 0.5008
P(|z| >= 2.426): 0.0148
At least one p < 0.05 in 20 tests: 0.6407, exact 0.6415
With the cut-off 0.05 / 20:        0.0486, exact 0.0488
```

Four facts are visible. Dividing by $n - 1$ is right. With $n = 100$ the interval covers about 94% (close to 95%), but with $n = 10$ and skewed data it covers only 87%, so the Central Limit Theorem needs a large enough $n$. When nothing is going on, p-values are uniform ($P(p \lt 0.05) \approx 0.05$ and $P(p \lt 0.5) \approx 0.5$; the small gaps come from the counts being whole numbers). And running 20 tests almost always produces a false alarm.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading a confidence interval as "95% chance that $\mu$ is inside".** The interval is random and $\mu$ is fixed. The 95% is the success rate of the method (see the Picture). A statement about the probability of $\mu$ needs a Bayesian model (see [10.7](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.7-Bayes-for-Random-Variables.md)).
- **Reading a p-value as the probability that $H_0$ is true.** The p-value is $P(\text{data} \mid H_0)$, and the question we care about is $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$. Swapping them is the same error as in [4.11.3 Prosecutor's Fallacy](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.11-Classic-Puzzles/4.11.3-Prosecutors-Fallacy.md). The answer needs a prior.
- **Saying "not significant" means "no effect".** A small sample has a wide interval. It may simply be unable to see a real effect.
- **Testing many things and reporting the winner.** With 20 tests you expect one false alarm (Example 3). Report how many tests you ran, or correct the level.
- **Using the normal interval for small samples or rare events.** For skewed data with $n = 10$ the coverage was 87%. For a rare event the same happens (problem 6). Use more data or a better method.
- **Dividing by $n$ for the variance of a sample.** It underestimates $\sigma^2$ by the factor $\frac{n-1}{n}$ (problem 5).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a confidence interval for a model's accuracy on a test set (problem 3); comparing two models with a test; A/B tests of a new recommendation system; reporting "mean plus or minus standard error" over several random seeds.
- **Product and web:** A/B testing, as in Example 2. [21.1.5](21.1-AI-and-Machine-Learning/21.1.5-Reinforcement-Learning.md) shows a bandit rule that shifts traffic to the better page while the test is running.
- **Polling and surveys:** the margin of error of Example 1.
- **Medicine:** clinical trials compare treatment and control groups with the same z-test ([21.6](21.6-Medicine-and-Diagnostic-Testing.md)).
- **Quality control and engineering:** checking whether a batch meets a standard ([21.7](21.7-Engineering-and-Reliability.md)).
- **Everyday life:** when someone says "this works, I tried it on 5 people", ask how much a different 5 people would change the answer.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A poll of $n = 400$ people finds $\hat{p} = 0.5$. What is the standard error and the 95% margin of error?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{400}} = \sqrt{0.000625} = 0.025, \qquad \text{margin} = 1.96 \times 0.025 = 0.049$$

So the poll gives 50% plus or minus about 4.9 percentage points.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** How many people must be polled so that the 95% margin of error is at most 0.03 in the worst case ($p = 0.5$)?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $1.96 \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{n}} \le 0.03$, which is $n \ge \left( \frac{1.96 \times 0.5}{0.03} \right)^2 \approx 1067.1$. Round up:

$$n = 1068$$

This is why many polls ask about 1,000 people.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A model gets 460 of 500 test images right. Find a 95% confidence interval for its true accuracy on this kind of image.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The sample accuracy is $\hat{p} = \frac{460}{500} = 0.92$. Then:

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.92 \times 0.08}{500}} = \sqrt{0.0001472} \approx 0.01213, \qquad \text{margin} = 1.96 \times 0.01213 \approx 0.0238$$

The interval is $0.92 \pm 0.0238$, about $(0.896, 0.944)$. A second model with 90% accuracy on the same 500 images would have an interval that overlaps this one.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Page A gets 30 clicks out of 500 visitors, and page B gets 45 out of 500. Is the difference significant at the 5% level? (Use the pooled z-test. $P(Z \gt 1.80) \approx 0.0359$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The rates are $0.06$ and $0.09$, difference $0.03$. The pooled rate is $\frac{75}{1000} = 0.075$. Then:

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.075 \times 0.925 \times \tfrac{2}{500}} = \sqrt{0.0002775} \approx 0.01666, \qquad z = \frac{0.03}{0.01666} \approx 1.80$$

The two-sided p-value is $2 \times 0.0359 \approx 0.072$, which is above 0.05. The difference is **not** significant at the 5% level. With so few visitors, the test cannot tell a real 3-point gain from chance.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $E[s^2] = \sigma^2$ when $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_i (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

First, $\sum_i (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2 = \sum_i X_i^2 - n \bar{X}_n^2$ (expand the square and use $\sum_i X_i = n \bar{X}_n$). Take expectations. Since $E[X_i^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2$ and $E[\bar{X}_n^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) + \mu^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2$:

$$E\left[ \sum_i (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2 \right] = n (\sigma^2 + \mu^2) - n \left( \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2 \right) = n \sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n - 1) \sigma^2$$

Divide by $n - 1$ to get $E[s^2] = \sigma^2$. Dividing by $n$ instead gives $\frac{n-1}{n} \sigma^2$, which is too small (the simulation shows 3.2 instead of 4 for $n = 5$).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** A rare event has $p = 0.02$. A sample of $n = 100$ uses the interval $\hat{p} \pm 1.96 \sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/n}$. Simulate how often the interval contains $p$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, p, n_reps = 100, 0.02, 200_000

# Poll-style interval p_hat +- 1.96 sqrt(p_hat (1 - p_hat) / n) for a rare event.
p_hat = rng.binomial(n, p, size=n_reps) / n
margin = 1.96 * np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
covered = np.abs(p_hat - p) <= margin
print(f"Coverage when p = 0.02 and n = 100: {covered.mean():.4f}   (target 0.95)")
```

Output:

```text
Coverage when p = 0.02 and n = 100: 0.8669   (target 0.95)
```

The coverage is only about 87%. With an expected count of $np = 2$ events, the normal approximation is poor (and when no event is seen, the interval has zero width). The CLT needs larger counts.

</details>

## Summary

- Data are a random sample, so every statistic is a random variable. The sample mean has mean $\mu$ and standard error $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$.
- A 95% confidence interval is $\bar{X}_n \pm 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$. The 95% describes the method over repeated samples, not one interval.
- A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if $H_0$ is true. It is uniform on $(0, 1)$ when $H_0$ is true, so a test at level $\alpha$ makes a false alarm $\alpha$ of the time.
- With $m$ tests the chance of at least one false alarm is $1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$. The Bonferroni level $\frac{\alpha}{m}$ keeps it below $\alpha$.
- These tools rely on the CLT, so they need enough data. Rare events and small, skewed samples need more care.

The one formula to remember (the interval that covers $\mu$ in about 95 percent of samples):

$$
\boxed{\bar{X}_n \pm 1.96 \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}}
$$
