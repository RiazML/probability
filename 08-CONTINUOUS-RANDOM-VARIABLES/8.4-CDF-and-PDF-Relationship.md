# 8.4 CDF and PDF Relationship

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.4 CDF and PDF Relationship

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [8.3 Densities Can Exceed One](8.3-Densities-Can-Exceed-One.md)

> **Big idea:** The CDF is the area under the density to the left of x, and the density is the slope of the CDF.

---

## Why It Matters

The density and the CDF are two views of the same distribution. Some questions are
easier with one, some with the other. "What is the chance $X$ is below 3?" is one
CDF lookup. "Where are the values most crowded?" is a density question. Often the
easiest way to **find** a density is to find the CDF first and then take its slope.
That trick is used again in
[13.1 CDF Method](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.1-CDF-Method.md) and in
[8.8 Change of Variables](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md).

## Intuition

Think of rain falling into a bucket during a storm. The **rate** of rain at each
moment (mm per hour) is like the density. The **total** in the bucket so far is like
the CDF. The total can only go up. It goes up fast when the rain is heavy and slowly
when the rain is light. At the end of the storm, the bucket holds everything.

The same is true for a random variable. Walk along the $x$-axis from left to right and
collect the area under the density as you go. The amount collected so far, at the
point $x$, is $F(x) = P(X \le x)$. Where the density is tall, $F$ climbs steeply. Where
the density is 0, $F$ stays flat. At the far right, you have collected all the area,
which is 1.

## Picture

```text
  f(x)
    |        .--.
    |      .'####'.                    density f: the shaded area
    |     /#######|'.                  left of x0 is F(x0)
    |    /########|  '.
    |   /#########|    '-.
    |  /##########|       '-.__
    +-------------+--------------''---> x
                  x0

  F(x)
  1 +- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
    |                       __..--''
    |                  _.-''
    |              _.-'                CDF F: its height at x0 is
    |            .*  <- F(x0)          that area, and its slope at
    |          .' :                    x0 is f(x0)
    |       .-'   :
    |  __..-'     :
  0 +-------------+------------------> x
                  x0
```

The height of the CDF at $x_0$ is the shaded area under the density, and the CDF
rises fastest where the density is tallest.

## Definition

### A One-Minute Reminder About Derivatives

The **derivative** $F'(x)$ is the **slope** of the curve $F$ at the point $x$: how fast
$F$ grows when $x$ moves a little to the right. Two rules cover this module: the
derivative of $x^n$ is $n x^{n-1}$, and the derivative of $e^{-\lambda x}$ is
$-\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$. The **fundamental theorem of calculus** links slopes and areas:
if $F(x)$ is the area under $f$ up to $x$, then the slope of $F$ is $f$. Integrals and
derivatives undo each other.

### The Two Directions

The **cumulative distribution function** (**CDF**) of $X$, first met in
[5.4 Cumulative Distribution Function](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.4-Cumulative-Distribution-Function.md),
is $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$. For a continuous $X$ with density $f_X$:

$$
F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t) \thinspace dt
$$

where $t$ is just the name of the variable we integrate over (we use $t$ because $x$ is
already the right end).

**In words:** the CDF at $x$ is all the area under the density to the left of $x$.

Going the other way, at every point where $f_X$ is continuous:

$$
f_X(x) = F_X'(x) = \frac{d}{dx} F_X(x)
$$

**In words:** the density is the slope of the CDF.

The CDF gives every interval probability by one subtraction:

$$
P(a \lt X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a)
$$

**In words:** the area between $a$ and $b$ is the area left of $b$ minus the area left
of $a$. For a continuous $X$, the endpoints do not matter
([8.2 Why Point Probabilities are Zero](8.2-Why-Point-Probabilities-are-Zero.md)).

A continuous CDF has four properties:

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $0 \le F_X(x) \le 1$ | it is a probability |
| non-decreasing | collecting more area never lowers the total |
| $F_X(x) \to 0$ as $x \to -\infty$ and $F_X(x) \to 1$ as $x \to \infty$ | nothing collected at the far left, everything at the far right |
| continuous, with no jumps | a jump would mean $P(X = x) \gt 0$ |

The last row is the big difference from a discrete CDF, which is a staircase of jumps.

## Why It Is True

**The CDF is an integral.** The event $X \le x$ is the interval from $-\infty$ to $x$, and
the probability of an interval is the area over it (definition of the density).

**The subtraction rule.** The events $\lbrace X \le a \rbrace$ and
$\lbrace a \lt X \le b \rbrace$ do not overlap, and together they make
$\lbrace X \le b \rbrace$. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_X(b) &= P(X \le a) + P(a \lt X \le b) && \text{(addition rule)} \\
&= F_X(a) + P(a \lt X \le b) && \text{(definition of the CDF)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtract $F_X(a)$ from both sides.

**The slope is the density.** Move a small step $h \gt 0$ to the right of $x$. The CDF
grows by the area of one thin strip:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{F_X(x + h) - F_X(x)}{h} &= \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f_X(t) \thinspace dt && \text{(subtraction rule)} \\
&\approx \frac{1}{h} \cdot f_X(x) \cdot h && \text{(thin strip: height times width)} \\
&= f_X(x) && \text{(cancel } h \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

As $h \to 0$ the approximation becomes exact, and the left side becomes the slope $F_X'(x)$.

**No jumps.** The same strip shows $F_X(x + h) - F_X(x) = P(x \lt X \le x + h)$, which
shrinks to 0 as $h \to 0$. So $F_X$ never jumps.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: From Density to CDF

**Problem.** $X$ has density $f(x) = 3x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ and 0 elsewhere (from
[8.1](8.1-Probability-Density-Function.md)). Find its CDF for every $x$, then find
$P(0.5 \lt X \le 0.8)$.

**Solution.**

1. For $x \lt 0$: no area yet, so $F(x) = 0$.
2. For $0 \le x \le 1$: collect the area from 0 to $x$:

$$
F(x) = \int_0^x 3t^2 \thinspace dt = \big[ t^3 \big]_0^x = x^3
$$

3. For $x \gt 1$: all the area is collected, so $F(x) = 1$. Together:

$$
F(x) =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } x \lt 0 \\
x^3 & \text{if } 0 \le x \le 1 \\
1 & \text{if } x \gt 1
\end{cases}
$$

4. Subtract:

$$
P(0.5 \lt X \le 0.8) = F(0.8) - F(0.5) = 0.512 - 0.125 = 0.387
$$

**Answer.** $F(x) = x^3$ on $[0, 1]$ (0 to the left, 1 to the right), and the
probability is $0.387$.

**Check.** The slope of $x^3$ is $3x^2$, which is the density we started with. The
pieces also join up: $F(0) = 0$ and $F(1) = 1$, so there are no jumps.

### Example 2: From CDF to Density

**Problem.** The lifetime $X$ of a hard drive, in years, has CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-x/4}$
for $x \ge 0$ (and 0 for $x \lt 0$). Find the density. Find the probability that a drive
fails between year 2 and year 5, and the probability that it lasts more than 4 years.

**Solution.**

1. Take the slope. The derivative of $e^{-x/4}$ is $-\frac{1}{4} e^{-x/4}$, so:

$$
f(x) = F'(x) = 0 - \left( -\tfrac{1}{4} e^{-x/4} \right) = \tfrac{1}{4} e^{-x/4} \quad \text{for } x \ge 0
$$

2. Between year 2 and year 5:

$$
P(2 \lt X \le 5) = F(5) - F(2) = (1 - e^{-1.25}) - (1 - e^{-0.5}) = e^{-0.5} - e^{-1.25} \approx 0.6065 - 0.2865 = 0.3200
$$

3. More than 4 years:

$$
P(X \gt 4) = 1 - F(4) = 1 - (1 - e^{-1}) = e^{-1} \approx 0.3679
$$

**Answer.** $f(x) = \frac{1}{4} e^{-x/4}$ for $x \ge 0$. About 32% of drives fail between
year 2 and year 5, and about 36.8% last more than 4 years.

**Check.** $f(x) \gt 0$ and $F$ climbs from $F(0) = 0$ toward 1, as a CDF must. This is the
exponential distribution with rate $\frac{1}{4}$ per year
([9.3 Exponential](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)).

### Example 3: Waiting for the Slower of Two Tasks

**Problem.** A program starts two tasks in parallel. Each takes a time spread evenly
on $[0, 1]$ second, independently of the other. The program finishes when **both**
tasks finish, at time $T = \max(\text{task 1}, \text{task 2})$. Find the CDF and the
density of $T$, and $P(T \gt 0.9)$.

**Solution.**

1. The density of $T$ is not obvious, so start with the CDF. $T \le t$ means **both**
   tasks are done by time $t$. For $0 \le t \le 1$, each task is done by $t$ with
   probability $t$. The tasks are independent
   ([4.7 Independence](../04-CONDITIONAL-PROBABILITY-AND-BAYES/4.7-Independence.md)), so:

$$
F_T(t) = P(\text{task 1} \le t) \cdot P(\text{task 2} \le t) = t \cdot t = t^2
$$

2. Take the slope: $f_T(t) = 2t$ for $0 \le t \le 1$.
3. The slow case:

$$
P(T \gt 0.9) = 1 - F_T(0.9) = 1 - 0.81 = 0.19
$$

**Answer.** $F_T(t) = t^2$ and $f_T(t) = 2t$ on $[0, 1]$, and $P(T \gt 0.9) = 0.19$.

**Check.** One task alone is slower than 0.9 seconds only 10% of the time, but with two
tasks the chance is almost doubled, to 19%. Waiting for the slowest of many tasks is
a real problem in parallel computing.
([13.6 Max and Min](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.6-Max-and-Min.md) studies it in general.)

## Simulate It

We simulate Example 3: compare the empirical CDF of $T$ with $t^2$, and estimate the
slope of the CDF at $t = 0.7$, which should be $f_T(0.7) = 1.4$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 4_000_000

# Two independent task times, uniform on [0, 1]; T is the slower one.
t = np.maximum(rng.random(n_trials), rng.random(n_trials))

for x in [0.3, 0.5, 0.9]:
    print(f"F({x}): sim {np.mean(t <= x):.4f}, exact {x**2:.4f}")

# Slope of the CDF near 0.7 estimates the density there.
h = 0.02
slope = (np.mean(t <= 0.7 + h / 2) - np.mean(t <= 0.7 - h / 2)) / h
print(f"slope at 0.7: sim {slope:.4f}, exact {2 * 0.7:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
F(0.3): sim 0.0900, exact 0.0900
F(0.5): sim 0.2500, exact 0.2500
F(0.9): sim 0.8099, exact 0.8100
slope at 0.7: sim 1.3957, exact 1.4000
```

The empirical CDF matches $t^2$, and its slope matches the density $2t$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the pieces of the CDF.** For $f(x) = 3x^2$ on $[0, 1]$, writing $F(x) = x^3$
  everywhere gives $F(2) = 8$, which is not a probability. Always state $F = 0$ to the
  left of the support and $F = 1$ to the right.
- **Starting the integral at the wrong place.** The CDF integral starts at the left end
  of the support, not always at 0. For $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ on $x \ge 1$,
  $F(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t^2} \thinspace dt = 1 - \frac{1}{x}$.
- **Mixing up $F$ and $f$.** $F(x)$ is a probability, between 0 and 1. $f(x)$ is a rate
  and can be any non-negative number.
- **Worrying about corners.** At a corner of $F$ (such as $x = 1$ in Example 1) the slope
  is not defined. It does not matter: changing a density at a single point does not
  change any area.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the sigmoid function $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ used in logistic
  regression is a CDF (of the logistic distribution,
  [9.10 Logistic and Gumbel](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md)).
- **Computer science:** latency dashboards plot the empirical CDF of response times to
  show what fraction of requests finish within each time.
- **Engineering:** the CDF of a lifetime is the fraction of machines failed by time
  $x$, and $1 - F(x)$ is the fraction still working.
- **Medicine:** growth charts give the CDF of height or weight for each age.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ has CDF $F(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ (0 to the left, 1 to the right). Find
$P(X \le 0.3)$ and $P(0.3 \lt X \le 0.6)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$P(X \le 0.3) = F(0.3) = 0.09$$

$$P(0.3 \lt X \le 0.6) = F(0.6) - F(0.3) = 0.36 - 0.09 = 0.27$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ has CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-3x}$ for $x \ge 0$ (and 0 for $x \lt 0$). Find its density.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The derivative of $e^{-3x}$ is $-3e^{-3x}$, so:

$$f(x) = F'(x) = 0 - (-3e^{-3x}) = 3e^{-3x} \quad \text{for } x \ge 0$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X$ has density $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ for $x \ge 1$, and 0 elsewhere. Find
the CDF and $P(X \le 4)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $x \lt 1$, $F(x) = 0$. For $x \ge 1$:

$$F(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t^2} \thinspace dt = \left[ -\frac{1}{t} \right]_1^x = 1 - \frac{1}{x}$$

So $P(X \le 4) = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = 0.75$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Which of these can be a CDF? (a) $F(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ for all
$x$. (b) $F(x) = x^2$ for $-1 \le x \le 1$ (0 to the left, 1 to the right).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Yes. It is between 0 and 1, always increasing, goes to 0 at the far left and to 1
at the far right, and has no jumps. It is the sigmoid function. Its density is the
slope, $f(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2}$.

(b) No. $F(-1) = 1$ but $F(0) = 0$, so it goes **down** on $[-1, 0]$. A CDF can never
decrease. (It also jumps from 0 to 1 at $x = -1$.)

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Two tasks take independent times spread evenly on $[0, 1]$. Let $M$ be the
time of the **faster** one, $M = \min(\text{task 1}, \text{task 2})$. Find the CDF and density of
$M$, and $P(M \le 0.5)$. Hint: $M \gt m$ means both tasks are slower than $m$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $0 \le m \le 1$, each task is slower than $m$ with probability $1 - m$, independently:

$$P(M \gt m) = (1 - m)(1 - m) = (1 - m)^2$$

So $F_M(m) = 1 - (1 - m)^2$, and the slope is $f_M(m) = 2(1 - m)$ on $[0, 1]$. Then:

$$P(M \le 0.5) = 1 - (1 - 0.5)^2 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 5 with a simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
m = np.minimum(rng.random(1_000_000), rng.random(1_000_000))
print(f"P(M <= 0.5): simulation {np.mean(m <= 0.5):.4f}, exact {0.75:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(M <= 0.5): simulation 0.7502, exact 0.7500
```

</details>

## Summary

- The CDF $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$ is the area under the density to the left of $x$.
- The density is the slope of the CDF: $f_X = F_X'$.
- Every interval probability is one subtraction: $P(a \lt X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a)$.
- A continuous CDF climbs from 0 to 1, never goes down, and never jumps.
- To find a hard density, find the CDF first, then take its slope.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t) \thinspace dt \qquad \text{and} \qquad f_X(x) = F_X'(x)}
$$
