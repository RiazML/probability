# 8.8 Change of Variables

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.8 Change of Variables

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 50 minutes · **Before this:** [8.7 Quantile Function](8.7-Quantile-Function.md)

> **Big idea:** When Y = g(X) for a strictly increasing or decreasing g, the density of Y is the density of X at the matching point, divided by how much g stretches space there.

---

## Why It Matters

We transform random variables all the time: Celsius to Fahrenheit, a uniform random
number into a waiting time, a raw feature into its logarithm. LOTUS
([8.6](8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md)) gives the **mean** of $g(X)$, but sometimes you
need the whole **density** of $g(X)$, for probabilities, quantiles or likelihoods. This
topic gives a formula for it when $g$ is monotone. It is the one-variable version of the
Jacobian formula behind normalizing flows in machine learning
([13.3](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)).

## Intuition

Today's temperature $C$ is spread evenly between 20 and 30 degrees Celsius. The
density is $\frac{1}{10}$ per degree Celsius. Now convert to Fahrenheit:
$F = 1.8C + 32$. The range becomes 68 to 86, which is 18 Fahrenheit degrees wide.

The probability did not change: there is still a 100% chance the temperature is in the
range. But the range is now 1.8 times wider. The same amount of probability spread
over 1.8 times more room must be 1.8 times thinner. So the new density is
$\frac{1}{10} \div 1.8 = \frac{1}{18}$ per degree Fahrenheit.

That is the whole idea. A small strip of $X$ maps to a small strip of $Y$. The two
strips hold the **same probability**. If $g$ stretches the strip, the density gets
lower; if $g$ squeezes it, the density gets higher.

## Picture

```text
          x   x+dx                              g(x)    g(x+dx)
    X: ---[####]------------ x      g     Y: ---[##########]----------- y
          width dx              ------->        width dy = g'(x) dx
          prob f_X(x) dx                        same prob f_X(x) dx

    new height = prob / width:   f_Y(y) = f_X(x) dx / dy = f_X(x) / g'(x)
```

The function $g$ moves a strip and changes its width, but not its probability, so the
height changes by the stretch factor.

## Definition

### A One-Minute Reminder About the Chain Rule

If you put one function inside another, $F(h(y))$, its derivative is the outer slope
times the inner slope: $\frac{d}{dy} F(h(y)) = F'(h(y)) \cdot h'(y)$. That is the
**chain rule**, and it is the only new calculus in this topic.

### The Formula

Let $X$ have density $f_X$, and let $Y = g(X)$, where $g$ is **strictly monotone**
(always increasing or always decreasing) and differentiable on the support of $X$. Let
$h = g^{-1}$ be the inverse function, so $x = h(y)$ exactly when $y = g(x)$. Then:

$$
f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y)) \cdot \left\lvert h'(y) \right\rvert = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big) \cdot \left\lvert \frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y) \right\rvert
$$

where:

- $y$ is any value that $g$ can produce (for all other $y$, $f_Y(y) = 0$),
- $h(y) = g^{-1}(y)$ is the value of $X$ that produces $y$,
- $\lvert h'(y) \rvert$ is the **stretch factor**: how much a small interval of $y$-values
  shrinks when we map it back to $x$-values.

**In words:** to find the density of $Y$ at $y$, go back to the $x$ that produced $y$,
read the density of $X$ there, and multiply by how much $g^{-1}$ squeezes space.

Since $h'(y) = \frac{1}{g'(x)}$ at the matching points, the same rule reads
$f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(x)}{\lvert g'(x) \rvert}$ with $x = h(y)$, which is the picture above.

## Why It Is True

Start from the CDF, as in [8.4](8.4-CDF-and-PDF-Relationship.md), and then take the
slope.

**Case 1: $g$ increasing.** Then $g(X) \le y$ exactly when $X \le h(y)$, because an
increasing function keeps the order of numbers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_Y(y) &= P(g(X) \le y) = P(X \le h(y)) = F_X(h(y)) && \text{(}g \text{ keeps order)} \\
f_Y(y) &= F_X'(h(y)) \cdot h'(y) = f_X(h(y)) \cdot h'(y) && \text{(chain rule)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $h$ is increasing too, so $h'(y) \ge 0$ and $h'(y) = \lvert h'(y) \rvert$.

**Case 2: $g$ decreasing.** Now $g(X) \le y$ exactly when $X \ge h(y)$, because a
decreasing function flips the order:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_Y(y) &= P(X \ge h(y)) = 1 - F_X(h(y)) && \text{(}g \text{ flips order; no point mass)} \\
f_Y(y) &= -f_X(h(y)) \cdot h'(y) && \text{(chain rule)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $h$ is decreasing, so $h'(y) \le 0$ and $-h'(y) = \lvert h'(y) \rvert$. Both cases give the
same formula, with the absolute value.

If $g$ is **not** monotone, such as $g(x) = x^2$ on $[-1, 1]$, several $x$-values give the
same $y$. Then use the CDF method directly
([13.1 CDF Method](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.1-CDF-Method.md), and Problem 5
below), or add one term per $x$-value
([13.2](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.2-Change-of-Variables-1D.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Celsius to Fahrenheit

**Problem.** $C$ is spread evenly on $[20, 30]$, with density $\frac{1}{10}$. Find the
density of $Y = 1.8C + 32$.

**Solution.**

1. $g(c) = 1.8c + 32$ is increasing. It maps $[20, 30]$ to $[68, 86]$.
2. Invert: $y = 1.8c + 32$ gives $h(y) = \frac{y - 32}{1.8}$, with slope $h'(y) = \frac{1}{1.8}$.
3. Apply the formula for $68 \le y \le 86$:

$$
f_Y(y) = f_C\left( \frac{y - 32}{1.8} \right) \cdot \frac{1}{1.8} = \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{1.8} = \frac{1}{18}
$$

**Answer.** $Y$ is spread evenly on $[68, 86]$ with density $\frac{1}{18}$.

**Check.** The area is $\frac{1}{18} \times (86 - 68) = 1$. In general, for $Y = aX + b$ with
$a \ne 0$, the formula gives $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\lvert a \rvert} f_X(\frac{y - b}{a})$, the unit
change rule of [8.3](8.3-Densities-Can-Exceed-One.md).

### Example 2: From Uniform to Waiting Time

**Problem.** $X$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$, with density 1. Find the density of
$Y = -\ln X$.

**Solution.**

1. $g(x) = -\ln x$ is **decreasing** on $(0, 1)$. As $x$ goes from 1 down to 0, $y$ goes
   from 0 up to $\infty$. So $Y$ takes values in $(0, \infty)$.
2. Invert: $y = -\ln x$ gives $x = e^{-y}$, so $h(y) = e^{-y}$ and $h'(y) = -e^{-y}$.
3. Apply the formula for $y \gt 0$:

$$
f_Y(y) = f_X(e^{-y}) \cdot \left\lvert -e^{-y} \right\rvert = 1 \cdot e^{-y} = e^{-y}
$$

**Answer.** $f_Y(y) = e^{-y}$ for $y \gt 0$. This is the exponential distribution with rate 1
([9.3 Exponential](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)).

**Check.** Without the absolute value we would get $-e^{-y}$, a negative density, which
is impossible. This transformation is a common way computers turn uniform random
numbers into exponential waiting times. The simulation below confirms it.

### Example 3: Squaring a Uniform Number

**Problem.** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. Find the density of $Y = X^2$.

**Solution.**

1. $g(x) = x^2$ is increasing on $[0, 1]$ (all values are non-negative), and maps
   $[0, 1]$ to $[0, 1]$.
2. Invert: $h(y) = \sqrt{y} = y^{1/2}$, with slope $h'(y) = \frac{1}{2} y^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$.
3. Apply the formula for $0 \lt y \le 1$:

$$
f_Y(y) = f_X(\sqrt{y}) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}
$$

**Answer.** $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$ on $(0, 1]$.

**Check.** This is the density with no maximum from Example 2 of
[8.3](8.3-Densities-Can-Exceed-One.md). Squaring squeezes the numbers near 0
together (for example $[0, 0.1]$ becomes $[0, 0.01]$), so the density there is very high.

## Simulate It

We check Example 2: transform uniform numbers by $-\ln X$, then compare densities and a
tail probability with $e^{-y}$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 4_000_000

# Y = -ln(X) with X uniform on (0, 1); claim: density e^(-y).
y = -np.log(1 - rng.random(n_trials))     # 1 - U avoids log(0)

h = 0.02                                   # strip width
for point in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    in_strip = np.abs(y - point) <= h / 2
    print(f"f_Y({point}): sim {in_strip.mean() / h:.4f}, "
          f"exact {np.exp(-point):.4f}")
print(f"P(Y > 1):   sim {np.mean(y > 1):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-1):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
f_Y(0.5): sim 0.6088, exact 0.6065
f_Y(1.0): sim 0.3658, exact 0.3679
f_Y(2.0): sim 0.1368, exact 0.1353
P(Y > 1):   sim 0.3675, exact 0.3679
```

Note that $1 - U$ is also uniform on $(0, 1]$, so it has the same distribution as $X$.
The estimated heights match $e^{-y}$ at every point.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the stretch factor.** Writing $f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y))$ alone is wrong. In
  Example 1 it gives $\frac{1}{10}$ on a range of width 18, so the area would be 1.8, not 1.
- **Forgetting the absolute value.** For a decreasing $g$, $h'(y)$ is negative. Without
  $\lvert \cdot \rvert$ the density comes out negative, as in Example 2.
- **Forgetting the new support.** State where $Y$ lives. In Example 2 it is $(0, \infty)$,
  not $(0, 1)$.
- **Using the formula for a non-monotone $g$.** For $Y = X^2$ with $X$ on $[-1, 1]$, two
  values of $x$ give the same $y$. Use the CDF method instead.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** **normalizing flows** build a complex density by
  pushing a simple one through invertible functions, and compute the new density with
  this formula (its many-variable version uses a determinant).
- **Computer science:** random number libraries turn uniform numbers into other
  distributions with transformations like $-\ln U$
  ([19.4 Inverse Transform Sampling](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md)).
- **Data science:** taking logs of skewed data (incomes, prices) changes the density;
  the formula tells you exactly how.
- **Physics and engineering:** converting units, or going from speed to kinetic energy,
  changes densities by the stretch factor.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. Find the density of $Y = 3X + 2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$h(y) = \frac{y - 2}{3}$ and $h'(y) = \frac{1}{3}$. $Y$ lives on $[2, 5]$:

$$f_Y(y) = 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{3} \quad \text{for } 2 \le y \le 5$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ has density $2x$ on $[0, 1]$. Find the density of $Y = 10X$, and check that
its area is 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$h(y) = \frac{y}{10}$ and $h'(y) = \frac{1}{10}$. For $0 \le y \le 10$:

$$f_Y(y) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{y}{10} \cdot \tfrac{1}{10} = \tfrac{y}{50}$$

$$\int_0^{10} \tfrac{y}{50} \thinspace dy = \left[ \tfrac{y^2}{100} \right]_0^{10} = 1$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. Find the density of $Y = \sqrt{X}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$g(x) = \sqrt{x}$ is increasing, from $[0, 1]$ to $[0, 1]$. Invert: $h(y) = y^2$, so
$h'(y) = 2y$:

$$f_Y(y) = 1 \cdot 2y = 2y \quad \text{for } 0 \le y \le 1$$

This is why $\sqrt{U}$ was used to draw from the density $2x$ in
[8.5](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md).

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X$ has density $e^{-x}$ for $x \ge 0$. Find the density of $Y = e^X$, and
$P(Y \gt 10)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$g(x) = e^x$ is increasing and maps $[0, \infty)$ to $[1, \infty)$. Invert: $h(y) = \ln y$, so
$h'(y) = \frac{1}{y}$. For $y \ge 1$:

$$f_Y(y) = e^{-\ln y} \cdot \tfrac{1}{y} = \tfrac{1}{y} \cdot \tfrac{1}{y} = \tfrac{1}{y^2}$$

Then:

$$P(Y \gt 10) = \int_{10}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{y^2} \thinspace dy = \left[ -\tfrac{1}{y} \right]_{10}^{\infty} = 0.1$$

A light tail for $X$ becomes a heavy tail for $e^X$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $X$ is uniform on $[-1, 1]$, with density $\frac{1}{2}$. Find the density of
$Y = X^2$. Why can you not use the formula from this topic directly?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$g(x) = x^2$ is not monotone on $[-1, 1]$: both $x$ and $-x$ give the same $y$. Use the CDF.
For $0 \le y \le 1$:

$$F_Y(y) = P(X^2 \le y) = P(-\sqrt{y} \le X \le \sqrt{y}) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2\sqrt{y} = \sqrt{y}$$

Take the slope: $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$ on $(0, 1]$. It is the same as in Example 3,
because $\lvert X \rvert$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $X^2 = \lvert X \rvert^2$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
y = np.exp(rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=1_000_000))   # Y = e^X
print(f"P(Y > 10): simulation {np.mean(y > 10):.4f}, exact {0.1:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(Y > 10): simulation 0.1001, exact 0.1000
```

</details>

## Summary

- For $Y = g(X)$ with $g$ strictly monotone, a strip of $X$ and its image strip of $Y$ hold
  the same probability.
- So the density changes by the stretch factor:
  $f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y)) \lvert h'(y) \rvert$ with $h = g^{-1}$.
- The absolute value handles decreasing functions. Always state the new support.
- The proof is the CDF method plus the chain rule. For non-monotone $g$, use the CDF
  method directly.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_Y(y) = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big) \cdot \left\lvert \frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y) \right\rvert}
$$
