# 8.9 Mixed Distributions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.9 Mixed Distributions

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [8.8 Change of Variables](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md)

> **Big idea:** Some random variables put a lump of probability on a few exact values and spread the rest as a density, so you handle the lumps with sums and the rest with integrals.

---

## Why It Matters

Tomorrow's rainfall is exactly 0 mm on many days, and some positive amount on the
others. An insurance claim is exactly 0 for most customers. A request that hits a
timeout is recorded as exactly the timeout. A ReLU unit in a neural network outputs
exactly 0 half the time. None of these is purely discrete or purely continuous, so
neither a PMF nor a density alone can describe them. A **mixed distribution** uses
both.

## Intuition

You drive up to a traffic light. 40% of the time it is green, and you wait exactly
0 seconds. The other 60% of the time it is red, and your wait is spread evenly between
0 and 60 seconds.

The wait "exactly 0" has probability 0.4. That is a lump of probability on one point,
which is impossible for a continuous variable
([8.2](8.2-Why-Point-Probabilities-are-Zero.md)). The remaining 0.6 is spread over
the interval from 0 to 60, like a density. So the wait is part discrete (one lump at
0) and part continuous (a flat density of height $\frac{0.6}{60} = 0.01$ per second).

To find any probability, add the two parts: the lumps that fall in your range, plus
the area of the density part over your range.

## Picture

```text
  F(x)
  1.0 +                                    ..'*-------------
  0.9 |                              ..''
  0.8 |                        ..''                  density part: F rises
  0.7 |                  ..''                        smoothly from 0.4 to 1
  0.6 |            ..''
  0.5 |      ..''
  0.4 +   *
  0.3 |   |
  0.2 |   |   <- jump of 0.4 = P(X = 0)
  0.1 |   |
  0.0 +---o-----------------------------------+---------------> x
          0                                  60
```

The CDF of the traffic-light wait jumps by 0.4 at $x = 0$ (the lump), then rises
smoothly to 1 at $x = 60$ (the density part).

## Definition

A **mixed random variable** $X$ has two parts:

- **point masses** (also called **atoms**): values $a_1, a_2, \dots$ with
  $P(X = a_i) = p_i \gt 0$;
- a **density part**: a function $g(x) \ge 0$ that spreads the rest of the probability.

Together they hold all the probability:

$$
\sum_i p_i + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \thinspace dx = 1
$$

**In words:** the lumps plus the area of the density part make 1. So the density part
alone has area $1 - \sum_i p_i$, **not** 1.

Every probability, CDF and expectation is "sum over the lumps plus integral over the
density part":

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X \in A) &= \sum_{a_i \in A} p_i + \int_A g(x) \thinspace dx \\
F_X(x) &= \sum_{a_i \le x} p_i + \int_{-\infty}^{x} g(t) \thinspace dt \\
E[h(X)] &= \sum_i h(a_i) \thinspace p_i + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(x) \thinspace g(x) \thinspace dx
\end{aligned}
$$

where $A$ is any set of values and $h$ is any function (with $h(x) = x$ for the mean).

**In words:** handle the lumps like a PMF, handle the rest like a density, and add.

The CDF jumps by exactly $p_i$ at each atom $a_i$ and has no other jumps. So, unlike
for a continuous variable, **endpoints matter** at an atom: $P(X \le a_i)$ and
$P(X \lt a_i)$ differ by $p_i$.

## Why It Is True

**Probabilities.** Split the event $\lbrace X \in A \rbrace$ into two parts that do not overlap:
$X$ equals one of the atoms in $A$, or $X$ falls in $A$ but on none of the atoms. The
addition rule adds them. The atoms are a countable list, so their part is the sum of
their $p_i$. The second part is described by the density part, so it is an area:

$$
P(X \in A) = P(X \text{ is an atom in } A) + P(X \in A, \text{ not an atom}) = \sum_{a_i \in A} p_i + \int_A g(x) \thinspace dx
$$

The CDF is the special case $A = (-\infty, x]$.

**Expectations.** Use the strip argument of
[8.5](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md). Each atom is a "strip" of zero
width but positive probability $p_i$, so it adds $h(a_i) p_i$. Each thin strip of the
density part adds about $h(x) g(x) \thinspace dx$. Adding everything gives the sum plus
the integral.

**Jumps.** The size of the jump of $F_X$ at $a$ is $P(X = a)$
(by [3.9 Continuity of Probability](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.9-Continuity-of-Probability.md)).
The density part adds no jumps, as shown in [8.4](8.4-CDF-and-PDF-Relationship.md).
So jumps happen exactly at the atoms.

A mixed variable can also be seen as a **mixture**: flip a coin to decide whether to
draw from a discrete distribution or from a continuous one. This view is studied in
[10.8 Mixture Distributions](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.8-Mixture-Distributions.md).
(There are also rare "singular" distributions that are neither; they are explained in
[20.10 Radon-Nikodym and Densities](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.10-Radon-Nikodym-and-Densities.md).)

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Traffic Light

**Problem.** The wait $X$ at a traffic light is 0 with probability 0.4. Otherwise it is
spread evenly on $(0, 60]$ seconds, so the density part is $g(x) = \frac{0.6}{60} = 0.01$
there. Find $P(X \le 0)$, $P(X \lt 0)$, $P(X \le 15)$, $E[X]$ and the median.

**Solution.**

1. Check the total: $0.4 + 0.01 \times 60 = 0.4 + 0.6 = 1$.
2. At the atom, endpoints matter: $P(X \le 0) = 0.4$, but $P(X \lt 0) = 0$.
3. Up to 15 seconds: the lump plus the area from 0 to 15:

$$
P(X \le 15) = 0.4 + 0.01 \times 15 = 0.4 + 0.15 = 0.55
$$

4. Mean: the atom adds $0 \times 0.4 = 0$, and the density part adds an integral:

$$
E[X] = 0 \times 0.4 + \int_0^{60} x \cdot 0.01 \thinspace dx = 0.01 \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^{60} = 0.01 \times 1800 = 18
$$

5. Median: for $0 \le x \le 60$, $F(x) = 0.4 + 0.01x$. Solve $0.4 + 0.01x = 0.5$: $x = 10$.

**Answer.** $P(X \le 0) = 0.4$, $P(X \lt 0) = 0$, $P(X \le 15) = 0.55$, the mean wait is 18
seconds, and the median is 10 seconds.

**Check.** Another way to get the mean: 60% of drivers wait on average 30 seconds, and
40% wait 0, so $0.6 \times 30 = 18$. Treating the wait as uniform on $[0, 60]$ would give 30
and ignore the green lights.

### Example 2: A ReLU Unit

**Problem.** In a neural network, a unit receives an input $X$ spread evenly on
$[-1, 1]$ (density $\frac{1}{2}$) and outputs $Y = \max(X, 0)$, called the **ReLU** of $X$.
Describe the distribution of $Y$ and find $E[Y]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$.

**Solution.**

1. Every negative input becomes 0, so $Y = 0$ exactly when $X \le 0$:
   $P(Y = 0) = P(X \le 0) = \frac{1}{2}$. That is an atom at 0.
2. For $0 \lt y \le 1$, $Y = X$, so the density part is $g(y) = \frac{1}{2}$ there.
   Check: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \times 1 = 1$.
3. Mean:

$$
E[Y] = 0 \times \frac{1}{2} + \int_0^1 y \cdot \frac{1}{2} \thinspace dy = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}
$$

4. Second moment and variance:

$$
E[Y^2] = 0^2 \times \frac{1}{2} + \int_0^1 y^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \thinspace dy = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{16} = \frac{8}{48} - \frac{3}{48} = \frac{5}{48} \approx 0.104
$$

**Answer.** $Y$ has an atom of $\frac{1}{2}$ at 0 and density $\frac{1}{2}$ on $(0, 1]$, with
$E[Y] = 0.25$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{5}{48} \approx 0.104$.

**Check.** Half the outputs are 0 and the other half average $0.5$, so the mean is
$\frac{1}{2} \times 0.5 = 0.25$. Treating $Y$ as continuous would miss the half of the
probability sitting on 0.

### Example 3: A Request With a Timeout

**Problem.** A server's response time $T$ has density $2e^{-2t}$ for $t \ge 0$ (from
[8.1](8.1-Probability-Density-Function.md)). The client gives up after 1 second, so it
records $Y = \min(T, 1)$. Find $P(Y = 1)$ and $E[Y]$.

**Solution.**

1. $Y = 1$ exactly when $T \ge 1$, so there is an atom at 1:

$$
P(Y = 1) = P(T \ge 1) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353
$$

2. For $0 \le y \lt 1$, $Y = T$, so the density part is $g(y) = 2e^{-2y}$ on $[0, 1)$.
3. For the mean we need $\int_0^1 y \cdot 2e^{-2y} \thinspace dy$. An antiderivative of
   $2y e^{-2y}$ is $-(y + \frac{1}{2}) e^{-2y}$. (Check it: its derivative is
   $-e^{-2y} + 2(y + \frac{1}{2}) e^{-2y} = 2y e^{-2y}$.) So:

$$
\int_0^1 2y e^{-2y} \thinspace dy = \left[ -\left( y + \tfrac{1}{2} \right) e^{-2y} \right]_0^1 = -1.5 e^{-2} + 0.5
$$

4. Add the atom, which contributes $1 \times e^{-2}$:

$$
E[Y] = 0.5 - 1.5 e^{-2} + e^{-2} = 0.5 - 0.5 e^{-2} = \frac{1 - e^{-2}}{2} \approx 0.4323
$$

**Answer.** About 13.5% of requests are recorded at exactly 1 second, and the mean
recorded time is about $0.432$ seconds.

**Check.** Without the timeout the mean would be $E[T] = 0.5$. Cutting the slow requests
down to 1 second can only lower the mean, and it does, a little.

## Simulate It

We simulate the traffic light and the timeout and compare with Examples 1 and 3.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Traffic light: green (wait 0) with prob 0.4, else uniform on (0, 60].
green = rng.random(n_trials) < 0.4
wait = np.where(green, 0.0, rng.uniform(0, 60, size=n_trials))
print(f"P(X = 0):   sim {np.mean(wait == 0):.4f}, exact 0.4000")
print(f"P(X <= 15): sim {np.mean(wait <= 15):.4f}, exact 0.5500")
print(f"E[X]:       sim {wait.mean():.4f}, exact 18.0000")

# Timeout: response time with density 2 e^(-2t), cut at 1 second.
y = np.minimum(rng.exponential(scale=0.5, size=n_trials), 1.0)
print(f"P(Y = 1):   sim {np.mean(y == 1.0):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-2):.4f}")
print(f"E[Y]:       sim {y.mean():.4f}, exact {(1 - np.exp(-2)) / 2:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(X = 0):   sim 0.3999, exact 0.4000
P(X <= 15): sim 0.5501, exact 0.5500
E[X]:       sim 17.9871, exact 18.0000
P(Y = 1):   sim 0.1353, exact 0.1353
E[Y]:       sim 0.4326, exact 0.4323
```

The lumps show up as exact repeated values (many waits of exactly 0, many times of
exactly 1), something a purely continuous variable never does.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a mixed variable as continuous.** Saying $P(\text{wait} = 0) = 0$ is wrong
  here; it is 0.4. Look for values that happen with positive probability.
- **Forgetting the atoms in the mean.** In Example 3, the atom at 1 adds $e^{-2}$ to the
  mean. Leaving it out gives the wrong answer.
- **Making the density part integrate to 1.** It must integrate to $1 - \sum_i p_i$. In
  Example 1 it is 0.6, not 1.
- **Ignoring endpoints at an atom.** $P(X \le 0) = 0.4$ but $P(X \lt 0) = 0$. At an atom,
  $\le$ and $\lt$ differ.
- **Solving $F(x) = u$ at a jump.** In Example 1, no $x$ has $F(x) = 0.3$. Use
  $Q(u) = \min \lbrace x : F(x) \ge u \rbrace$ ([8.7](8.7-Quantile-Function.md)), which gives $Q(0.3) = 0$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** ReLU activations are mixed. **Spike-and-slab** priors put
  an atom at 0 plus a density elsewhere to make models sparse. **Zero-inflated** models
  handle data with many exact zeros.
- **Computer science:** timeouts and retries record capped times, like Example 3.
  Measured latencies often mix cache hits and misses.
- **Insurance and finance:** most customers claim exactly 0; the rest claim a
  continuous amount. Deductibles create an atom at 0 in the payout.
- **Medicine:** in a study that ends after 5 years, every patient still alive is
  recorded at exactly 5 years (this is called **censoring**).
- **Everyday life:** daily rainfall is 0 on dry days and a continuous amount otherwise.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** On a given day, there is no rain with probability 0.7. Otherwise the rain is
spread evenly on $(0, 20]$ mm. Find $P(\text{rain} \le 5)$ and the expected rainfall.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The density part is $\frac{0.3}{20} = 0.015$ on $(0, 20]$.

$$P(X \le 5) = 0.7 + 0.015 \times 5 = 0.7 + 0.075 = 0.775$$

$$E[X] = 0 \times 0.7 + \int_0^{20} x \cdot 0.015 \thinspace dx = 0.015 \times 200 = 3 \text{ mm}$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** For the traffic light of Example 1, find $P(X \lt 15)$ and $P(X = 15)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

15 is not an atom, so $P(X = 15) = 0$, and $P(X \lt 15) = P(X \le 15) = 0.55$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X$ has atoms $P(X = 0) = 0.2$ and $P(X = 1) = 0.3$, and a density part
$g(x) = c$ on $(0, 1)$. Find $c$ and $E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The total must be 1: $0.2 + 0.3 + c \times 1 = 1$, so $c = 0.5$. Then:

$$E[X] = 0 \times 0.2 + 1 \times 0.3 + \int_0^1 x \cdot 0.5 \thinspace dx = 0.3 + 0.25 = 0.55$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For the timeout of Example 3, what is the median of $Y$? Is it affected
by the timeout?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \lt 1$, $F_Y(y) = P(T \le y) = 1 - e^{-2y}$. Solve $1 - e^{-2y} = 0.5$:

$$y = \tfrac{\ln 2}{2} \approx 0.3466 \text{ seconds}$$

This is below 1, so the timeout does not change the median. The timeout changes only
the slow values, so it moves the mean but not the median.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** A loss $L$ is spread evenly on $[0, 1000]$ taka. An insurance policy with a
deductible of 200 taka pays $Y = \max(L - 200, 0)$. Describe the distribution of $Y$ and
find the expected payout.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Y = 0$ when $L \le 200$, so there is an atom $P(Y = 0) = \frac{200}{1000} = 0.2$. For
$0 \lt y \le 800$, $Y = L - 200$, so the density part is $\frac{1}{1000}$ there. Check:
$0.2 + 800 \times \frac{1}{1000} = 1$. The mean is:

$$E[Y] = 0 \times 0.2 + \int_0^{800} y \cdot \tfrac{1}{1000} \thinspace dy = \tfrac{1}{1000} \cdot \tfrac{800^2}{2} = \tfrac{320000}{1000} = 320 \text{ taka}$$

</details>


</details>

## Summary

- A mixed variable has atoms (values with positive probability) plus a density part.
- The atoms and the area of the density part add up to 1, so the density part alone has
  area less than 1.
- Every probability and expectation is a sum over the atoms plus an integral over the
  density part.
- The CDF jumps at each atom by its probability, so $\le$ and $\lt$ differ there.
- Timeouts, ReLU, rainfall and insurance claims are everyday mixed variables.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[h(X)] = \sum_i h(a_i) \thinspace p_i + \int h(x) \thinspace g(x) \thinspace dx}
$$
