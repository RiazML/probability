# 8.6 LOTUS for Continuous

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.6 LOTUS for Continuous

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [8.5 Expectation and Variance with Integrals](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md)

> **Big idea:** To find the mean of g(X), integrate g(x) times the density of X; you never need the density of g(X) itself.

---

## Why It Matters

We often care about a function of a random variable, not the variable itself: the
area of a random square, the travel time at a random speed, the loss of a model on a
random input. Finding the density of $g(X)$ first is hard work
([8.8 Change of Variables](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md)). The **law of the unconscious
statistician** (**LOTUS**) skips that work. You met the discrete version in
[6.3 LOTUS](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.3-LOTUS.md). The continuous
version is the same idea with an integral.

## Intuition

A factory cuts metal squares. The side length $X$ is random, spread evenly between
0 and 2 cm. What is the average area?

A tempting answer: the average side is 1 cm, so the average area is $1^2 = 1$ square
cm. That is **wrong**. Here is the right way to think. Cut the possible side lengths
into thin strips. A strip near side length $x$ has probability about
$f(x) \thinspace dx$. Every square in that strip has area about $x^2$. So the average
area is "area times probability", added over all strips: $\int x^2 f(x) \thinspace dx$.

The weights $f(x) \thinspace dx$ belong to $X$ and never change. We only replace each
value $x$ by the new value $g(x) = x^2$. Big squares (side near 2, area near 4) pull
the average area up more than small squares pull it down, so the answer is more than 1.

## Picture

```text
  strip of X near x                 g                 value of g(X)
  +--------------------+                        +--------------------+
  | value:  x          |  --------------------> | value:  g(x)       |
  | prob:   f(x) dx    |                        | prob:   f(x) dx    |
  +--------------------+                        +--------------------+

  E[g(X)]  =  add up  g(x) * f(x) dx  over all strips
           =  integral of g(x) f(x) dx
```

The function $g$ changes each value, but each strip keeps its probability, so we can
weight $g(x)$ by the density of $X$.

## Definition

Let $X$ be continuous with density $f_X$, and let $g$ be a function. The **law of the
unconscious statistician** (**LOTUS**) says:

$$
E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx
$$

where $g(x)$ is the new value and $f_X(x)$ is the density of the **original** variable
$X$. The mean exists when $\int \lvert g(x) \rvert f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ is finite.

**In words:** the average of $g(X)$ is "new value times old probability", added up
over all values of $X$.

Some special cases you already use:

| $g(x)$ | What LOTUS gives |
|---|---|
| $x^2$ | the second moment $E[X^2]$, used for the variance in [8.5](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md) |
| $ax + b$ | linearity: $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$ |
| $(x - \mu)^2$ | the variance itself |
| $e^{tx}$ | the moment generating function ([15.1](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.1-Moment-Generating-Function.md)) |
| $1$ if $x \in A$, else $0$ | $P(X \in A)$, the indicator trick ([6.4](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.4-Indicator-Trick.md)) |

> **Warning:** In general $E[g(X)] \ne g(E[X])$. The average of the squares is not the
> square of the average. They are equal when $g$ is a straight line, $g(x) = ax + b$.

## Why It Is True

**The strip argument.** Cut the line into thin strips of width $\Delta$ with left ends
$x_k$. When $X$ is in strip $k$, $g(X)$ is close to $g(x_k)$ (for a smooth $g$). Strip $k$
has probability about $f_X(x_k) \Delta$. So, as in
[8.5](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md):

$$
E[g(X)] \approx \sum_k g(x_k) \thinspace f_X(x_k) \thinspace \Delta \ \xrightarrow{\ \Delta \to 0\ } \ \int g(x) \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx
$$

**Linearity follows.** Take $g(x) = ax + b$ and split the integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[aX + b] &= \int (ax + b) \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx && \text{(LOTUS)} \\
&= a \int x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx + b \int f_X(x) \thinspace dx && \text{(split the integral)} \\
&= aE[X] + b && \text{(definition of the mean, total area 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Full proof for an increasing g (optional)</summary>

Let $g$ be strictly increasing and smooth, with inverse function $h$, and let
$Y = g(X)$. Topic [8.8 Change of Variables](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md) shows that
the density of $Y$ is:

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y)) \thinspace h'(y)$$

So, by the definition of the mean of $Y$:

$$E[Y] = \int y \thinspace f_Y(y) \thinspace dy = \int y \thinspace f_X(h(y)) \thinspace h'(y) \thinspace dy$$

Now substitute $x = h(y)$. Then $y = g(x)$ and $dx = h'(y) \thinspace dy$, so the integral
becomes:

$$E[Y] = \int g(x) \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx$$

This is LOTUS. The general case (any $g$) needs the tools of
[Module 20](../20-ADVANCED-PROBABILITY/20.5-Lebesgue-Integral-and-Expectation.md).

</details>

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Average Area of a Random Square

**Problem.** The side $X$ of a square is spread evenly on $[0, 2]$ cm, with density
$\frac{1}{2}$. Find the average area $E[X^2]$, and compare it with $(E[X])^2$.

**Solution.**

1. Use LOTUS with $g(x) = x^2$:

$$
E[X^2] = \int_0^2 x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{8}{3} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333
$$

2. The average side is the midpoint, $E[X] = 1$, so $(E[X])^2 = 1$.

**Answer.** The average area is $\frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333$ square cm, not 1.

**Check.** The difference is $\frac{4}{3} - 1 = \frac{1}{3}$, which is exactly
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(2 - 0)^2}{12} = \frac{1}{3}$. That matches
$E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)$ from 8.5.

### Example 2: Travel Time at a Random Speed

**Problem.** You drive 10 km. Your average speed $V$, in km per minute, is spread
evenly on $[1, 2]$ (that is 60 to 120 km per hour), so its density is 1 there. The trip
takes $T = \frac{10}{V}$ minutes. Find $E[T]$, and compare it with the time at the average
speed.

**Solution.**

1. Use LOTUS with $g(v) = \frac{10}{v}$ and the antiderivative $10 \ln v$ of $\frac{10}{v}$:

$$
E[T] = \int_1^2 \frac{10}{v} \cdot 1 \thinspace dv = \big[ 10 \ln v \big]_1^2 = 10 \ln 2 - 10 \ln 1 = 10 \ln 2 \approx 6.931
$$

2. The average speed is $E[V] = 1.5$, and the time at that speed is
   $\frac{10}{1.5} \approx 6.667$ minutes.

**Answer.** The average trip takes about $6.931$ minutes, which is longer than the
$6.667$ minutes at the average speed.

**Check.** Slow speeds hurt more than fast speeds help: at speed 1 the trip takes 10
minutes (3.33 more than 6.67), but at speed 2 it takes 5 minutes (only 1.67 less). So
the average time is pulled up. [16.3 Jensen Inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md)
explains this in general.

### Example 3: The Expected Loss of a Prediction

**Problem.** A model must predict a value $X$ with density $f(x) = 3x^2$ on $[0, 1]$. It
predicts a fixed number $c$ and pays the **squared error** $(X - c)^2$. Find the
expected loss as a function of $c$. Which $c$ is best, and what is the loss then?

**Solution.**

1. Use LOTUS with $g(x) = (x - c)^2 = x^2 - 2cx + c^2$:

$$
E[(X - c)^2] = \int_0^1 (x^2 - 2cx + c^2) \thinspace 3x^2 \thinspace dx = \int_0^1 3x^4 \thinspace dx - 2c \int_0^1 3x^3 \thinspace dx + c^2 \int_0^1 3x^2 \thinspace dx
$$

2. The three integrals are $\frac{3}{5}$, $\frac{3}{4}$ and $1$:

$$
E[(X - c)^2] = \frac{3}{5} - 2c \cdot \frac{3}{4} + c^2 \cdot 1 = 0.6 - 1.5c + c^2
$$

3. This is a parabola in $c$. Its lowest point is where its slope $-1.5 + 2c$ is 0,
   so $c = 0.75$.
4. The loss at $c = 0.75$ is $0.6 - 1.5 \times 0.75 + 0.75^2 = 0.6 - 1.125 + 0.5625 = 0.0375$.
   For comparison, at $c = 0.5$ it is $0.6 - 0.75 + 0.25 = 0.1$.

**Answer.** The expected loss is $0.6 - 1.5c + c^2$. The best prediction is $c = 0.75$,
with expected loss $0.0375$.

**Check.** The best $c$ is $E[X] = 0.75$, and the smallest loss is $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.0375$,
both from Example 2 of [8.5](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md). This is
the result of [6.10 Expectation as Best Guess](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md).

## Simulate It

We check all three examples by drawing samples of $X$ and averaging $g(X)$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Example 1: side uniform on [0, 2]; average area.
side = rng.uniform(0, 2, size=n_trials)
print(f"E[X^2]:      sim {np.mean(side**2):.4f}, exact {4 / 3:.4f}")

# Example 2: speed uniform on [1, 2]; average trip time for 10 km.
speed = rng.uniform(1, 2, size=n_trials)
t_exact = 10 * np.log(2)
print(f"E[10 / V]:   sim {np.mean(10 / speed):.4f}, exact {t_exact:.4f}")

# Example 3: X with density 3x^2; squared loss for two predictions.
x = rng.random(n_trials) ** (1 / 3)
for c in [0.5, 0.75]:
    exact = 0.6 - 1.5 * c + c**2
    loss = np.mean((x - c) ** 2)
    print(f"loss c={c:<4}: sim {loss:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[X^2]:      sim 1.3333, exact 1.3333
E[10 / V]:   sim 6.9321, exact 6.9315
loss c=0.5 : sim 0.1000, exact 0.1000
loss c=0.75: sim 0.0375, exact 0.0375
```

Every average of $g(X)$ matches the LOTUS integral, and none of them needed the
density of $g(X)$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Plugging in the mean.** $E[g(X)] = g(E[X])$ is false in general (Examples 1 and 2).
  It is true only for straight lines $g(x) = ax + b$.
- **Using the wrong density.** In $\int g(x) f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ the density is the one of $X$,
  not of $g(X)$. You never need $f_{g(X)}$.
- **Forgetting the density.** $\int g(x) \thinspace dx$ alone is not an expectation. Each value
  must be weighted by $f_X(x)$.
- **Changing the limits.** The integral runs over the support of $X$. In Example 2 the
  limits are the speeds 1 and 2, not the times 5 and 10.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** every expected loss $E[\text{loss}(X)]$ and every expected
  reward in reinforcement learning is a LOTUS integral. Training estimates it with an
  average over samples.
- **Computer science:** the expected cost of an algorithm whose running time depends on
  a random input size is a LOTUS integral.
- **Finance:** the price of an option is the expected value of a payoff function
  $g(S)$ of a random future price $S$.
- **Physics:** the average kinetic energy $E[\frac{1}{2} m V^2]$ of gas molecules uses LOTUS
  with the speed distribution.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$ (density 1). Find $E[X^3]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X^3] = \int_0^1 x^3 \cdot 1 \thinspace dx = \left[ \tfrac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^1 = \tfrac{1}{4}$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. Find $E[\sqrt{X}]$ and compare it with $\sqrt{E[X]}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$, with antiderivative $\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} = \frac{2}{3} x^{3/2}$:

$$E[\sqrt{X}] = \int_0^1 x^{1/2} \thinspace dx = \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$$

But $\sqrt{E[X]} = \sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.707$. They are different, because $\sqrt{x}$ is not a
straight line.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A payment arrives after a random time $X$ (in years) with density
$2e^{-2x}$ for $x \ge 0$. Its value today is $e^{-X}$ per taka. Find $E[e^{-X}]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[e^{-X}] = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-x} \cdot 2e^{-2x} \thinspace dx = \int_0^{\infty} 2e^{-3x} \thinspace dx = \left[ -\tfrac{2}{3} e^{-3x} \right]_0^{\infty} = \tfrac{2}{3}$$

So 1 taka paid at the random time is worth about $0.667$ taka today, on average.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X^2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $Y = X^2$. By LOTUS, $E[Y] = \int_0^1 x^2 \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{3}$ and
$E[Y^2] = \int_0^1 x^4 \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{5}$. So:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X^2) = \tfrac{1}{5} - \left( \tfrac{1}{3} \right)^2 = \tfrac{9}{45} - \tfrac{5}{45} = \tfrac{4}{45} \approx 0.0889$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Use LOTUS to prove that for any number $c$,
$E[(X - c)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (E[X] - c)^2$. Explain why this shows that $c = E[X]$ is
the best prediction under squared error.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $\mu = E[X]$ and write $x - c = (x - \mu) + (\mu - c)$. Squaring gives
$(x - \mu)^2 + 2(\mu - c)(x - \mu) + (\mu - c)^2$. Integrate each part against $f_X$:

$$E[(X - c)^2] = \int (x - \mu)^2 f_X \thinspace dx + 2(\mu - c) \int (x - \mu) f_X \thinspace dx + (\mu - c)^2 \int f_X \thinspace dx$$

The first integral is $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. The second is $E[X] - \mu = 0$. The third is 1. So:

$$E[(X - c)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (\mu - c)^2$$

The first term does not depend on $c$, and the second is never negative. It is 0 only
when $c = \mu$, so $c = E[X]$ gives the smallest expected loss.

</details>


</details>

## Summary

- LOTUS: $E[g(X)] = \int g(x) f_X(x) \thinspace dx$. Use the density of $X$, not of $g(X)$.
- Each strip keeps its probability; only its value changes from $x$ to $g(x)$.
- In general $E[g(X)] \ne g(E[X])$, unless $g$ is a straight line.
- Second moments, variances, moment generating functions and expected losses are all
  LOTUS integrals.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx}
$$
