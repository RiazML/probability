# 8.3 Densities Can Exceed One

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.3 Densities Can Exceed One

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 30 minutes · **Before this:** [8.2 Why Point Probabilities are Zero](8.2-Why-Point-Probabilities-are-Zero.md)

> **Big idea:** A density is probability per unit length, not a probability, so it can be larger than 1 as long as the total area stays 1.

---

## Why It Matters

Students often see a density value like 25 and think "a probability of 2500%? That is
wrong!" It is not wrong. A density measures how **crowded** the probability is, and
crowding has no upper limit. This matters in machine learning: the log-likelihood
of continuous data can be positive, so a "negative log-likelihood" loss can go below
zero. That is not a bug, and this topic explains why.

## Intuition

Think about the population density of a crowded city: maybe 20,000 people per square
kilometer. Nobody thinks one square meter holds 20,000 people. The number is a
**rate**, people per unit of area. To get a count, you multiply by an area.

A probability density works the same way. It is probability **per unit length**. To
get a probability, you multiply by a length (or, for a curve, take the area).

Now picture a factory making ball bearings. Every diameter is between 9.98 mm and
10.02 mm, spread evenly. All of the probability, which is 1, sits on a range only
0.04 mm wide. To make the area of the rectangle equal 1, its height must be
$\frac{1}{0.04} = 25$ per millimeter. The density is 25, and nothing is broken.

## Picture

```text
  f(x)                              f(x)
  4.0 +----+                        4.0
      |    |
  3.0 |    |                        3.0
      |    |
  2.0 |    |                        2.0
      |    |
  1.0 |    |                        1.0
      |    |                        0.5 +-----------------------+
  0.0 +----+---------------> x      0.0 +-----------------------+---> x
      0   0.25                          0                       2

   tall and thin:                    short and wide:
   height 4 x width 0.25 = 1         height 0.5 x width 2 = 1
```

Both rectangles have area 1, so both are valid densities. The thin one must be tall,
with height 4.

## Definition

The density at a point is the probability of a tiny interval **divided by its width**:

$$
f_X(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{P(x \le X \le x + h)}{h}
$$

where $h \gt 0$ is the width of the interval (this holds at every point where $f_X$ is
continuous).

**In words:** the density is the probability per unit length near $x$. Its units are
"probability per unit of x", such as "per millimeter" or "per second".

A density has only two rules (from
[8.1 Probability Density Function](8.1-Probability-Density-Function.md)): it is never
negative, and its total area is 1. There is **no** rule that says $f_X(x) \le 1$.

The value of a density depends on the units. If $Y = cX$ for a number $c \gt 0$ (for
example $c = 1000$ to change meters into millimeters), then:

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{c} \thinspace f_X\left( \frac{y}{c} \right)
$$

**In words:** stretching the scale by a factor $c$ spreads the same probability over a
range $c$ times wider, so the density becomes $c$ times lower.

## Why It Is True

**Areas stay at most 1, even when heights do not.** For any interval, the area over it
is part of the total area, so:

$$
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x) \thinspace dx \le \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) \thinspace dx = 1
$$

So probabilities can never exceed 1, but nothing limits the height of the curve.

**A short range forces a tall density.** Suppose $X$ always lies in an interval of
length $L$. If the density were below $\frac{1}{L}$ everywhere in that interval, the
area would be less than height times width:

$$
\int f_X(x) \thinspace dx \lt \frac{1}{L} \cdot L = 1
$$

That breaks the total-area rule. So somewhere the density is at least $\frac{1}{L}$.
When $L \lt 1$, this means the density **must** be bigger than 1 somewhere.

**The change of units.** Take $Y = cX$ with $c \gt 0$. A small interval
$[y, y + h]$ for $Y$ is the interval $[\frac{y}{c}, \frac{y}{c} + \frac{h}{c}]$ for $X$.
So, by the strip rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(y \le Y \le y + h) &= P\left( \frac{y}{c} \le X \le \frac{y}{c} + \frac{h}{c} \right) && \text{(divide by } c \gt 0 \text{)} \\
&\approx f_X\left( \frac{y}{c} \right) \cdot \frac{h}{c} && \text{(strip rule, width } h/c \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing by $h$ gives $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{c} f_X(\frac{y}{c})$. The general version of
this formula is [8.8 Change of Variables](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Ball Bearings in Two Units

**Problem.** Ball bearing diameters are spread evenly between 9.98 mm and 10.02 mm.
Find the density in "per millimeter" and in "per meter". Then find the probability
that a diameter is above 10.01 mm, using each unit.

**Solution.**

1. The range is $10.02 - 9.98 = 0.04$ mm wide. The density is flat, so its height is

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{0.04} = 25 \text{ per mm}
$$

2. In meters, the range is $0.00004$ m wide, so the height is
   $\frac{1}{0.00004} = 25000$ per meter. This matches the formula with $c = \frac{1}{1000}$:
   $\frac{1}{c} \times 25 = 1000 \times 25 = 25000$.
3. In millimeters, "above 10.01" is a strip of width $10.02 - 10.01 = 0.01$ mm:

$$
P(X \gt 10.01) = 0.01 \times 25 = 0.25
$$

4. In meters, the same strip has width $0.00001$ m:

$$
P(X \gt 0.01001) = 0.00001 \times 25000 = 0.25
$$

**Answer.** The density is 25 per mm, or 25,000 per meter. The probability is 0.25 in
both units.

**Check.** The heights differ by a factor of 1000, but the widths differ by the same
factor the other way, so the areas agree. Probabilities never depend on units.

### Example 2: A Density With No Maximum

**Problem.** Let $X$ have density $f(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ for $0 \lt x \le 1$, and 0
elsewhere. Show that the total area is 1, even though $f(x)$ grows without bound near
0. Find $f(0.0001)$ and $P(X \le 0.01)$.

**Solution.**

1. An antiderivative of $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{2} x^{-1/2}$ is
   $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{x^{1/2}}{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$, by the power rule.
2. Total area:

$$
\int_0^1 \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \thinspace dx = \big[ \sqrt{x} \big]_0^1 = 1 - 0 = 1
$$

3. Height near 0: $f(0.0001) = \frac{1}{2 \times 0.01} = 50$.
4. Probability of the first one-hundredth:

$$
P(X \le 0.01) = \big[ \sqrt{x} \big]_0^{0.01} = 0.1 - 0 = 0.1
$$

**Answer.** The area is 1, so it is a valid density. The height at $0.0001$ is 50, and
$P(X \le 0.01) = 0.1$.

**Check.** The interval $[0, 0.01]$ is only 1% of the range but holds 10% of the
probability, so the density there is high on average: $\frac{0.1}{0.01} = 10$. This
$X$ is the square of a uniform random number, as shown in
[8.8 Change of Variables](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md).

### Example 3: A Positive Log-Likelihood

**Problem.** A weather model predicts tomorrow's temperature. It says: "the temperature
is spread evenly between 19.95 and 20.05 degrees". The real temperature is 20.01
degrees. Find the density the model gave to the real value, and its log-likelihood.

**Solution.**

1. The range has width $20.05 - 19.95 = 0.1$, so the density is $\frac{1}{0.1} = 10$
   per degree. The value 20.01 is inside the range, so $f(20.01) = 10$.
2. The **log-likelihood** of an observation is the natural log of the density the model
   gives it:

$$
\ln f(20.01) = \ln 10 \approx 2.303
$$

**Answer.** The density is 10 and the log-likelihood is about $2.303$, a positive number.
The negative log-likelihood loss is about $-2.303$.

**Check.** A confident model squeezes its probability into a narrow range, so its
density is high. For a discrete model, a log-probability is never positive, but for
a density it can be.

## Simulate It

We estimate densities from samples by dividing the fraction in a narrow bin by the
bin width, for the bearings and for Example 2.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Bearings: diameter spread evenly on [9.98, 10.02] mm.
d = rng.uniform(9.98, 10.02, size=n_trials)
width = 0.004
in_bin = np.mean((d >= 10.0) & (d < 10.0 + width))
print(f"density near 10 mm: sim {in_bin / width:.2f}, exact 25.00")
print(f"P(d > 10.01):       sim {np.mean(d > 10.01):.4f}, exact 0.2500")

# Example 2: X = U^2 has density 1 / (2 sqrt(x)) on (0, 1].
x = rng.random(n_trials) ** 2
print(f"P(X <= 0.01):       sim {np.mean(x <= 0.01):.4f}, exact 0.1000")
avg = np.mean(x <= 0.0004) / 0.0004      # exact: sqrt(0.0004) / 0.0004
print(f"avg density on [0, 0.0004]: sim {avg:.2f}, exact 50.00")
```

Output:

```text
density near 10 mm: sim 25.00, exact 25.00
P(d > 10.01):       sim 0.2501, exact 0.2500
P(X <= 0.01):       sim 0.0997, exact 0.1000
avg density on [0, 0.0004]: sim 49.74, exact 50.00
```

The estimated densities are 25 and 50, far above 1, while every probability stays
between 0 and 1.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading a density as a probability.** $f(x) = 25$ does not mean "2500% chance". It
  means 25 per unit of $x$. Multiply by a width, or integrate, to get a probability.
- **Comparing densities measured in different units.** A density of 25 per millimeter
  and 25,000 per meter describe the same distribution. Always say the units.
- **Thinking a negative loss means a bug.** For continuous data, a density above 1
  gives a positive log-likelihood and a negative "negative log-likelihood". This is
  normal.
- **Checking whether $f(x) \le 1$ to test a density.** That is not a rule. Check
  $f(x) \ge 0$ and total area 1 instead.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** models of continuous outputs, such as regression with a
  normal noise model ([9.2 Normal](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)),
  are trained by negative log-likelihood. Small noise means high density and losses
  below 0.
- **Computer science:** generative models that output densities, such as normalizing
  flows, report "bits per dimension" that depend on the scale of the data.
- **Engineering:** tight manufacturing tolerances give tall, narrow densities, like the
  bearings.
- **Everyday life:** any rate (people per km, rain per hour, speed in km per hour) can
  be large without being a contradiction. A density is one more rate.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is spread evenly on $[0, 0.1]$. What is its density? Find
$P(0.02 \le X \le 0.05)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The height must make the area 1: $f(x) = \frac{1}{0.1} = 10$ on $[0, 0.1]$. Then:

$$P(0.02 \le X \le 0.05) = (0.05 - 0.02) \times 10 = 0.03 \times 10 = 0.3$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Which of these are valid densities (each is 0 outside its interval)?
(a) $f(x) = 3$ on $[0, \frac{1}{3}]$. (b) $f(x) = 3$ on $[0, 1]$. (c) $f(x) = 0.5$ on $[0, 1]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Check the area (height times width). (a) $3 \times \frac{1}{3} = 1$, valid, even though the
height is 3. (b) $3 \times 1 = 3$, not valid. (c) $0.5 \times 1 = 0.5$, not valid. Note that
(c) fails even though its height is below 1.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A task takes a time spread evenly between 0 and 3 minutes. Give its
density in "per minute", "per second" and "per hour".

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Per minute the range is 3 wide, so the density is $\frac{1}{3}$. Per second the range is
180 wide, so the density is $\frac{1}{180}$. Per hour the range is $\frac{3}{60} = 0.05$
wide, so the density is:

$$f = \frac{1}{0.05} = 20 \text{ per hour}$$

Same task, three very different numbers.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that $f(x) = 6x(1 - x)$ on $[0, 1]$ is a valid density, and find its
largest value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is never negative on $[0, 1]$. Its area is:

$$\int_0^1 (6x - 6x^2) \thinspace dx = \big[ 3x^2 - 2x^3 \big]_0^1 = 3 - 2 = 1$$

The parabola $6x(1 - x)$ is largest in the middle, at $x = 0.5$, where it equals
$6 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 1.5$. So the density goes above 1.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that if $X$ always lies in an interval of length $L = 0.2$, then its
density is at least 5 somewhere. Give a density where the largest value is exactly 5.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

If $f(x) \lt 5$ at every point of the interval, then the area is less than height times
width:

$$\int f(x) \thinspace dx \lt 5 \times 0.2 = 1$$

This contradicts the total-area rule, so $f(x) \ge 5$ somewhere. The flat density
$f(x) = 5$ on $[0, 0.2]$ has area exactly 1 and largest value exactly 5.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** The middle value (median) of three uniform random numbers has density
$6x(1 - x)$, from Problem 4 (topic
[13.7 Order Statistics](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.7-Order-Statistics.md)
explains why). Estimate the average density on $[0.45, 0.55]$ and compare with the
exact value $\frac{0.1495}{0.1} = 1.495$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
u = rng.random((1_000_000, 3))
m = np.median(u, axis=1)             # density 6x(1 - x)
avg = np.mean((m >= 0.45) & (m <= 0.55)) / 0.1
print(f"average density on [0.45, 0.55]: sim {avg:.3f}, exact 1.495")
```

Output:

```text
average density on [0.45, 0.55]: sim 1.496, exact 1.495
```

The exact value comes from the antiderivative $3x^2 - 2x^3$: the area is
$0.57475 - 0.42525 = 0.1495$, and dividing by the width $0.1$ gives $1.495$.

</details>

## Summary

- A density is a rate: probability per unit length. It is not a probability.
- A density can be larger than 1, and can even grow without bound, as long as the
  total area is 1.
- Areas (probabilities) are always between 0 and 1. Heights are not.
- If $X$ lives on a range shorter than 1, its density must exceed 1 somewhere.
- Changing units changes the density by the same factor that changes the width, so
  probabilities stay the same.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_X(x) \approx \frac{P(x \le X \le x + h)}{h} \quad \text{for small } h}
$$
