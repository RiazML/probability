# 8.5 Expectation and Variance with Integrals

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.5 Expectation and Variance with Integrals

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [8.4 CDF and PDF Relationship](8.4-CDF-and-PDF-Relationship.md)

> **Big idea:** For a continuous variable, the mean is the integral of x times the density, and the variance is the mean squared distance from that mean; sums simply become integrals.

---

## Why It Matters

The mean and the variance are the two numbers you use most to describe a random
variable: where it is centered and how much it spreads. In
[Module 06](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/README.md) you computed them with
sums. Waiting times, measurement errors and prices need the same two numbers, but
there is no list of values to sum over. This topic shows how integrals take the
place of sums, so all the rules you already know keep working.

## Intuition

Cut a shape out of a thin metal sheet, with the shape of a density curve. Where would
it balance on your finger? At its center of mass. That balance point is the
**mean**. A tall part of the curve is heavy and pulls the balance point toward it.

To compute it, cut the shape into thin vertical strips. A strip at position $x$ with
width $dx$ has probability about $f(x) \thinspace dx$. Now pretend $X$ takes only the
strip positions, like a discrete variable. Its mean is "value times probability,
added up": $\sum x \cdot f(x) \thinspace dx$. When the strips get thinner, this sum
becomes an integral. Nothing new is needed, only the step from sum to integral.

## Picture

```text
  f(x)
   |          +--+
   |       +--+  +--+                   each strip: value about x,
   |    +--+  |  |  +--+                probability about f(x) dx
   | +--+  |  |  |  |  +--+
   | |  |  |  |  |  |  |  +--+
   +-+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+---- x
                 ^
                 |
          balance point E[X] = sum of value x probability
                             -> integral of x f(x) dx
```

Chop the density into thin strips, treat each strip like one value of a discrete
variable, and add value times probability.

## Definition

The **expected value** (or **mean**) of a continuous random variable $X$ with
density $f_X$ is:

$$
E[X] = \mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx
$$

where $x$ is a possible value and $f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ is the probability of a thin strip
around it. The mean exists only when $\int \lvert x \rvert f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ is finite.

**In words:** the mean is the sum of "value times probability", over all thin strips.

The **variance** is the expected squared distance from the mean:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2 f_X(x) \thinspace dx = E[X^2] - \mu^2
$$

where $E[X^2] = \int x^2 f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ is the **second moment**, and the
**standard deviation** is $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$.

**In words:** the variance is the average squared distance from the mean, and the
shortcut is "mean of the square minus square of the mean".

The second moment is found with $x^2$ in place of $x$. Why this is allowed is the topic
of [8.6 LOTUS for Continuous](8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md).

| Discrete ([6.1](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.1-Expected-Value.md), [6.5](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)) | Continuous |
|---|---|
| $E[X] = \sum_x x \thinspace p_X(x)$ | $E[X] = \int x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ |
| $E[X^2] = \sum_x x^2 p_X(x)$ | $E[X^2] = \int x^2 f_X(x) \thinspace dx$ |
| $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2$ | $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2$ (the same) |

## Why It Is True

**The mean as a limit of sums.** Cut the line into strips of width $\Delta$, with left
ends $x_k$. Make a discrete variable $X_\Delta$ that equals $x_k$ whenever $X$ falls in strip
$k$. By the strip rule, $P(X_\Delta = x_k) \approx f_X(x_k) \Delta$. So:

$$
E[X_\Delta] = \sum_k x_k \thinspace P(X_\Delta = x_k) \approx \sum_k x_k \thinspace f_X(x_k) \thinspace \Delta \ \xrightarrow{\ \Delta \to 0\ } \ \int x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx
$$

$X_\Delta$ is never more than $\Delta$ away from $X$, so its mean approaches the mean of $X$.
The last step is the definition of the integral as the limit of thin rectangles.

**The variance shortcut.** Expand the square and use that integrals split over sums
and let constants out, exactly like sums do:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= \int (x - \mu)^2 f_X(x) \thinspace dx && \text{(definition)} \\
&= \int x^2 f_X(x) \thinspace dx - 2\mu \int x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx + \mu^2 \int f_X(x) \thinspace dx && \text{(expand the square)} \\
&= E[X^2] - 2\mu \cdot \mu + \mu^2 \cdot 1 && \text{(definitions, total area 1)} \\
&= E[X^2] - \mu^2 && \text{(collect terms)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Because integrals behave like sums, every rule from Module 06 still holds for
continuous variables:
[linearity](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)
$E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$, and
[scaling](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)
$\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Waiting for a Bus

**Problem.** Your wait $X$ for a bus is spread evenly over $[0, 10]$ minutes, so
$f(x) = \frac{1}{10}$ there. Find the mean, variance and standard deviation.

**Solution.**

1. Mean, using the antiderivative $\frac{x^2}{2}$ of $x$:

$$
E[X] = \int_0^{10} x \cdot \frac{1}{10} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{10} \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^{10} = \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{100}{2} = 5
$$

2. Second moment, using the antiderivative $\frac{x^3}{3}$ of $x^2$:

$$
E[X^2] = \int_0^{10} x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{10} \thinspace dx = \frac{1}{10} \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^{10} = \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{1000}{3} = \frac{100}{3}
$$

3. Variance and standard deviation:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{100}{3} - 5^2 = \frac{100}{3} - \frac{75}{3} = \frac{25}{3} \approx 8.333, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\tfrac{25}{3}} \approx 2.887
$$

**Answer.** The mean wait is 5 minutes, the variance is about $8.333$ square minutes,
and the standard deviation is about $2.887$ minutes.

**Check.** The density is flat and symmetric around 5, so the balance point must be 5.
The general formula for a uniform on $[a, b]$, $\frac{(b - a)^2}{12}$
([9.1 Uniform](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.1-Uniform.md)), gives
$\frac{100}{12} = \frac{25}{3}$ too.

### Example 2: A Lopsided Density

**Problem.** $X$ has density $f(x) = 3x^2$ on $[0, 1]$. Find $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\sigma$.

**Solution.**

1. Mean:

$$
E[X] = \int_0^1 x \cdot 3x^2 \thinspace dx = \int_0^1 3x^3 \thinspace dx = \left[ \frac{3x^4}{4} \right]_0^1 = \frac{3}{4}
$$

2. Second moment:

$$
E[X^2] = \int_0^1 x^2 \cdot 3x^2 \thinspace dx = \int_0^1 3x^4 \thinspace dx = \left[ \frac{3x^5}{5} \right]_0^1 = \frac{3}{5}
$$

3. Variance, with a common denominator of 80:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{3}{5} - \left( \frac{3}{4} \right)^2 = \frac{48}{80} - \frac{45}{80} = \frac{3}{80} = 0.0375
$$

4. Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.0375} \approx 0.194$.

**Answer.** $E[X] = 0.75$, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.0375$, and $\sigma \approx 0.194$.

**Check.** The density is heavy near 1, so the mean is pulled to the right of the
middle, 0.5. The variance is smaller than for a flat density on $[0, 1]$, which is
$\frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083$, because the values crowd near 1.

### Example 3: File Sizes With a Heavy Tail

**Problem.** The size $X$ of a file on a server, in MB, has density $f(x) = \frac{2}{x^3}$
for $x \ge 1$. Find the mean. Does the variance exist?

**Solution.**

1. Mean, using the antiderivative $-\frac{2}{x}$ of $\frac{2}{x^2}$:

$$
E[X] = \int_1^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{2}{x^3} \thinspace dx = \int_1^{\infty} \frac{2}{x^2} \thinspace dx = \left[ -\frac{2}{x} \right]_1^{\infty} = 0 + 2 = 2
$$

2. Second moment, using the antiderivative $2 \ln x$ of $\frac{2}{x}$:

$$
E[X^2] = \int_1^{\infty} x^2 \cdot \frac{2}{x^3} \thinspace dx = \int_1^{\infty} \frac{2}{x} \thinspace dx = \big[ 2 \ln x \big]_1^{\infty} = \infty
$$

because $\ln x$ grows without bound.

**Answer.** The mean file size is 2 MB, but $E[X^2]$ is infinite, so the variance is
infinite (it does not exist as a number).

**Check.** A few huge files appear now and then. They are rare enough to keep the mean
finite, but their squares are so large that the average square never settles. The
simulation below shows the sample variance growing as we collect more files. With an
even heavier tail, such as $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$, even the mean is infinite; see
[9.9 Cauchy](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md) for a famous case.

## Simulate It

We check Example 2, then watch the sample variance of the file sizes in Example 3 refuse
to settle down.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Density 3x^2 on [0, 1]: draw it as U^(1/3) (see 8.7).
x = rng.random(n_trials) ** (1 / 3)
print(f"E[X]:   sim {x.mean():.4f}, exact {3 / 4:.4f}")
print(f"Var(X): sim {x.var():.4f}, exact {3 / 80:.4f}")

# File sizes with density 2 / x^3 on [1, inf): mean 2, infinite variance.
for n in [1_000, 100_000, 10_000_000]:
    sizes = 1 / np.sqrt(1 - rng.random(n))
    print(f"n = {n:>10,}: mean {sizes.mean():.3f}, var {sizes.var():.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[X]:   sim 0.7500, exact 0.7500
Var(X): sim 0.0375, exact 0.0375
n =      1,000: mean 2.058, var 5.0
n =    100,000: mean 1.985, var 7.9
n = 10,000,000: mean 2.000, var 13.6
```

The mean and variance of Example 2 match exactly. For the file sizes, the sample mean
approaches 2, but the sample variance keeps growing, as an infinite variance predicts.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the density.** $E[X] = \int x \thinspace dx$ is wrong. Each value must be weighted
  by its density: $\int x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx$.
- **Forgetting to square the mean.** $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - E[X]$ is wrong. The
  shortcut is $E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$.
- **Integrating over the wrong range.** Integrate only over the support. For a density on
  $[0, 1]$, the limits are 0 and 1, not $-\infty$ and $\infty$ with the same formula.
- **Assuming every distribution has a mean and a variance.** Heavy tails can make them
  infinite, as in Example 3. Then averages of data behave badly.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the loss a model minimizes is an expectation over a
  continuous data distribution. Batch normalization estimates the mean and variance of
  each layer's activations.
- **Computer science:** the mean response time of a server decides how many requests
  it can handle; the variance decides how often users see slow pages.
- **Finance:** the mean return of an investment is its expected profit, and the
  standard deviation is its risk.
- **Engineering:** the mean lifetime of a part (with Example 2 of
  [8.4](8.4-CDF-and-PDF-Relationship.md), 4 years) sets the replacement schedule.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is spread evenly on $[2, 6]$, with density $\frac{1}{4}$. Find $E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = \int_2^6 x \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} \thinspace dx = \tfrac{1}{4} \left[ \tfrac{x^2}{2} \right]_2^6 = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \tfrac{36 - 4}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 16 = 4$$

This is the midpoint of $[2, 6]$, as symmetry predicts.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ has $E[X] = 3$ and $E[X^2] = 13$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\sigma$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 13 - 3^2 = 4, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{4} = 2$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X$ has density $f(x) = 2x$ on $[0, 1]$. Find $E[X]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = \int_0^1 2x^2 \thinspace dx = \tfrac{2}{3}$$

$$E[X^2] = \int_0^1 2x^3 \thinspace dx = \tfrac{2}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{4}{9} = \tfrac{9}{18} - \tfrac{8}{18} = \tfrac{1}{18} \approx 0.0556$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A temperature $C$ in degrees Celsius has mean 25 and variance 4. The
temperature in Fahrenheit is $F = 1.8C + 32$. Find its mean and variance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use linearity and the scaling rule, which hold for continuous variables too:

$$E[F] = 1.8 \times 25 + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(F) = 1.8^2 \times 4 = 3.24 \times 4 = 12.96$$

The shift by 32 moves the mean but does not change the spread.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Show that the density $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ for $x \ge 1$ has an infinite mean.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = \int_1^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{1}{x^2} \thinspace dx = \int_1^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \thinspace dx = \big[ \ln x \big]_1^{\infty} = \infty$$

The tail $\frac{1}{x^2}$ is too heavy: big values are rare, but not rare enough to keep
"value times probability" finite.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 3 by simulation. One way to draw from $f(x) = 2x$ on
$[0, 1]$ is $X = \sqrt{U}$ for a uniform $U$ (see [8.8](8.8-Change-of-Variables.md)).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = np.sqrt(rng.random(1_000_000))   # density 2x on [0, 1]
print(f"E[X]:   sim {x.mean():.4f}, exact {2 / 3:.4f}")
print(f"Var(X): sim {x.var():.4f}, exact {1 / 18:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[X]:   sim 0.6667, exact 0.6667
Var(X): sim 0.0555, exact 0.0556
```

</details>

## Summary

- The mean of a continuous variable is $\int x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx$: value times
  probability, with an integral in place of a sum.
- The variance is $\int (x - \mu)^2 f_X(x) \thinspace dx$, and the shortcut
  $E[X^2] - \mu^2$ still works.
- Linearity and the scaling rule for variance hold exactly as in Module 06.
- Integrate only over the support of the density.
- Heavy tails can make the variance, or even the mean, infinite.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \thinspace f_X(x) \thinspace dx, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2}
$$
