# 8.7 Quantile Function

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 08: Continuous Random Variables](README.md) / 8.7 Quantile Function

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [8.6 LOTUS for Continuous](8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md)

> **Big idea:** The quantile function runs the CDF backward: Q(u) is the value that has a fraction u of the probability below it.

---

## Why It Matters

"Your baby is at the 90th percentile for weight." "99% of requests finish within
250 ms." "The top 10% of students get an A." Each of these sentences is about a
**quantile**: a value chosen so that a given fraction of the probability lies below
it. The quantile function also turns uniform random numbers into any distribution
you like, which is how computers simulate. The discrete version is in
[6.8 Median, Mode and Quantiles](../06-EXPECTATION-VARIANCE-AND-MOMENTS/6.8-Median-Mode-and-Quantiles.md).

## Intuition

The CDF answers the question: "Given a value $x$, what fraction of the probability is
below it?" For example, a web server's CDF might say: 90% of requests take at most
230 ms.

Very often we want to ask the question the other way around: "Given a fraction, say
90%, which value has that fraction below it?" The answer, 230 ms, is the **90th
percentile**. Engineers call it "p90". Going from the fraction back to the value is
running the CDF backward. On a graph of the CDF, you start on the vertical axis at
height 0.9, walk right until you hit the curve, and walk down to read the value.

## Picture

```text
  F(x)
  1 +- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
    |                                 __....-------''''
    |                          __.--''
  u +- - - - - - - - - - - - -*'          1. start at height u
    |                       .':           2. go right to the curve
    |                    .-'  :           3. go down: you land on Q(u)
    |                _.-'     :
    |         __..--'         :
  0 +---..--''----------------+---------------------------> x
                             Q(u)
```

The CDF turns a value into a fraction; the quantile function turns a fraction $u$
back into a value $Q(u)$.

## Definition

Let $X$ be continuous with CDF $F_X$, and suppose $F_X$ is strictly increasing on the
support. For each fraction $0 \lt u \lt 1$, the **quantile function** is the inverse of
the CDF:

$$
Q_X(u) = F_X^{-1}(u), \qquad \text{the one value } x \text{ with } F_X(x) = u
$$

where $u$ is a probability and $Q_X(u)$ is a value of $X$.

**In words:** $Q_X(u)$ is the value that has exactly a fraction $u$ of the probability
below it, so $P(X \le Q_X(u)) = u$.

When the CDF has flat parts (or jumps, as in [8.9](8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md)), many
values or no value may solve $F_X(x) = u$. The general definition takes the
**smallest** value that reaches $u$:

$$
Q_X(u) = \min \lbrace x : F_X(x) \ge u \rbrace
$$

**In words:** walk right along the $x$-axis and stop at the first point where the CDF
reaches $u$.

Some quantiles have their own names:

| Name | Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **median** | $Q(0.5)$ | half the probability below, half above |
| **quartiles** | $Q(0.25)$, $Q(0.5)$, $Q(0.75)$ | cut the probability into four equal parts |
| **p-th percentile** | $Q(p/100)$ | $p$ percent below; p99 is $Q(0.99)$ |
| **interquartile range** (**IQR**) | $Q(0.75) - Q(0.25)$ | width of the middle half; a measure of spread |

## Why It Is True

**The quantile undoes the CDF.** Because $F_X$ is strictly increasing, each height $u$
is reached at exactly one point, and that point is $Q_X(u)$. So
$F_X(Q_X(u)) = u$, which means $P(X \le Q_X(u)) = u$.

**Turning uniform numbers into any distribution.** Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$, so
$P(U \le u) = u$. Then $Q_X(U)$ has exactly the distribution of $X$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Q_X(U) \le x) &= P(U \le F_X(x)) && \text{(apply the increasing } F_X \text{ to both sides)} \\
&= F_X(x) && \text{(}U \text{ is uniform on } [0, 1] \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $Q_X(U)$ has CDF $F_X$. This is **inverse transform sampling**, the standard way to
simulate a distribution from uniform random numbers
([19.4 Inverse Transform Sampling](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.4-Inverse-Transform-Sampling.md)).
It is the reason $U^{1/3}$ has density $3x^2$, as used in earlier topics.

**Quantiles move with increasing functions.** If $g$ is strictly increasing, then
$Q_{g(X)}(u) = g(Q_X(u))$. Here is why:

$$
P\big( g(X) \le g(Q_X(u)) \big) = P\big( X \le Q_X(u) \big) = u
$$

The first step holds because $g$ keeps the order of numbers. So the median of $g(X)$ is
$g$ of the median of $X$. Means do **not** behave like this
([8.6 LOTUS for Continuous](8.6-LOTUS-for-Continuous.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Median and Quartiles

**Problem.** $X$ has density $3x^2$ on $[0, 1]$, so its CDF is $F(x) = x^3$ there (from
[8.4](8.4-CDF-and-PDF-Relationship.md)). Find the quantile function, the median, the
quartiles and the IQR. Compare the median with the mean $0.75$.

**Solution.**

1. Solve $F(x) = u$ for $x$: $x^3 = u$, so $Q(u) = u^{1/3}$ for $0 \lt u \lt 1$.
2. Median: $Q(0.5) = 0.5^{1/3} \approx 0.7937$.
3. Quartiles: $Q(0.25) = 0.25^{1/3} \approx 0.6300$ and $Q(0.75) = 0.75^{1/3} \approx 0.9086$.
4. Interquartile range:

$$
\text{IQR} = Q(0.75) - Q(0.25) \approx 0.9086 - 0.6300 = 0.2786
$$

**Answer.** $Q(u) = u^{1/3}$. The median is about $0.794$, the quartiles are about $0.630$
and $0.909$, and the IQR is about $0.279$.

**Check.** $F(0.7937) = 0.7937^3 \approx 0.5$, as it should be. The median $0.794$ is
**above** the mean $0.75$: the density has a long thin tail to the left, and the few
small values pull the mean down more than the median.

### Example 2: Latency Percentiles

**Problem.** A web server's response time $X$, in ms, has CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-x/100}$ for
$x \ge 0$. Its mean is 100 ms (an exponential distribution,
[9.3 Exponential](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)). Find the
quantile function, the median, p90 and p99.

**Solution.**

1. Solve $1 - e^{-x/100} = u$ for $x$:

$$
e^{-x/100} = 1 - u \quad \Rightarrow \quad -\frac{x}{100} = \ln(1 - u) \quad \Rightarrow \quad Q(u) = -100 \ln(1 - u)
$$

2. Median: $Q(0.5) = -100 \ln 0.5 = 100 \ln 2 \approx 69.31$ ms.
3. p90: $Q(0.9) = -100 \ln 0.1 = 100 \ln 10 \approx 230.26$ ms.
4. p99: $Q(0.99) = -100 \ln 0.01 = 100 \ln 100 \approx 460.52$ ms.

**Answer.** $Q(u) = -100 \ln(1 - u)$. The median is about 69 ms, p90 about 230 ms, and
p99 about 461 ms.

**Check.** Half of all requests finish in under 69 ms, faster than the mean of 100 ms,
because a few very slow requests pull the mean up. The slowest 1% take more than
4.6 times the mean. This is why engineers watch p99, not only the mean.

### Example 3: A CDF With a Flat Part

**Problem.** A request arrives at a random time in a 3-hour window, but the server is
offline from hour 1 to hour 2. So the arrival time $X$ is spread evenly over
$[0, 1] \cup [2, 3]$, with density $\frac{1}{2}$ on each piece. Find the median and $Q(0.75)$.

**Solution.**

1. The CDF is $F(x) = \frac{x}{2}$ on $[0, 1]$, stays at $\frac{1}{2}$ on $[1, 2]$, and is
   $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{x - 2}{2}$ on $[2, 3]$.
2. Every $x$ in $[1, 2]$ has $F(x) = \frac{1}{2}$, so "the" value with $F(x) = 0.5$ is not
   unique. The general definition takes the smallest one:
   $Q(0.5) = \min \lbrace x : F(x) \ge 0.5 \rbrace = 1$.
3. For $u = 0.75$, solve on the last piece: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{x - 2}{2} = 0.75$, so
   $x - 2 = 0.5$ and $x = 2.5$.

**Answer.** The median is $Q(0.5) = 1$ hour (by the "smallest value" rule) and
$Q(0.75) = 2.5$ hours.

**Check.** Half the arrivals happen in $[0, 1]$ and half in $[2, 3]$, so any time in the
offline gap splits them in half. Software such as numpy may report a different point
in $[1, 2]$, depending on its rule; all of them are valid medians.

## Simulate It

We use inverse transform sampling for the server in Example 2, then compare the sample
percentiles with the exact quantiles.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Inverse transform: plug uniform numbers into Q(u) = -100 ln(1 - u).
u = rng.random(n_trials)
latency = -100 * np.log(1 - u)

print(f"mean    : sim {latency.mean():7.2f}, exact {100:7.2f}")
for p in [0.5, 0.9, 0.99]:
    sim = np.quantile(latency, p)
    exact = -100 * np.log(1 - p)
    label = f"Q({p})"
    print(f"{label:<8}: sim {sim:7.2f}, exact {exact:7.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
mean    : sim  100.02, exact  100.00
Q(0.5)  : sim   69.28, exact   69.31
Q(0.9)  : sim  230.43, exact  230.26
Q(0.99) : sim  461.49, exact  460.52
```

The samples made from uniform numbers have the right mean and the right percentiles,
so $Q(U)$ really has the latency distribution.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up percentile and percentage.** "p90 = 230 ms" means 90% of requests take
  at most 230 ms. The percentile is a **value** of $X$ (230 ms), not a probability.
- **Mixing up $Q$ and $F$.** $F$ takes a value and returns a probability. $Q$ takes a
  probability and returns a value. Check the units of your answer.
- **Using the mean as the "typical" value of a skewed variable.** In Example 2 the mean
  (100 ms) is much larger than the median (69 ms). For skewed data, report the median.
- **Assuming $F(x) = u$ has exactly one solution.** With flat parts or jumps in the CDF,
  use $Q(u) = \min \lbrace x : F(x) \ge u \rbrace$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** quantile regression predicts the 10th and 90th
  percentiles of a target, not only its mean. Conformal prediction builds prediction
  intervals from quantiles of past errors.
- **Computer science:** service level objectives are written as p99 or p99.9 latency.
  Inverse transform sampling generates random numbers from many distributions.
- **Finance:** **value at risk** is a quantile of the loss distribution, such as "the
  loss we exceed only 1% of the time".
- **Medicine and everyday life:** growth charts, exam percentile ranks and income
  percentiles are all quantiles.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is spread evenly on $[0, 20]$, so $F(x) = \frac{x}{20}$ there. Find the
quantile function, the median and the 90th percentile.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $\frac{x}{20} = u$: $Q(u) = 20u$. So the median is $Q(0.5) = 10$ and the 90th
percentile is $Q(0.9) = 18$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ has CDF $F(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$. Find $Q(u)$ and the median.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $x^2 = u$ with $x \ge 0$: $Q(u) = \sqrt{u}$. The median is $Q(0.5) = \sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.7071$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The wait at a call center, in minutes, has CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-x/2}$ for
$x \ge 0$. Which wait is exceeded by only 5% of callers?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We want the 95th percentile. Solve $1 - e^{-x/2} = 0.95$:

$$e^{-x/2} = 0.05 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = -2 \ln 0.05 = 2 \ln 20 \approx 5.99 \text{ minutes}$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $X$ has density $\frac{1}{x^2}$ for $x \ge 1$, with CDF $F(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{x}$.
Its mean is infinite ([8.5](8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md), Problem 5).
Find its median and its 99th percentile.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Solve $1 - \frac{1}{x} = u$: $Q(u) = \frac{1}{1 - u}$. So:

$$Q(0.5) = \tfrac{1}{0.5} = 2, \qquad Q(0.99) = \tfrac{1}{0.01} = 100$$

The median exists and equals 2, even though the mean is infinite. Quantiles always exist.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that if $g$ is strictly increasing, the median of $g(X)$ is $g$ of the
median of $X$. Then use it: the natural log of a household's income has median 10. What
is the median income?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $m$ be the median of $X$, so $P(X \le m) = 0.5$. Because $g$ is strictly increasing,
$g(X) \le g(m)$ happens exactly when $X \le m$:

$$P(g(X) \le g(m)) = P(X \le m) = 0.5$$

So $g(m)$ is the median of $g(X)$. Income is $e^{\ln(\text{income})}$, and $e^x$ is increasing, so
the median income is $e^{10} \approx 22026$ (in the same units). The mean income is
**not** $e^{10}$ in general.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Use inverse transform sampling to draw from Problem 4 and check the
median and the 99th percentile.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = 1 / (1 - rng.random(4_000_000))     # Q(U) = 1 / (1 - U)
print(f"median: sim {np.quantile(x, 0.5):.3f}, exact 2")
print(f"p99:    sim {np.quantile(x, 0.99):.3f}, exact 100")
```

Output:

```text
median: sim 1.999, exact 2
p99:    sim 100.553, exact 100
```

</details>

## Summary

- The quantile function $Q(u)$ is the inverse of the CDF: it turns a fraction into a value.
- $P(X \le Q(u)) = u$. The median is $Q(0.5)$, and p99 is $Q(0.99)$.
- With flat parts or jumps in the CDF, use $Q(u) = \min \lbrace x : F(x) \ge u \rbrace$.
- If $U$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$, then $Q(U)$ has the distribution of $X$. This is how
  computers simulate.
- Quantiles move with increasing functions, and they exist even when the mean does not.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{Q_X(u) = F_X^{-1}(u), \qquad P(X \le Q_X(u)) = u}
$$
