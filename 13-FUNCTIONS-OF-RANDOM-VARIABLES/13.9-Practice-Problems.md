# 13.9 Practice Problems

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.9 Practice Problems

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 3 hours · **Before this:** [13.8 Box-Muller Transform](13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md)

> **Big idea:** Finding the distribution of a function of random variables always comes down to one question, "which inputs give this output?", and practice teaches you which tool answers it fastest.

---

## How to Use This Set

- Each problem says which topic it uses. If you get stuck, reread that topic first.
- Try every problem on paper before opening the answer. Write each step, as in the
  worked examples.
- **Warm-Up** problems check the basic formulas. **Core** problems mix two or three ideas.
  **Challenge** problems include proofs. **Simulation Projects** ask you to write code.
- Before you compute, choose the tool: the **CDF method** (always works), the
  **change-of-variables** formula (for one-to-one maps), **convolution** or the **table of
  known sums** (for sums), the **product rule** (for max and min), or the **order statistic**
  formula (for sorted values).
- Always state the support of the answer, and check that a density integrates to 1.

## Warm-Up

**W1. (Easy)** *Uses [13.1 CDF Method](13.1-CDF-Method.md).*
A call lasts $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ minutes (rate $\lambda$, mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$). Show that its length in
seconds, $Y = 60X$, is exponential, and find its rate. For $\lambda = 2$, what is the mean of $Y$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \ge 0$, $Y \le y$ means $X \le \frac{y}{60}$:

$$F_Y(y) = P\left(X \le \tfrac{y}{60}\right) = 1 - e^{-\lambda y / 60}$$

So $Y \sim \text{Exp}(\frac{\lambda}{60})$. With $\lambda = 2$ per minute the rate is $\frac{2}{60} = \frac{1}{30}$ per second, and the mean is 30 seconds, which is half a minute, as it should be.

</details>

**W2. (Easy)** *Uses [13.2 Change of Variables 1D](13.2-Change-of-Variables-1D.md).*
$Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find the density of $Y = e^Z$ (the **log-normal** distribution).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$g(z) = e^z$ is increasing, from $\mathbb{R}$ onto $(0, \infty)$, with one root $z = \ln y$ and $g'(z) = e^z = y$:

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{\varphi(\ln y)}{y} = \frac{1}{y\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(\ln y)^2/2}, \qquad y \gt 0$$

</details>

**W3. (Easy)** *Uses [13.5 Sums of Known Distributions](13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md).*
Work emails arrive as a Poisson stream at 2 per hour and personal emails at 3 per hour,
independently. What is the chance of at least one email in the next 30 minutes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The merged stream has rate $2 + 3 = 5$ per hour, so the count in half an hour is $\text{Pois}(2.5)$:

$$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - e^{-2.5} \approx 1 - 0.0821 = 0.9179$$

</details>

**W4. (Easy)** *Uses [13.6 Max and Min](13.6-Max-and-Min.md).*
Three numbers are independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find $P(\min \gt 0.5)$ and $P(\max \lt 0.5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Both events need all three numbers on one side of $0.5$:

$$P(\min \gt 0.5) = 0.5^3 = 0.125, \qquad P(\max \lt 0.5) = 0.5^3 = 0.125$$

</details>

**W5. (Easy)** *Uses [13.7 Order Statistics](13.7-Order-Statistics.md).*
Nine numbers are independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Give the distribution and the mean of the median
and of the third smallest.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The median is $U_{(5)} \sim \text{Beta}(5, 5)$, with mean $\frac{5}{10} = 0.5$. The third smallest is $U_{(3)} \sim \text{Beta}(3, 7)$, with mean $\frac{3}{10} = 0.3$.

</details>

## Core

**C1. (Medium)** *Uses [13.4 Convolution](13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md).*
$X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ are independent. Find the density of $Z = X + Y$, check that
it integrates to 1, and find $P(Z \gt 2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$f_Z(z) = \int f_X(x) f_Y(z - x) \thinspace dx$ needs $0 \le x \le 1$ and $z - x \ge 0$. For $0 \le z \le 1$, $x$ runs over $[0, z]$; for $z \gt 1$, over $[0, 1]$:

$$f_Z(z) = \int_0^z e^{-(z - x)} \thinspace dx = 1 - e^{-z} \ (0 \le z \le 1), \qquad f_Z(z) = \int_0^1 e^{-(z - x)} \thinspace dx = (e - 1) e^{-z} \ (z \gt 1)$$

Areas: $\int_0^1 (1 - e^{-z}) dz = e^{-1}$ and $\int_1^{\infty} (e - 1)e^{-z} dz = (e - 1)e^{-1} = 1 - e^{-1}$. Total 1. Finally:

$$P(Z \gt 2) = (e - 1) e^{-2} \approx 1.71828 \times 0.13534 \approx 0.2325$$

</details>

**C2. (Medium)** *Uses [13.6 Max and Min](13.6-Max-and-Min.md).*
Show that the minimum of $n$ independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ variables is $\text{Exp}(n\lambda)$. A sign has 5 bulbs,
each with an exponential lifetime of mean 1000 hours. Find the mean time until the first bulb
fails, and the chance that no bulb fails in the first 100 hours.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(\min \gt t) = (e^{-\lambda t})^n = e^{-n\lambda t}$, the survival function of $\text{Exp}(n\lambda)$. Here $\lambda = \frac{1}{1000}$ per hour and $n = 5$, so the minimum is $\text{Exp}(0.005)$:

$$E[\min] = \frac{1}{0.005} = 200 \text{ hours}, \qquad P(\min \gt 100) = e^{-0.005 \times 100} = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065$$

</details>

**C3. (Medium)** *Uses [13.3 Jacobian](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md).*
$X$ and $Y$ are independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Let $U = X + Y$ and $V = X - Y$. Find the joint density of
$(U, V)$. Show that $U$ and $V$ are uncorrelated but **not** independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The inverse is $x = \frac{u + v}{2}$, $y = \frac{u - v}{2}$ with $\lvert \det J_h \rvert = \frac{1}{2}$, as in Example 1 of 13.3. So $f_{U,V} = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$ on the image of the square: the diamond with corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(2, 0)$, $(1, -1)$, where $\lvert v \rvert \le u$ and $\lvert v \rvert \le 2 - u$.

Uncorrelated: $\operatorname{Cov}(X + Y, X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) - \operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0$.

Not independent: $P(U \lt 0.2) = \frac{0.2^2}{2} = 0.02 \gt 0$ and $P(V \gt 0.5) = \frac{0.5^2}{2} = 0.125 \gt 0$, but $P(U \lt 0.2, V \gt 0.5) = 0$, because $\lvert V \rvert \le U$. For independence this would have to be $0.02 \times 0.125$. The support is a diamond, not a rectangle.

</details>

**C4. (Medium)** *Uses [13.2](13.2-Change-of-Variables-1D.md).*
$X \sim \mathcal{N}(1, 1)$. Find the density of $Y = X^2$ and evaluate it at $y = 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The roots of $x^2 = y$ are $\pm\sqrt{y}$, with $\lvert g' \rvert = 2\sqrt{y}$. The density of $X$ is $\varphi(x - 1)$:

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{\varphi(\sqrt{y} - 1) + \varphi(-\sqrt{y} - 1)}{2\sqrt{y}}, \qquad y \gt 0$$

At $y = 1$: $f_Y(1) = \frac{\varphi(0) + \varphi(-2)}{2} \approx \frac{0.39894 + 0.05399}{2} \approx 0.2265$. The two roots now count differently, because $X$ is not centered at 0.

</details>

**C5. (Medium)** *Uses [13.7 Order Statistics](13.7-Order-Statistics.md).*
(a) For 4 independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ numbers, find the mean gap $E[U_{(3)} - U_{(2)}]$. (b) Three machines
have independent $\text{Exp}(1)$ lifetimes. Find the chance that the second failure (the median) comes
after time 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(a) $E[U_{(k)}] = \frac{k}{5}$, so $E[U_{(3)} - U_{(2)}] = \frac{3}{5} - \frac{2}{5} = \frac{1}{5}$: every gap has the same mean.

(b) $X_{(2)} \gt 1$ means at most 1 of the 3 has failed by time 1. Each has failed with probability $p = 1 - e^{-1}$:

$$P(X_{(2)} \gt 1) = (1 - p)^3 + 3p(1 - p)^2 = e^{-3} + 3(1 - e^{-1}) e^{-2} = 3e^{-2} - 2e^{-3} \approx 0.3064$$

</details>

**C6. (Medium)** *Uses [13.4](13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md) and [13.5](13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md).*
$X \sim \text{Pois}(2)$ and $Y \sim \text{Bin}(2, 0.5)$ are independent. The families differ, so no table row
applies. Find $P(X + Y = 0)$ and $P(X + Y = 1)$ by convolution.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(X = 0) = e^{-2}$, $P(X = 1) = 2e^{-2}$, and $P(Y = 0) = 0.25$, $P(Y = 1) = 0.5$:

$$P(X + Y = 0) = e^{-2} \times 0.25 \approx 0.0338$$

$$P(X + Y = 1) = P(X = 0)P(Y = 1) + P(X = 1)P(Y = 0) = 0.5e^{-2} + 0.5e^{-2} = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$$

</details>

## Challenge

**H1. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [13.3 Jacobian](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md).*
$X$ and $Y$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Rotate the point by an angle $\alpha$:
$U = X \cos \alpha - Y \sin \alpha$ and $V = X \sin \alpha + Y \cos \alpha$. Prove that $U$ and $V$ are again independent
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The map is linear with rows $(\cos \alpha, -\sin \alpha)$ and $(\sin \alpha, \cos \alpha)$, so:

$$\det J_g = \cos^2 \alpha + \sin^2 \alpha = 1$$

A rotation keeps distances from the origin, so $u^2 + v^2 = x^2 + y^2$ at matching points. (Check: $(x\cos\alpha - y\sin\alpha)^2 + (x\sin\alpha + y\cos\alpha)^2 = x^2 + y^2$, since the cross terms $\mp 2xy \sin\alpha\cos\alpha$ cancel.) Therefore:

$$f_{U,V}(u, v) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{1} = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2} = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(u^2 + v^2)/2}$$

This is the product of two $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ densities. The 2-D standard normal looks the same after any rotation; that is the reason its angle is uniform in Box-Muller.

</details>

**H2. (Hard, proof)** *Uses [13.7 Order Statistics](13.7-Order-Statistics.md).*
Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. continuous with median $m$, so $F(m) = \frac{1}{2}$. Prove that
$P(X_{(1)} \lt m \lt X_{(n)}) = 1 - 2 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^n$, whatever $F$ is. For $n = 10$, find this value and
also $P(X_{(2)} \lt m \lt X_{(9)})$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The interval misses $m$ only if all values are above $m$ or all are below $m$. These two events are disjoint, and each value is below $m$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, independently:

$$P(X_{(1)} \lt m \lt X_{(n)}) = 1 - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n = 1 - 2 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n$$

For $n = 10$ this is $1 - \frac{2}{1024} \approx 0.9980$. For the narrower interval, let $N \sim \text{Bin}(10, \frac{1}{2})$ count the values below $m$. It misses $m$ when $N \le 1$ (then $X_{(2)} \gt m$) or $N \ge 9$ (then $X_{(9)} \lt m$):

$$P(X_{(2)} \lt m \lt X_{(9)}) = 1 - \frac{1 + 10}{1024} - \frac{10 + 1}{1024} = 1 - \frac{22}{1024} \approx 0.9785$$

This is a **distribution-free** confidence interval for the median.

</details>

**H3. (Hard)** *Uses [13.5](13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md), [13.6](13.6-Max-and-Min.md) and [13.2](13.2-Change-of-Variables-1D.md).*
$Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Show that $E[\max(Z_1, Z_2)] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}$. (Hint: $\max(a, b) = \frac{a + b}{2} + \frac{\lvert a - b \rvert}{2}$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the hint and linearity, $E[\max] = \frac{1}{2} E[Z_1 + Z_2] + \frac{1}{2} E\lvert Z_1 - Z_2 \rvert = 0 + \frac{1}{2} E\lvert Z_1 - Z_2 \rvert$. The difference is $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$ (13.5), so $Z_1 - Z_2 = \sqrt{2} W$ with $W \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The half-normal density $2\varphi(w)$ of $\lvert W \rvert$ (13.2, Practice 1) gives:

$$E\lvert W \rvert = \int_0^{\infty} w \cdot \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-w^2/2} \thinspace dw = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \left[ -e^{-w^2/2} \right]_0^{\infty} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}$$

So:

$$E[\max(Z_1, Z_2)] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \approx 0.5642$$

</details>

## Simulation Projects

**S1. (Project)** *Uses [13.4](13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md) and [13.8](13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md).*
An old trick makes "normal" numbers as $Z = U_1 + \dots + U_{12} - 6$ (mean 0, variance $12 \times \frac{1}{12} = 1$). Its
exact distribution is the 12-fold convolution of uniforms, whose CDF is
$F(x) = \frac{1}{12!} \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x \rfloor} (-1)^k \binom{12}{k} (x - k)^{12}$ (the **Irwin-Hall** distribution). Compare the
tails $P(\lvert Z \rvert \gt t)$ for $t = 1, 2, 3$ from simulation, from the exact formula and from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and
compare $E[Z^4]$ with 3. Why is Box-Muller better?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, n = 1_000_000, 12

def irwin_hall_cdf(x, n):
    # Exact CDF of a sum of n uniforms (repeated convolution).
    return sum((-1)**k * math.comb(n, k) * (x - k)**n
               for k in range(math.floor(x) + 1)) / math.factorial(n)

z = rng.random((n_trials, n)).sum(axis=1) - 6    # mean 0, variance 12/12 = 1
for t in [1, 2, 3]:
    exact = 2 * (1 - irwin_hall_cdf(6 + t, n))
    print(f"P(|Z| > {t}): sim {np.mean(np.abs(z) > t):.5f}, "
          f"exact {exact:.5f}, normal {2 * stats.norm.sf(t):.5f}")
print(f"E[Z^4]: sim {np.mean(z**4):.4f}, exact {3 - 6 / (5 * n):.4f}, normal 3")
```

Output:

```text
P(|Z| > 1): sim 0.32167, exact 0.32145, normal 0.31731
P(|Z| > 2): sim 0.04476, exact 0.04455, normal 0.04550
P(|Z| > 3): sim 0.00198, exact 0.00201, normal 0.00270
E[Z^4]: sim 2.9036, exact 2.9000, normal 3
```

The sum of 12 uniforms is close to normal in the middle, but its tails are too thin: beyond 3 it has about 25% fewer values than a normal, and it can never go beyond $\pm 6$. Its fourth moment is $2.9$, not 3. Box-Muller is **exactly** normal, with the right tails, and it uses one uniform per normal instead of twelve.

</details>

**S2. (Project)** *Uses [13.7 Order Statistics](13.7-Order-Statistics.md) and [13.6](13.6-Max-and-Min.md).*
A team estimates the p99 latency from $n = 1000$ response times by taking the 990-th smallest,
$X_{(990)}$. Suppose the times are $\text{Exp}(1)$, so the true 99th percentile is $q = \ln 100 \approx 4.605$.
Simulate the estimate many times. Compare its mean with the exact value
$E[X_{(990)}] = \frac{1}{1000} + \frac{1}{999} + \dots + \frac{1}{11}$ (the gaps of 13.6, Example 3), and find how often it
**underestimates** $q$; compare with the binomial answer $P(\text{at least 990 values} \le q)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, k, n_trials = 1_000, 990, 20_000
q = np.log(100)                        # true 99th percentile of Exp(1)

# The 990-th smallest of 1000 response times estimates the 99th percentile.
est = np.array([np.partition(rng.exponential(1.0, n), k - 1)[k - 1]
                for _ in range(n_trials)])
exact_mean = sum(1 / i for i in range(n - k + 1, n + 1))   # gaps 1/1000 + ... + 1/11
print(f"E[X_(990)]:     sim {est.mean():.4f}, exact {exact_mean:.4f}, true q {q:.4f}")
print(f"P(X_(990) < q): sim {np.mean(est < q):.4f}, "
      f"exact {stats.binom.sf(k - 1, n, 0.99):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[X_(990)]:     sim 4.5542, exact 4.5565, true q 4.6052
P(X_(990) < q): sim 0.5843, exact 0.5830
```

The estimate is a little too low on average ($4.557$ versus $4.605$) and underestimates the true p99 about 58% of the time. With only 10 values above it, the tail is poorly measured; a p99 from 1000 requests is a noisy number.

</details>
