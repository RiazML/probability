# 13.8 Box-Muller Transform

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.8 Box-Muller Transform

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [13.7 Order Statistics](13.7-Order-Statistics.md)

> **Big idea:** Two independent uniform numbers become two independent standard normals by using one as a random angle and the other to build a random distance, $R = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1}$, because a normal pair in polar coordinates is exactly "uniform angle, exponential squared distance".

---

## Why It Matters

Computers make uniform random numbers, but science, finance and machine learning need
**normal** random numbers: weight initialization in neural networks, noise in diffusion
models, measurement errors in simulations, stock price paths. The inverse transform trick
([8.7 Quantile Function](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.7-Quantile-Function.md)) needs
the inverse of the normal CDF $\Phi$, which has no simple formula. The **Box-Muller
transform** avoids it. It needs only a logarithm, a square root, a cosine and a sine, and it
is exact. It is also the best example of the Jacobian rule of
[13.3](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md) at work.

## Intuition

Think backwards first. Take a pair $(Z_1, Z_2)$ of independent standard normals and plot it
as a point in the plane. Example 2 of [13.3](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)
showed two facts about this point:

- Its **angle** is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$: the bell-shaped cloud looks the same in every direction.
- Its **squared distance** $R^2 = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2$ is exponential with rate $\frac{1}{2}$ (mean 2),
  independent of the angle.

Both of these are easy to make from uniforms. An angle is $2\pi U_2$. An exponential with
mean 2 is $-2 \ln U_1$ (the inverse transform). So: draw an angle, draw a squared
distance, and convert from polar to ordinary coordinates. For example, $U_1 = 0.5$ gives
$R^2 = -2 \ln 0.5 \approx 1.386$, so $R \approx 1.177$; and $U_2 = 0.25$ gives the angle $\frac{\pi}{2}$, straight
up. The point is $(0, 1.177)$: two normal numbers, $Z_1 = 0$ and $Z_2 \approx 1.177$.

## Picture

```text
                                                  Z2
   U1 ~ Unif(0,1) --> R = sqrt(-2 ln U1)           |       * (Z1, Z2)
                      (distance from 0)            |      /
                                                   |   R /
   U2 ~ Unif(0,1) --> Θ = 2π U2                    |    /
                      (angle, uniform)             |   /  Θ
                                                   |  / )
                                                   | /  )
                        Z1 = R cos Θ        -------+----------------- Z1
                        Z2 = R sin Θ               |
                                                   |
   result: Z1, Z2 independent N(0, 1)
```

One uniform picks how far from the center, the other picks the direction; together they
place a point of the 2-D standard normal cloud.

## Definition

The **Box-Muller transform** takes two independent $U_1, U_2 \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and returns:

$$
Z_1 = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1} \thinspace \cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1} \thinspace \sin(2\pi U_2)
$$

Then $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are **independent**, each $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

where:

- $R = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1}$ is the distance from the origin; $-2 \ln U_1$ is positive because $\ln U_1 \lt 0$,
- $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$ is the angle, in radians, uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$,
- $(Z_1, Z_2) = (R \cos \Theta, R \sin \Theta)$ is the usual change from polar to ordinary coordinates.

**In words:** turn one uniform into a distance with $\sqrt{-2 \ln U_1}$, turn the other into an angle
with $2\pi U_2$, and read off the two coordinates of the point. Both coordinates are standard
normal, and they are independent.

To get $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, use $X = \mu + \sigma Z_1$.

## Why It Is True

**Step 1: the distance.** Let $D = R^2 = -2 \ln U_1$. For $t \gt 0$, use the CDF method of
[13.1](13.1-CDF-Method.md):

$$
P(D \gt t) = P(-2 \ln U_1 \gt t) = P(U_1 \lt e^{-t/2}) = e^{-t/2}
$$

So $D \sim \text{Exp}(\frac{1}{2})$. For $R = \sqrt{D}$: $P(R \le r) = P(D \le r^2) = 1 - e^{-r^2/2}$, and the slope
gives the density $f_R(r) = r e^{-r^2/2}$ for $r \gt 0$.

**Step 2: the angle.** $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$, with density $\frac{1}{2\pi}$. It is a
function of $U_2$ only, so it is independent of $R$. The joint density is the product:

$$
f_{R, \Theta}(r, \theta) = r e^{-r^2/2} \cdot \frac{1}{2\pi}, \qquad r \gt 0, \ 0 \le \theta \lt 2\pi
$$

**Step 3: polar to ordinary coordinates.** The map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (z_1, z_2) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)$ is
one-to-one here and stretches area by $\lvert \det J \rvert = r$
([13.3](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)). So we **divide** by $r$, and use
$r^2 = z_1^2 + z_2^2$:

$$
f_{Z_1, Z_2}(z_1, z_2) = \frac{r e^{-r^2/2} / (2\pi)}{r} = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z_1^2/2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z_2^2/2}
$$

The joint density is the product of two $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ densities, so $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent
standard normals ([10.5](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)).
The factor $r$ from the distance cancels exactly with the area factor $r$ of polar
coordinates; that is why the method works. (Practice 5 does the same proof in one step,
with the Jacobian of the whole map from $(u_1, u_2)$ to $(z_1, z_2)$.)

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: One Pair by Hand

**Problem.** A generator gives $U_1 = 0.3$ and $U_2 = 0.8$. Compute the Box-Muller pair $(Z_1, Z_2)$.

**Solution.**

1. The squared distance: $-2 \ln 0.3 = -2 \times (-1.20397) \approx 2.4079$. So $R = \sqrt{2.4079} \approx 1.5518$.
2. The angle: $\Theta = 2\pi \times 0.8 = 1.6\pi \approx 5.0265$ radians (that is $288$ degrees).
3. $\cos(1.6\pi) \approx 0.3090$ and $\sin(1.6\pi) \approx -0.9511$.
4. The coordinates:

$$
Z_1 \approx 1.5518 \times 0.3090 \approx 0.4795, \qquad Z_2 \approx 1.5518 \times (-0.9511) \approx -1.4758
$$

**Answer.** $(Z_1, Z_2) \approx (0.480, -1.476)$.

**Check.** The point is $1.55$ from the origin, and $0.4795^2 + 1.4758^2 \approx 0.2299 + 2.1780 = 2.4079 = R^2$.
An angle of 288 degrees is in the lower right, so $Z_1 \gt 0$ and $Z_2 \lt 0$, as found.

### Example 2: Heights and Correlated Pairs

**Problem.** Use the pair from Example 1 to simulate (a) one adult height from $\mathcal{N}(170, 49)$
in cm (variance 49, so standard deviation 7), and (b) a pair of standard normals with
correlation $\rho = 0.8$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) Scale and shift: $X = 170 + 7 Z_1 \approx 170 + 7 \times 0.4795 \approx 173.36$ cm.
2. (b) Use the 2-D Cholesky trick of
   [12.4](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md):
   $W_1 = Z_1$ and $W_2 = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \thinspace Z_2$. With $\rho = 0.8$, $\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 0.6$:

$$
W_2 = 0.8 \times 0.4795 + 0.6 \times (-1.4758) \approx 0.3836 - 0.8855 \approx -0.5019
$$

3. Check the correlation of the recipe: $\operatorname{Var}(W_2) = 0.8^2 + 0.6^2 = 1$ and
   $\operatorname{Cov}(W_1, W_2) = 0.8 \operatorname{Var}(Z_1) = 0.8$, because $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent.

**Answer.** (a) about $173.4$ cm; (b) $(W_1, W_2) \approx (0.480, -0.502)$, one draw of a pair with correlation $0.8$.

**Check.** Box-Muller gives **independent** normals, and every other normal distribution in this
book is built from them by shifting, scaling and mixing with a matrix.

### Example 3: The Polar Method Without Sines and Cosines

**Problem.** A faster variant, the **polar method** (Marsaglia's method), avoids $\cos$ and $\sin$.
Draw $V_1, V_2$ independent $\text{Unif}(-1, 1)$, let $S = V_1^2 + V_2^2$, and **reject** the pair unless
$0 \lt S \lt 1$. Then output $Z_i = V_i \sqrt{-2 \ln S / S}$. Explain why it works, and find the fraction
of pairs that are kept.

**Solution.**

1. A kept point $(V_1, V_2)$ is uniform on the unit disk: the square's uniform density, restricted
   to the disk, is still flat. The fraction kept is the area ratio:

$$
P(\text{keep}) = \frac{\text{area of disk}}{\text{area of square}} = \frac{\pi \cdot 1^2}{2 \times 2} = \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785
$$

2. For a uniform point in the disk, Example 1 of [13.1](13.1-CDF-Method.md) gave
   $P(\text{distance} \le r) = r^2$. So $P(S \le s) = P(\text{distance} \le \sqrt{s}) = s$: $S$ is $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$.
3. The angle of a uniform point in the disk is uniform and independent of its distance (the
   disk looks the same in every direction). And $\frac{V_1}{\sqrt{S}} = \cos \Theta$, $\frac{V_2}{\sqrt{S}} = \sin \Theta$.
4. So $Z_1 = \sqrt{-2 \ln S} \cdot \frac{V_1}{\sqrt{S}}$ is exactly Box-Muller, with the uniform $S$ playing the role
   of $U_1$ and the angle of the point playing the role of $2\pi U_2$.

**Answer.** It is Box-Muller in disguise. About 78.5% of the pairs are kept, so on average a
pair of normals costs $\frac{4}{\pi} \approx 1.27$ tries.

**Check.** The rejection idea is studied in general in
[19.5 Rejection Sampling](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.5-Rejection-Sampling.md).
The simulation in Practice 6 measures the acceptance rate.

## Simulate It

We implement Box-Muller and check that $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ have the right mean, variance and fourth
moment ($E[Z^4] = 3$ for a standard normal), the 68% rule, and that they are uncorrelated and
behave independently.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_pairs = 1_000_000

# Box-Muller: two independent uniforms -> two independent N(0, 1).
u1 = 1 - rng.random(n_pairs)              # in (0, 1], so log(u1) is finite
u2 = rng.random(n_pairs)
r = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u1))              # R^2 ~ Exp(rate 1/2)
theta = 2 * np.pi * u2                    # uniform angle
z1, z2 = r * np.cos(theta), r * np.sin(theta)

print(f"mean, var of Z1: {z1.mean():.4f}, {z1.var():.4f}   (exact 0, 1)")
print(f"E[Z1^4]:         {np.mean(z1**4):.4f}           (exact 3)")
print(f"P(|Z2| < 1):     {np.mean(np.abs(z2) < 1):.4f}           "
      f"(exact {stats.norm.cdf(1) - stats.norm.cdf(-1):.4f})")
print(f"corr(Z1, Z2):    {np.corrcoef(z1, z2)[0, 1]:.4f}          (exact 0)")
print(f"P(Z1>1, Z2>1):   {np.mean((z1 > 1) & (z2 > 1)):.4f}           "
      f"(independent: {stats.norm.sf(1) ** 2:.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
mean, var of Z1: -0.0003, 1.0010   (exact 0, 1)
E[Z1^4]:         3.0065           (exact 3)
P(|Z2| < 1):     0.6824           (exact 0.6827)
corr(Z1, Z2):    0.0013          (exact 0)
P(Z1>1, Z2>1):   0.0254           (independent: 0.0252)
```

Even though $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ share the same $R$, the chance that both exceed 1 is the product of the
single chances, as independence requires.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the square root.** $R = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1}$, not $-2 \ln U_1$. Without the root the
  output has the wrong variance and is not normal.
- **Taking the log of 0.** Most generators can return exactly 0, and $\ln 0 = -\infty$. Use
  $1 - U$ (which lies in $(0, 1]$), as in the code.
- **Using the same uniform twice.** $U_1$ and $U_2$ must be independent. Using $U_1$ for both the
  distance and the angle gives a spiral, not a normal cloud.
- **Thinking $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are dependent because they share $R$.** They are independent; the
  proof shows the joint density factors. Throwing $Z_2$ away is safe but wastes half the work.
- **Mixing degrees and radians.** `np.cos` expects radians, so the angle is $2\pi U_2$, not $360 U_2$.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** normal noise for weight initialization, the
  reparameterization trick $z = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon$ in variational autoencoders,
  and the noise of diffusion models.
- **Computer science:** random number libraries turn uniform bits into normals with Box-Muller
  or its relatives; NumPy's older `RandomState` used the polar method of Example 3, and the
  newer `Generator` uses a faster method called the ziggurat. Box-Muller suits GPUs well
  because it has no rejection step, so every thread does the same work.
- **Finance:** Monte Carlo simulation of stock prices needs millions of normal shocks.
- **Physics and engineering:** simulated measurement noise and thermal noise in circuits.
- **Statistics:** building any $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ sample from independent standard normals with the
  Cholesky trick ([12.4](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.4-Standard-Normal-Vectors-and-Cholesky.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Compute the Box-Muller pair for $U_1 = 0.5$ and $U_2 = 0.125$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$R = \sqrt{-2 \ln 0.5} = \sqrt{2 \ln 2} \approx 1.1774$, and $\Theta = 2\pi \times 0.125 = \frac{\pi}{4}$, where $\cos = \sin = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.7071$:

$$Z_1 = Z_2 \approx 1.1774 \times 0.7071 \approx 0.8326$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** What is the chance that a Box-Muller point lands farther than 3 from the origin?
Which values of $U_1$ cause it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$R \gt 3$ means $R^2 = -2 \ln U_1 \gt 9$, that is, $U_1 \lt e^{-4.5}$:

$$P(R \gt 3) = e^{-9/2} = e^{-4.5} \approx 0.0111$$

So only very small values of $U_1$, below about $0.011$, make far-away points.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Using independent $Z_1, Z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ from Box-Muller, build a pair $(X, Y)$, each
$\mathcal{N}(5, 4)$, with correlation $0.6$. Check the variance and the covariance.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Take $X = 5 + 2Z_1$ and $Y = 5 + 2(0.6 Z_1 + 0.8 Z_2)$, since $\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 0.8$. Then:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 4(0.36 + 0.64) = 4, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 0.6 = 2.4, \qquad \rho = \frac{2.4}{2 \cdot 2} = 0.6$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In the polar method of Example 3, the number of tries until a pair is kept is
geometric. What is its mean? How many uniforms does it use on average per normal number?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each try is kept with probability $\frac{\pi}{4}$, independently, so the number of tries is $\text{Geom}(\frac{\pi}{4})$ with mean $\frac{4}{\pi} \approx 1.273$. Each try uses 2 uniforms and a kept try gives 2 normals, so on average:

$$\frac{2 \times 1.273}{2} \approx 1.27 \text{ uniforms per normal}$$

Box-Muller uses exactly 1 uniform per normal, but needs a cosine and a sine.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove Box-Muller in one step: compute the Jacobian determinant of the map
$(u_1, u_2) \mapsto (z_1, z_2)$ and show that $f_{Z_1, Z_2}(z_1, z_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Write $\rho = \sqrt{-2 \ln u_1}$, so $\frac{d\rho}{du_1} = -\frac{1}{u_1 \rho}$, and let $c = \cos(2\pi u_2)$, $s = \sin(2\pi u_2)$. With $z_1 = \rho c$ and $z_2 = \rho s$, the Jacobian has rows $\left( -\frac{c}{u_1 \rho}, \ -2\pi \rho s \right)$ and $\left( -\frac{s}{u_1 \rho}, \ 2\pi \rho c \right)$:

$$\det J_g = -\frac{c}{u_1 \rho} \cdot 2\pi \rho c - (-2\pi \rho s) \cdot \left( -\frac{s}{u_1 \rho} \right) = -\frac{2\pi c^2}{u_1} - \frac{2\pi s^2}{u_1} = -\frac{2\pi}{u_1}$$

The uniforms have joint density 1, so $f_{Z_1, Z_2} = \frac{1}{\lvert \det J_g \rvert} = \frac{u_1}{2\pi}$. Finally $z_1^2 + z_2^2 = \rho^2 = -2 \ln u_1$, so $u_1 = e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$:

$$f_{Z_1, Z_2}(z_1, z_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Implement the polar method of Example 3. Check the acceptance rate, that $S$
is uniform, and that the outputs are standard normal.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_tries = 1_000_000

# Polar method: a uniform point in the square, kept only if it is inside the disk.
v1, v2 = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n_tries), rng.uniform(-1, 1, n_tries)
s = v1**2 + v2**2
keep = (s < 1) & (s > 0)
v1, v2, s = v1[keep], v2[keep], s[keep]
factor = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(s) / s)
z1, z2 = v1 * factor, v2 * factor

print(f"acceptance rate: {keep.mean():.4f}  (exact pi/4 = {np.pi / 4:.4f})")
print(f"P(S <= 0.3):     {np.mean(s <= 0.3):.4f}  (exact 0.3000)")
print(f"var of Z1, Z2:   {z1.var():.4f}, {z2.var():.4f}  (exact 1)")
print(f"P(Z1 > 1.96):    {np.mean(z1 > 1.96):.4f}  (exact {stats.norm.sf(1.96):.4f})")
```

Output:

```text
acceptance rate: 0.7854  (exact pi/4 = 0.7854)
P(S <= 0.3):     0.3005  (exact 0.3000)
var of Z1, Z2:   0.9996, 1.0025  (exact 1)
P(Z1 > 1.96):    0.0248  (exact 0.0250)
```

</details>

## Summary

- A pair of independent standard normals, seen in polar coordinates, has a uniform angle and
  an $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{2})$ squared distance, independent of each other.
- Box-Muller runs this backwards: $R = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1}$, $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$, then $Z_1 = R \cos \Theta$, $Z_2 = R \sin \Theta$.
- The proof is the Jacobian rule: the density $r e^{-r^2/2}$ of the distance and the area factor $r$
  of polar coordinates cancel, leaving the 2-D normal density.
- The polar method gets the same result with a rejection step instead of $\cos$ and $\sin$.
- Any normal, and any correlated normal vector, is then built by shifting and scaling.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{Z_1 = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1} \thinspace \cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1} \thinspace \sin(2\pi U_2)}
$$
