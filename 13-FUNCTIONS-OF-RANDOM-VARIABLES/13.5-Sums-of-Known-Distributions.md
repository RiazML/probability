# 13.5 Sums of Known Distributions

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.5 Sums of Known Distributions

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [13.4 Convolution Sums of Random Variables](13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md)

> **Big idea:** A few families stay in the family when you add independent members (binomial with the same $p$, Poisson, normal, Gamma with the same rate), and then you only add the right parameters; most other sums change shape.

---

## Why It Matters

Convolution ([13.4](13.4-Convolution-Sums-of-Random-Variables.md)) always works, but it
can be long. For the most common distributions the answer is already known, and it is
very simple: the sum of two Poisson counts is again Poisson, the sum of two normal
errors is again normal, and so on. Knowing this short list saves a lot of work. Knowing
its **conditions** (independence, the same $p$, the same rate) prevents the most common
mistakes about sums.

## Intuition

A company has two call centers. Calls reach the first at about 3 per minute and the
second at about 5 per minute, each as a Poisson count, independently. How many calls
arrive in total in a minute? Think of the calls as tiny independent chances spread over
the minute. Putting the two streams together gives one stream of tiny independent
chances, with about $3 + 5 = 8$ calls per minute. So the total should again be Poisson,
with mean 8. It is.

Now flip 10 coins with $p = 0.3$ and then 20 more with the same $p$. Together you have
just flipped 30 coins with $p = 0.3$, so the number of heads is $\text{Bin}(30, 0.3)$. But
if the second batch had $p = 0.8$, the 30 flips would not all be alike, and the total
is not binomial at all. The rule "the family is kept" depends on the parts being the
same kind of thing.

## Picture

```text
   independent parts                    their sum               what adds
   -------------------------------      --------------------    -----------
   Bin(n, p)      + Bin(m, p)      -->  Bin(n + m, p)           trials
   Pois(λ)        + Pois(μ)        -->  Pois(λ + μ)             rates
   N(μ1, σ1²)     + N(μ2, σ2²)     -->  N(μ1 + μ2, σ1² + σ2²)   mean, var
   Gamma(a, β)    + Gamma(b, β)    -->  Gamma(a + b, β)         shapes
   chi-sq(k)      + chi-sq(m)      -->  chi-sq(k + m)           df

   needs: independence, and the same p (binomial) or the same rate β (Gamma)
```

Each closed family has one parameter that simply adds; the others must match.

## Definition

A family of distributions is **closed under independent sums** (also called
**reproductive**) if the sum of independent members is again a member. The main
results, for **independent** $X$ and $Y$:

| $X$ | $Y$ | $X + Y$ | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{Bin}(n, p)$ | $\text{Bin}(m, p)$ | $\text{Bin}(n + m, p)$ | same $p$ |
| $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ | $\text{Pois}(\mu)$ | $\text{Pois}(\lambda + \mu)$ | none |
| $\text{NegBin}(r, p)$ | $\text{NegBin}(s, p)$ | $\text{NegBin}(r + s, p)$ | same $p$ |
| $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ | $\mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ | $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$ | none |
| $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_1, \beta)$ | $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_2, \beta)$ | $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta)$ | same rate $\beta$ |
| $\chi^2(k)$ | $\chi^2(m)$ | $\chi^2(k + m)$ | none |

Here $\text{NegBin}(r, p)$ counts the failures before the $r$-th success, $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ has
shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$, and $\chi^2(k)$ is the chi-square with $k$ degrees of freedom,
which is $\text{Gamma}(\frac{k}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$. Two useful special cases:

$$
X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bern}(p) \implies \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \sim \text{Bin}(n, p), \qquad X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exp}(\lambda) \implies \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \sim \text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)
$$

**In words:** for these families, add the counting parameter (trials, rate, shape,
degrees of freedom) and keep the "kind" parameter ($p$ or the rate $\beta$) fixed. For normals,
add the means and add the **variances**.

## Why It Is True

Some rows are already proved elsewhere: Poisson plus Poisson by a discrete
convolution and the binomial theorem in
[7.11](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.11-Relationships-Between-Distributions.md), and
exponentials to Gamma by induction in [9.4](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md).
The binomial and negative binomial rows follow from a story: $n$ trials and then $m$
more trials with the same $p$ are just $n + m$ trials; waiting for $r$ successes and then
for $s$ more is waiting for $r + s$ successes. Here we prove the two continuous rows with
the convolution integral.

### Normal Plus Normal

First take means 0: $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_2^2)$, and write
$s^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2$. The convolution is:

$$
f_Z(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sigma_1 \sigma_2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left( -\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_1^2} - \frac{(z - x)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} \right) dx
$$

Put the exponent over the common denominator $2\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2$ and **complete the square**
in $x$ (write it as a perfect square plus a part without $x$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_2^2 x^2 + \sigma_1^2 (z - x)^2 &= s^2 x^2 - 2\sigma_1^2 z x + \sigma_1^2 z^2 && \text{(expand, collect } x^2 \text{)} \\
&= s^2 (x - c)^2 + \sigma_1^2 z^2 - \frac{\sigma_1^4 z^2}{s^2} && \text{(complete the square, } c = \sigma_1^2 z / s^2 \text{)} \\
&= s^2 (x - c)^2 + \frac{\sigma_1^2 \sigma_2^2}{s^2} z^2 && \text{(since } s^2 - \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $2\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2$. The exponent becomes $-\frac{(x - c)^2}{2\tau^2} - \frac{z^2}{2s^2}$ with
$\tau = \frac{\sigma_1 \sigma_2}{s}$. A normal density with variance $\tau^2$ integrates to 1, so
$\int e^{-(x - c)^2 / (2\tau^2)} dx = \sqrt{2\pi} \tau$:

$$
f_Z(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sigma_1 \sigma_2} \cdot \sqrt{2\pi} \thinspace \frac{\sigma_1 \sigma_2}{s} \cdot e^{-z^2/(2s^2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \thinspace s} e^{-z^2/(2s^2)}
$$

That is the $\mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$ density. For general means, write
$X + Y = (X - \mu_1) + (Y - \mu_2) + (\mu_1 + \mu_2)$: the first two parts are normal with
mean 0, and adding a constant shifts the mean. (Two other proofs are in
[12.9 Sums of Gaussians](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md).)

### Gamma Plus Gamma With the Same Rate

Both densities are 0 below 0, so $x$ runs from 0 to $z$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_Z(z) &= \int_0^z \frac{\beta^{\alpha_1} x^{\alpha_1 - 1} e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)} \cdot \frac{\beta^{\alpha_2} (z - x)^{\alpha_2 - 1} e^{-\beta (z - x)}}{\Gamma(\alpha_2)} \thinspace dx && \text{(convolution)} \\
&= \frac{\beta^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2} e^{-\beta z}}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\Gamma(\alpha_2)} \int_0^z x^{\alpha_1 - 1} (z - x)^{\alpha_2 - 1} \thinspace dx && \text{(the } e^{\pm \beta x} \text{ cancel: same rate)} \\
&= \frac{\beta^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2} e^{-\beta z}}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\Gamma(\alpha_2)} \cdot z^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - 1} B(\alpha_1, \alpha_2) && \text{(put } x = zt \text{, } dx = z \thinspace dt \text{)} \\
&= \frac{\beta^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2}}{\Gamma(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)} z^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - 1} e^{-\beta z} && \text{(}B = \Gamma(\alpha_1)\Gamma(\alpha_2) / \Gamma(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2) \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta)$ density. The Beta function $B$ and its Gamma form
are in [9.5 Beta](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md). The step that needs the same
rate is the second line: with different rates, $e^{-\beta_1 x} e^{\beta_2 x}$ does not cancel.
The chi-square row is the case $\beta = \frac{1}{2}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Two Call Centers

**Problem.** Calls to center A are $\text{Pois}(3)$ per minute and to center B $\text{Pois}(5)$ per minute,
independently. Find the chance of exactly 10 calls in total in one minute.

**Solution.**

1. The total is $T \sim \text{Pois}(3 + 5) = \text{Pois}(8)$.
2. Use the Poisson PMF:

$$
P(T = 10) = \frac{e^{-8} \thinspace 8^{10}}{10!} \approx 0.00033546 \times \frac{1073741824}{3628800} \approx 0.00033546 \times 295.89 \approx 0.0993
$$

**Answer.** $P(T = 10) \approx 0.099$, about 1 minute in 10.

**Check.** The mean is 8, so 10 calls is a little above average, and a probability near
$0.1$ is reasonable. Without the rule, we would need the 11-term convolution sum
$\sum_{k=0}^{10} P(A = k) P(B = 10 - k)$; the simulation confirms both agree.

### Example 2: A Trip With Two Legs

**Problem.** A trip has a bus leg of $\mathcal{N}(30, 16)$ minutes and a train leg of
$\mathcal{N}(45, 9)$ minutes (the second parameter is the variance), independent. What is the
chance the whole trip takes more than 85 minutes?

**Solution.**

1. The total is normal. Means add: $30 + 45 = 75$. Variances add: $16 + 9 = 25$.
   So $T \sim \mathcal{N}(75, 25)$, with standard deviation $\sqrt{25} = 5$.
2. Standardize ([9.2](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md)):

$$
P(T \gt 85) = P\left( Z \gt \frac{85 - 75}{5} \right) = P(Z \gt 2) \approx 0.0228
$$

**Answer.** About $0.023$, so roughly 1 trip in 44 takes over 85 minutes.

**Check.** The standard deviations are 4 and 3, but the total has standard deviation 5,
not $4 + 3 = 7$. Variances add; standard deviations do not.

### Example 3: When the Rule Breaks

**Problem.** Two servers are each up or down. Server 1 is up with probability $0.1$, server 2
with probability $0.9$, independently. Let $S$ be the number of servers up. Is $S$ binomial?

**Solution.**

1. $S = X_1 + X_2$ with $X_1 \sim \text{Bern}(0.1)$ and $X_2 \sim \text{Bern}(0.9)$. The $p$ values differ.
2. Find the PMF by convolution:

$$
P(S = 0) = 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.09, \qquad P(S = 2) = 0.1 \times 0.9 = 0.09, \qquad P(S = 1) = 1 - 0.09 - 0.09 = 0.82
$$

3. A binomial on $\lbrace 0, 1, 2 \rbrace$ must be $\text{Bin}(2, q)$. The mean of $S$ is $0.1 + 0.9 = 1$, so
   $q = 0.5$, and $\text{Bin}(2, 0.5)$ has PMF $0.25, 0.50, 0.25$.

**Answer.** No. $S$ has PMF $0.09, 0.82, 0.09$, far from $\text{Bin}(2, 0.5)$.

**Check.** $S$ is much less spread out than the binomial: $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 0.09 + 0.09 = 0.18$,
while $\text{Bin}(2, 0.5)$ has variance $0.5$. Practice 5 shows that unequal $p$ values always lower
the variance.

## Simulate It

We check Examples 1, 2 and 3, and one Gamma row, by adding simulated parts and comparing
with the family the table predicts.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000

# Poisson: Pois(3) + Pois(5) should be Pois(8).
calls = rng.poisson(3, n) + rng.poisson(5, n)
print(f"P(calls = 10): sim {np.mean(calls == 10):.4f}, "
      f"Pois(8) {stats.poisson.pmf(10, 8):.4f}")

# Normal: N(30, 16) + N(45, 9) should be N(75, 25).
trip = rng.normal(30, 4, n) + rng.normal(45, 3, n)     # numpy takes the SD
print(f"P(trip > 85):  sim {np.mean(trip > 85):.4f}, "
      f"N(75, 25) {stats.norm.sf(85, 75, 5):.4f}")

# Gamma, same rate: Gamma(2, 1) + Gamma(3, 1) should be Gamma(5, 1).
g = rng.gamma(2, 1, n) + rng.gamma(3, 1, n)             # shape, scale = 1/rate
print(f"P(G > 7):      sim {np.mean(g > 7):.4f}, "
      f"Gamma(5, 1) {stats.gamma.sf(7, a=5):.4f}")

# Broken rule: Bern(0.1) + Bern(0.9) is NOT Bin(2, 0.5).
s = (rng.random(n) < 0.1).astype(int) + (rng.random(n) < 0.9)
print("P(S = 0, 1, 2): sim", np.round(np.bincount(s) / n, 4),
      " Bin(2, 0.5): [0.25 0.5 0.25]")
```

Output:

```text
P(calls = 10): sim 0.0990, Pois(8) 0.0993
P(trip > 85):  sim 0.0229, N(75, 25) 0.0228
P(G > 7):      sim 0.1729, Gamma(5, 1) 0.1730
P(S = 0, 1, 2): sim [0.09   0.8204 0.0896]  Bin(2, 0.5): [0.25 0.5 0.25]
```

The first three sums match their predicted families; the last one does not, as Example 3 said.

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding standard deviations.** For normals the **variances** add. Two parts with
  standard deviations 4 and 3 give a total with standard deviation 5, not 7.
- **Forgetting the conditions.** Binomials need the same $p$, Gammas the same rate.
  $\text{Gamma}(2, 1) + \text{Gamma}(3, 5)$ is not a Gamma.
- **Forgetting independence.** Two normal variables that are not jointly normal can
  have a sum that is not normal at all ([12.9](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.9-Sums-of-Gaussians.md)).
  The table needs independence.
- **Using the rules for differences.** $X - Y$ of two Poissons can be negative, so it is not
  Poisson. For normals the difference works: $X - Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 - \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$ (the
  variances still **add**).
- **Expecting every family to be closed.** Uniforms, exponentials with different rates and
  geometrics (as counts of trials) change shape when added.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** diffusion models add independent Gaussian noise to the data
  at each step. The total noise after many steps is again Gaussian, with the variances
  added, so any step can be sampled in one jump.
- **Computer science:** merged Poisson streams (requests from many users) are Poisson,
  so a load balancer's total load has a simple distribution.
- **Statistics:** sums of squared normals are chi-square, which gives the distribution of
  sample variances and of goodness-of-fit tests.
- **Operations:** the time to finish $n$ jobs with exponential service times is Gamma
  (Erlang), used to plan call-center staffing.
- **Everyday life:** total travel time over several legs, and goals scored by two teams
  in a match (Poisson plus Poisson).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Machine A makes 100 parts and machine B makes 50 parts. Each part is
defective with probability $0.02$, independently. What is the distribution of the total
number of defects, and its mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The counts are $\text{Bin}(100, 0.02)$ and $\text{Bin}(50, 0.02)$ with the same $p$, so the total is $\text{Bin}(150, 0.02)$, with mean $150 \times 0.02 = 3$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** In a football match, team A scores $\text{Pois}(1.4)$ goals and team B scores
$\text{Pois}(1.1)$ goals, independently. What is the chance of a 0-0 draw?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The total is $\text{Pois}(2.5)$, and 0-0 means the total is 0:

$$P(\text{total} = 0) = e^{-2.5} \approx 0.0821$$

You get the same from $P(A = 0) P(B = 0) = e^{-1.4} e^{-1.1}$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A server handles 3 jobs in a row. Each takes $\text{Exp}(2)$ minutes (rate 2, mean
$0.5$ minute), independently. Find the distribution of the total time $S$, its mean and
variance, and $P(S \gt 2)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$S \sim \text{Gamma}(3, 2)$, so $E[S] = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5$ minutes and $\operatorname{Var}(S) = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75$. With the Erlang formula from [9.4](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md), $S \gt 2$ means fewer than 3 events in 2 minutes of a rate-2 stream, a $\text{Pois}(4)$ count:

$$P(S \gt 2) = e^{-4} \left( 1 + 4 + \tfrac{4^2}{2} \right) = 13 e^{-4} \approx 0.2381$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Runner A's 100 m time is $\mathcal{N}(12.0, 0.25)$ seconds and runner B's is
$\mathcal{N}(12.5, 0.36)$, independent. What is the chance that A beats B?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A beats B when $D = A - B \lt 0$. The difference is normal with mean $12.0 - 12.5 = -0.5$ and variance $0.25 + 0.36 = 0.61$ (variances add even for a difference):

$$P(D \lt 0) = P\left( Z \lt \frac{0 - (-0.5)}{\sqrt{0.61}} \right) = P(Z \lt 0.640) \approx 0.739$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $S = X_1 + \dots + X_N$ with independent $X_i \sim \text{Bern}(p_i)$, and let
$\bar{p} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_i p_i$. Prove that $\operatorname{Var}(S) \le N \bar{p} (1 - \bar{p})$, with equality only
when all $p_i$ are equal. Conclude that $S$ is binomial only if all the $p_i$ are equal
(assume $0 \lt p_i \lt 1$).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By independence the variances add, so $\operatorname{Var}(S) = \sum_i p_i(1 - p_i) = N\bar{p} - \sum_i p_i^2$. The spread of the numbers $p_i$ around their average is never negative:

$$0 \le \sum_i (p_i - \bar{p})^2 = \sum_i p_i^2 - N \bar{p}^2$$

So $\sum_i p_i^2 \ge N\bar{p}^2$, and $\operatorname{Var}(S) \le N\bar{p} - N\bar{p}^2 = N\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})$, with equality only when every $p_i = \bar{p}$.

Now $S$ takes every value $0, \dots, N$ with positive probability, so if it were binomial it would be $\text{Bin}(N, q)$. Its mean $N\bar{p}$ forces $q = \bar{p}$, and then its variance would be $N\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})$. That happens only when all $p_i$ are equal.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
a = rng.normal(12.0, 0.5, 1_000_000)     # SD = sqrt(0.25)
b = rng.normal(12.5, 0.6, 1_000_000)     # SD = sqrt(0.36)
exact = stats.norm.cdf(0.5 / np.sqrt(0.61))
print(f"P(A beats B): simulation {np.mean(a < b):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(A beats B): simulation 0.7395, exact 0.7390
```

</details>

## Summary

- Binomial (same $p$), Poisson, negative binomial (same $p$), normal, Gamma (same rate) and
  chi-square families are closed under independent sums.
- Add the counting parameter (trials, rate, shape, degrees of freedom); for normals add
  the means and the variances, never the standard deviations.
- The conditions matter: independence always, and equal $p$ or equal rate where the table
  says so. Otherwise the sum leaves the family.
- The proofs are convolutions (or stories); generating functions give shorter proofs in
  [15.3 MGF of Sums](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.3-MGF-of-Sums.md).

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2), \ Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2), \ X \perp Y \implies X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)}
$$
