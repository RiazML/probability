# 13.7 Order Statistics

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.7 Order Statistics

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [13.6 Max and Min](13.6-Max-and-Min.md)

> **Big idea:** The $k$-th smallest of $n$ i.i.d. values is near $x$ when $k - 1$ values fall below $x$, one falls at $x$ and $n - k$ fall above, so its density is a count of arrangements times $F(x)^{k-1} f(x) (1 - F(x))^{n-k}$; for uniform values this is exactly $\text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)$.

---

## Why It Matters

Sorting a sample is one of the most common things we do with data. The median, the
quartiles, the "p99 latency" of a web service, the second-highest bid in an auction and
the time of the third failure among ten machines are all values from a **sorted**
sample. Topic [13.6](13.6-Max-and-Min.md) handled the two ends (max and min). This topic
handles every position in between. It also explains a beautiful fact used all over
statistics: sorted uniform numbers follow Beta distributions.

## Intuition

Draw 3 random numbers, each uniform on $[0, 1]$, and take the middle one (the median).
What is the chance that the median is at most $0.25$? The median is at most $0.25$ exactly
when **at least 2** of the 3 numbers are at most $0.25$. Each number is at most $0.25$ with
probability $0.25$, independently, so the count of small numbers is $\text{Bin}(3, 0.25)$:

$$
P(\text{median} \le 0.25) = \binom{3}{2} (0.25)^2 (0.75) + (0.25)^3 = 0.140625 + 0.015625 = 0.15625
$$

A single uniform number is at most $0.25$ with probability $0.25$, but the median of three
only with about $0.16$. The median is pulled toward the middle, because it needs **two**
small numbers to be small.

For the density, think about where the values sit. For the $k$-th smallest to be at $x$, you
need $k - 1$ values to the left of $x$, one value right at $x$, and the remaining $n - k$ to the
right. Count the ways to choose which values go where, and multiply the chances.

## Picture

```text
      k - 1 values              1 value             n - k values
      below x                   in [x, x+dx]        above x
   |----*----*-----*------------[#]------------*--------*-------*---|
   0                             x                                  1

   chance of one such arrangement:  F(x)^(k-1) * f(x) dx * (1-F(x))^(n-k)
   number of arrangements:          n! / ( (k-1)! * 1! * (n-k)! )
```

The $k$-th smallest value lands in the small strip when exactly $k - 1$ values are on its
left and $n - k$ on its right.

## Definition

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. and continuous, with CDF $F$ and density $f$. Sort them:

$$
X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}
$$

The **$k$-th order statistic** $X_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th smallest value. So $X_{(1)}$ is the minimum,
$X_{(n)}$ is the maximum, and for odd $n$ the middle one $X_{((n+1)/2)}$ is the **sample median**.
(For continuous values, ties have probability 0.)

**CDF.** $X_{(k)}$ is at most $x$ exactly when at least $k$ of the $n$ values are at most $x$:

$$
F_{(k)}(x) = P(X_{(k)} \le x) = \sum_{j=k}^{n} \binom{n}{j} F(x)^j \big(1 - F(x)\big)^{n-j}
$$

**Density.**

$$
f_{(k)}(x) = \frac{n!}{(k-1)! \thinspace (n-k)!} \thinspace F(x)^{k-1} \thinspace f(x) \thinspace \big(1 - F(x)\big)^{n-k}
$$

where:

- $F(x)^{k-1}$ is the chance that $k - 1$ chosen values are below $x$,
- $f(x)$ is the density of the one value at $x$,
- $(1 - F(x))^{n-k}$ is the chance that the other $n - k$ values are above $x$,
- $\frac{n!}{(k-1)! \thinspace 1! \thinspace (n-k)!}$ counts the ways to split the $n$ values into these three groups.

**In words:** the $k$-th smallest is near $x$ when $k - 1$ values fall below, one falls at $x$, and
$n - k$ fall above; count the arrangements and multiply the chances.

**The uniform case.** For $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ values, $F(x) = x$ and $f(x) = 1$, so:

$$
f_{(k)}(x) = \frac{n!}{(k-1)! \thinspace (n-k)!} \thinspace x^{k-1} (1 - x)^{n-k}, \quad 0 \le x \le 1 \qquad \text{so} \qquad U_{(k)} \sim \text{Beta}(k, \ n - k + 1)
$$

**In words:** the $k$-th smallest of $n$ uniforms is a Beta with parameters "how many at or
below it" ($k$) and "how many above it, plus one" ($n - k + 1$). Its mean is $\frac{k}{n + 1}$.

## Why It Is True

**The CDF.** For a fixed $x$, each value is at most $x$ with probability $F(x)$, independently.
So the number $N_x$ of values at most $x$ is $\text{Bin}(n, F(x))$. The $k$-th smallest is at most $x$
exactly when $N_x \ge k$, and the binomial PMF gives the sum above.

**The density, by the strip argument.** Take a tiny strip $[x, x + dx]$. The chance that two
or more values fall in it is of size $(dx)^2$, which we can ignore. So $X_{(k)}$ is in the strip
when **exactly** one value is in it, $k - 1$ are below $x$ and $n - k$ are above. For one fixed
choice of which values go where, independence gives the chance
$F(x)^{k-1} \cdot f(x) \thinspace dx \cdot (1 - F(x))^{n-k}$. The number of choices is a multinomial
coefficient ([2.6](../02-COUNTING/2.6-Multinomial-Coefficients.md)): pick the one value in the
strip ($n$ ways), then the $k - 1$ values below it from the remaining $n - 1$:

$$
n \binom{n-1}{k-1} = n \cdot \frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)! \thinspace (n-k)!} = \frac{n!}{(k-1)! \thinspace (n-k)!}
$$

Multiply and divide by $dx$ to get the density.

<details>
<summary>Full proof (optional): differentiate the CDF</summary>

Differentiate each term of the CDF with the product rule, using $F' = f$:

$$\frac{d}{dx} \binom{n}{j} F^j (1 - F)^{n-j} = \binom{n}{j} \left[ j F^{j-1} (1 - F)^{n-j} - (n - j) F^j (1 - F)^{n-j-1} \right] f$$

Use $j\binom{n}{j} = n\binom{n-1}{j-1}$ and $(n - j)\binom{n}{j} = n\binom{n-1}{j}$. Write $a_j = \binom{n-1}{j-1} F^{j-1} (1 - F)^{n-j}$. Then the bracket times $\binom{n}{j}$ equals $n(a_j - a_{j+1})$, so the sum over $j = k, \dots, n$ telescopes:

$$f_{(k)}(x) = n f \sum_{j=k}^{n} (a_j - a_{j+1}) = n f (a_k - a_{n+1}) = n \binom{n-1}{k-1} F^{k-1} (1 - F)^{n-k} f$$

since $a_{n+1}$ contains $\binom{n-1}{n} = 0$. This is the density formula.

</details>

**The uniform case is a Beta.** The $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ density is
$\frac{x^{\alpha - 1}(1 - x)^{\beta - 1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}$ ([9.5 Beta](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md)). With
$\alpha = k$ and $\beta = n - k + 1$, the powers are $x^{k-1}(1 - x)^{n-k}$, matching the formula. The
constants match too, because $\Gamma(m) = (m - 1)!$ for whole numbers:

$$
\frac{1}{B(k, n - k + 1)} = \frac{\Gamma(n + 1)}{\Gamma(k) \thinspace \Gamma(n - k + 1)} = \frac{n!}{(k-1)! \thinspace (n-k)!}
$$

So $U_{(k)} \sim \text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)$, with mean $\frac{k}{k + (n - k + 1)} = \frac{k}{n + 1}$. Two checks: $k = n$
gives $n x^{n-1}$ and $k = 1$ gives $n(1 - x)^{n-1}$, the max and min densities of
[13.6](13.6-Max-and-Min.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Median of Three

**Problem.** Find the density of the median of 3 independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ numbers, and check
$P(\text{median} \le 0.25) = 0.15625$ from the Intuition.

**Solution.**

1. The median is $U_{(2)}$ with $n = 3$, $k = 2$. The coefficient is $\frac{3!}{1! \thinspace 1!} = 6$:

$$
f_{(2)}(x) = 6 x (1 - x), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1
$$

   This is $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$, a symmetric hump with mean $\frac{2}{4} = 0.5$.
2. Integrate:

$$
P(U_{(2)} \le 0.25) = \int_0^{0.25} (6x - 6x^2) \thinspace dx = \left[ 3x^2 - 2x^3 \right]_0^{0.25} = 0.1875 - 0.03125 = 0.15625
$$

**Answer.** $f_{(2)}(x) = 6x(1 - x)$, and the integral gives $0.15625$, the same as the binomial count.

**Check.** The density is 0 at both ends and highest at $0.5$, where it is $1.5$. The median
of three is much less spread out than one uniform number.

### Example 2: The Price in a Second-Price Auction

**Problem.** In a second-price auction the highest bidder wins but pays the **second-highest**
bid. Five bidders bid independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ amounts (in millions of taka). Find the
distribution of the price, its mean, and the chance that the price is above $0.8$.

**Solution.**

1. The second-highest of 5 is the 4-th smallest: $k = 4$, $n = 5$. So the price is
   $U_{(4)} \sim \text{Beta}(4, 2)$, with coefficient $\frac{5!}{3! \thinspace 1!} = 20$:

$$
f_{(4)}(x) = 20 x^3 (1 - x), \qquad E[U_{(4)}] = \frac{4}{5 + 1} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667
$$

2. For the tail, use the CDF: the price is at most $0.8$ when at least 4 of the 5 bids are at most $0.8$:

$$
P(U_{(4)} \le 0.8) = \binom{5}{4} (0.8)^4 (0.2) + (0.8)^5 = 5 \times 0.4096 \times 0.2 + 0.32768 = 0.4096 + 0.32768 = 0.73728
$$

3. So $P(U_{(4)} \gt 0.8) = 1 - 0.73728 = 0.26272$.

**Answer.** The price is $\text{Beta}(4, 2)$, with mean about $0.667$ million taka, and it is above $0.8$
million with probability about $0.263$.

**Check.** The highest bid has mean $\frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833$, so the seller loses about $0.167$ on
average by charging the second price. In exchange, bidders have a reason to bid honestly,
which is why ad auctions on the web use versions of this rule.

### Example 3: The Median Lifetime of Five Machines

**Problem.** Five machines have independent $\text{Exp}(1)$ lifetimes, in years (rate 1, mean 1 year).
Let $T = X_{(3)}$ be the time of the third failure, the sample median. Find its density,
$E[T]$, and $P(T \le 1)$.

**Solution.**

1. With $F(x) = 1 - e^{-x}$, $f(x) = e^{-x}$, $n = 5$, $k = 3$, the coefficient is $\frac{5!}{2! \thinspace 2!} = 30$:

$$
f_{(3)}(x) = 30 (1 - e^{-x})^2 \thinspace e^{-x} \thinspace (e^{-x})^2 = 30 (1 - e^{-x})^2 e^{-3x}, \qquad x \ge 0
$$

2. For the mean, use the gaps from Example 3 of [13.6](13.6-Max-and-Min.md): the first failure
   comes after an $\text{Exp}(5)$ time, the next after a further $\text{Exp}(4)$, then $\text{Exp}(3)$ (memoryless).
   So:

$$
E[T] = \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{12 + 15 + 20}{60} = \frac{47}{60} \approx 0.783
$$

3. For $P(T \le 1)$, count failures by year 1. Each machine has failed with probability
   $p = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321$, so the count is $\text{Bin}(5, p)$, and we need at least 3:

$$
P(T \le 1) = \sum_{j=3}^{5} \binom{5}{j} p^j (1 - p)^{5-j} \approx 0.3418 + 0.2937 + 0.1009 \approx 0.7364
$$

**Answer.** $f_{(3)}(x) = 30(1 - e^{-x})^2 e^{-3x}$, $E[T] \approx 0.783$ years, $P(T \le 1) \approx 0.736$.

**Check.** The true median of one lifetime is $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$. The sample median of 5 has a
mean a bit above it, because the exponential is skewed to the right. The simulation
agrees.

## Simulate It

We sort many samples of 5 uniforms and compare three order statistics with
$\text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)$, then check Example 3.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, n = 400_000, 5

# Sort each row of 5 uniforms; column k-1 holds the k-th smallest.
u = np.sort(rng.random((n_trials, n)), axis=1)
for k in [1, 2, 4]:
    beta = stats.beta(k, n - k + 1)               # claim: Beta(k, n - k + 1)
    print(f"k={k}: mean sim {u[:, k-1].mean():.4f} exact {beta.mean():.4f} | "
          f"P(<= 0.3) sim {np.mean(u[:, k-1] <= 0.3):.4f} exact {beta.cdf(0.3):.4f}")

# Example 3: median of 5 Exp(1) lifetimes.
med = np.median(rng.exponential(1.0, (n_trials, n)), axis=1)
print(f"Exp median: E sim {med.mean():.4f}, exact {1/5 + 1/4 + 1/3:.4f}; "
      f"P(<= 1) sim {np.mean(med <= 1):.4f}, "
      f"exact {stats.binom.sf(2, 5, 1 - np.exp(-1)):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
k=1: mean sim 0.1668 exact 0.1667 | P(<= 0.3) sim 0.8316 exact 0.8319
k=2: mean sim 0.3334 exact 0.3333 | P(<= 0.3) sim 0.4722 exact 0.4718
k=4: mean sim 0.6665 exact 0.6667 | P(<= 0.3) sim 0.0307 exact 0.0308
Exp median: E sim 0.7831, exact 0.7833; P(<= 1) sim 0.7367, exact 0.7364
```

The means $\frac{1}{6}, \frac{2}{6}, \frac{4}{6}$ show the pattern $\frac{k}{n + 1}$: on average, 5 uniform points
cut $[0, 1]$ into 6 equal gaps.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up $X_{(k)}$ and $X_k$.** $X_3$ is the third value **drawn**; $X_{(3)}$ is the third
  **smallest**. They have different distributions.
- **Forgetting the counting factor.** $F^{k-1} f (1 - F)^{n-k}$ alone does not integrate to 1. The
  factor $\frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}$ counts the arrangements.
- **Counting from the wrong end.** The $k$-th **largest** is $X_{(n - k + 1)}$. The second-highest
  of 5 is $X_{(4)}$, not $X_{(2)}$.
- **Treating order statistics as independent.** They are not: $X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)}$ always, so knowing
  $X_{(1)}$ is large forces $X_{(2)}$ to be large too.
- **Using the density formula for discrete values.** With ties (like dice), use the CDF and
  take differences, as for the max of dice in [13.6](13.6-Max-and-Min.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer systems:** a "p99 latency" is estimated from a sorted list of response times.
  The binomial CDF tells you how reliable that estimate is, whatever the true
  distribution (Practice 4).
- **Statistics:** the sample median, quartiles and trimmed means are order statistics. They
  are **robust**: one wild value moves them very little.
- **AI and machine learning:** top $k$ retrieval, nearest-neighbor distances, and ranking
  metrics all use sorted scores. Sorted p-values of uniform nulls are Beta, which underlies
  multiple-testing corrections.
- **Reliability:** a system that works while at least $k$ of its $n$ parts work (a "k-out-of-n"
  system) has an order statistic as its lifetime (Practice 3).
- **Economics:** second-price auctions (Example 2) and the order of finishing times in a race.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Four numbers are independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. What is the distribution of the
third smallest, and its mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$n = 4$, $k = 3$, so $U_{(3)} \sim \text{Beta}(3, 2)$, with density $\frac{4!}{2! \thinspace 1!} x^2 (1 - x) = 12 x^2 (1 - x)$ and mean $\frac{3}{5} = 0.6$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Find the mean and variance of the median of 5 independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ numbers.
Compare with the variance $\frac{1}{12}$ of one number.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The median is $U_{(3)} \sim \text{Beta}(3, 3)$. With the Beta formulas from [9.5](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md):

$$E = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = \frac{3 \cdot 3}{6^2 \cdot 7} = \frac{9}{252} = \frac{1}{28} \approx 0.0357$$

That is less than half of $\frac{1}{12} \approx 0.0833$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A 2-out-of-3 system works while at least 2 of its 3 parts work. The parts have
independent $\text{Exp}(1)$ lifetimes. Find $P(\text{system works past } t)$ and the mean system lifetime.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The system fails at the second failure, $X_{(2)}$. It works past $t$ when at most 1 part has failed. With $q = e^{-t}$ (one part survives past $t$):

$$P(X_{(2)} \gt t) = q^3 + 3(1 - q) q^2 = 3e^{-2t} - 2e^{-3t}$$

Integrate for the mean (the tail-sum formula), or use the gaps $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2}$:

$$E[X_{(2)}] = \int_0^{\infty} (3e^{-2t} - 2e^{-3t}) \thinspace dt = \tfrac{3}{2} - \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{5}{6}$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A service records $n = 100$ i.i.d. continuous response times. Let $q$ be the true
99th percentile, so $F(q) = 0.99$. Find the chance that the largest observed time is above $q$,
and the chance that $X_{(99)}$ is above $q$. Why does the answer not depend on $F$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The number of values at most $q$ is $\text{Bin}(100, 0.99)$, whatever $F$ is. The maximum is above $q$ unless all 100 are at most $q$:

$$P(X_{(100)} \gt q) = 1 - 0.99^{100} \approx 1 - 0.3660 = 0.6340$$

$X_{(99)} \gt q$ means at most 98 values are at most $q$:

$$P(X_{(99)} \gt q) = 1 - \left[ 100 (0.99)^{99} (0.01) + 0.99^{100} \right] \approx 1 - (0.3697 + 0.3660) = 0.2642$$

Only the count below $q$ matters, and its distribution uses just $F(q) = 0.99$. So order statistics give **distribution-free** statements about percentiles.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** For $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ numbers, the joint density of the minimum and maximum is
$n(n - 1)(y - x)^{n-2}$ for $0 \lt x \lt y \lt 1$ (by the strip argument with two strips). Use it to show
that the **range** $R = U_{(n)} - U_{(1)}$ is $\text{Beta}(n - 1, 2)$, and find $E[R]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a fixed range $r$, the minimum $x$ can be anywhere in $(0, 1 - r)$, and then $y = x + r$. Integrate the joint density along this line (the map $(x, y) \mapsto (x, r)$ has Jacobian 1, see [13.3](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md)):

$$f_R(r) = \int_0^{1 - r} n(n - 1) r^{n-2} \thinspace dx = n(n - 1) r^{n-2} (1 - r), \qquad 0 \lt r \lt 1$$

Since $\frac{1}{B(n - 1, 2)} = \frac{n!}{(n-2)! \thinspace 1!} = n(n - 1)$, this is $\text{Beta}(n - 1, 2)$, with mean $\frac{n - 1}{n + 1}$. For example, 9 uniform numbers have an average range of $0.8$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 4 with $\text{Exp}(1)$ response times (so $q = \ln 100$).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials, n = 200_000, 100
q = -np.log(0.01)                     # true 99th percentile of Exp(1)

x = np.sort(rng.exponential(1.0, (n_trials, n)), axis=1)
print(f"P(max > q):   simulation {np.mean(x[:, 99] > q):.4f}, exact {1 - 0.99**100:.4f}")
exact_99 = 1 - (100 * 0.99**99 * 0.01 + 0.99**100)
print(f"P(X_(99) > q): simulation {np.mean(x[:, 98] > q):.4f}, exact {exact_99:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(max > q):   simulation 0.6335, exact 0.6340
P(X_(99) > q): simulation 0.2632, exact 0.2642
```

</details>

## Summary

- Sorting gives the order statistics $X_{(1)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}$: min, median, max and everything between.
- CDF: $X_{(k)} \le x$ exactly when at least $k$ values are $\le x$, a binomial count with success
  probability $F(x)$.
- Density: count the arrangements ($k - 1$ below, one at $x$, $n - k$ above) and multiply the chances.
- For uniforms, $U_{(k)} \sim \text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)$ with mean $\frac{k}{n + 1}$: $n$ points cut $[0, 1]$ into $n + 1$
  gaps of the same average length.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_{(k)}(x) = \frac{n!}{(k-1)! \thinspace (n-k)!} \thinspace F(x)^{k-1} \thinspace f(x) \thinspace \big(1 - F(x)\big)^{n-k}}
$$
