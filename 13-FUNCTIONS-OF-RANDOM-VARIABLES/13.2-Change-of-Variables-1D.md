# 13.2 Change of Variables 1D

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.2 Change of Variables 1D

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [13.1 CDF Method](13.1-CDF-Method.md)

> **Big idea:** For $Y = g(X)$, every $x$ that $g$ sends to $y$ adds its density divided by the local stretch $\lvert g'(x) \rvert$, so a function that folds the line just adds one term per fold.

---

## Why It Matters

Topic [8.8 Change of Variables](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.8-Change-of-Variables.md)
gave a formula for the density of $g(X)$ when $g$ is monotone. Many useful functions
are not monotone: squares (energy, squared error), absolute values (size of an
error), and waves like $\sin$ (signals). They **fold** the line, so several values of
$x$ land on the same $y$. This topic reviews the monotone formula and extends it to
folding functions. It is also the warm-up for the many-variable version with the
Jacobian in [13.3](13.3-Jacobian-Multivariate-Change-of-Variables.md).

## Intuition

A sensor error $X$ is uniform on $[-1, 1]$, with density $\frac{1}{2}$. The loss is the
squared error $Y = X^2$. How dense are the losses near $0.25$?

Look at a thin strip of losses from $0.25$ to $0.26$, of width $0.01$. Which errors give
these losses? Both $x$ near $0.5$ and $x$ near $-0.5$. Near $x = 0.5$ the slope of $x^2$ is
$2x = 1$, so the matching strip of $x$-values also has width about $\frac{0.01}{1} = 0.01$.
It holds probability $\frac{1}{2} \times 0.01 = 0.005$. The strip near $x = -0.5$ holds
another $0.005$. Together: $0.01$ of probability in a $y$-strip of width $0.01$, so the
density of $Y$ near $0.25$ is $\frac{0.01}{0.01} = 1$.

Two ideas did all the work. Each $x$-strip is **stretched** by the slope $\lvert g'(x) \rvert$
when it is mapped to $y$, so its density is divided by that slope. And when $g$ folds
the line, **several** strips land on the same $y$-strip, so their contributions add.

## Picture

```text
   g(x) = x^2
        |  .                                   .
        |    .                               .
    y   +======*===========================*======  strip of y, width dy
        |       .                         .
        |         .                     .
        |            .               .
        |                .  . .  .
        +------[#]-----------+-----------[#]------- x
               x1            0           x2

   strip at x1: width dy / |g'(x1)|      strip at x2: width dy / |g'(x2)|
   f_Y(y) dy  =  f_X(x1) dy / |g'(x1)|  +  f_X(x2) dy / |g'(x2)|
```

Two $x$-strips map onto the same $y$-strip; the probability of the $y$-strip is the
sum of the probabilities of both $x$-strips.

## Definition

### Review: the Monotone Case

If $g$ is strictly monotone and differentiable with inverse $h = g^{-1}$, then
([8.8](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.8-Change-of-Variables.md)):

$$
f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y)) \thinspace \lvert h'(y) \rvert = \frac{f_X(x)}{\lvert g'(x) \rvert} \quad \text{with } x = h(y)
$$

**In words:** go back to the one $x$ that gives $y$, read the density there, and divide
by how much $g$ stretches space at that point.

### The Many-to-One Case

Now let $g$ be **piecewise monotone**: the line can be cut into a few pieces, and on
each piece $g$ is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing. For a value $y$, let
$x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m$ be all the solutions of $g(x) = y$, and assume $g'(x_i) \ne 0$ at
each one. Then:

$$
f_Y(y) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{f_X(x_i)}{\lvert g'(x_i) \rvert}
$$

where:

- $x_1, \dots, x_m$ are the **roots**: all the inputs that $g$ sends to $y$ (their number
  $m$ can change with $y$),
- $f_X(x_i)$ is the density of $X$ at that root (it is 0 if the root lies outside the
  support of $X$),
- $\lvert g'(x_i) \rvert$ is the stretch factor at that root.

**In words:** find every $x$ that produces $y$. Each one contributes its density
divided by the local stretch. Add the contributions.

## Why It Is True

Use the CDF method of [13.1](13.1-CDF-Method.md). Cut the support of $X$ into pieces
$I_1, \dots, I_m$ on which $g$ is monotone, with inverse $h_i$ on piece $I_i$. The events
$X \in I_i$ do not overlap, so the CDF splits into pieces:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_Y(y) &= P(g(X) \le y) = \sum_{i} P\big(g(X) \le y, \ X \in I_i\big) && \text{(the pieces do not overlap)} \\
f_Y(y) &= \sum_{i} \frac{d}{dy} P\big(g(X) \le y, \ X \in I_i\big) && \text{(slope of a sum)} \\
&= \sum_{i} f_X(h_i(y)) \thinspace \lvert h_i'(y) \rvert && \text{(8.8 on each piece)}
\end{aligned}
$$

On piece $I_i$, $g$ is monotone, so each term is exactly the monotone case of 8.8.
A piece contributes only when $y$ is one of its values, and then $h_i(y)$ is the root $x_i$
in that piece. Finally, $h_i'(y) = \frac{1}{g'(x_i)}$ by the slope rule for inverse
functions, which gives the formula in the Definition.

The proof also shows when the formula fails: if $g$ is **flat** on an interval (for
example $g(x) = \max(x, 0)$ for $x \lt 0$), then $Y$ has a point mass there, and a density
alone cannot describe it ([8.9](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md)).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Square of a Standard Normal

**Problem.** $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, with density $\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}$.
Find the density of $Y = Z^2$ with the many-to-one formula.

**Solution.**

1. $Y$ lives on $(0, \infty)$. For $y \gt 0$, $z^2 = y$ has two roots, $z_1 = -\sqrt{y}$ and $z_2 = \sqrt{y}$.
2. The slope is $g'(z) = 2z$, so $\lvert g'(z_1) \rvert = \lvert g'(z_2) \rvert = 2\sqrt{y}$.
3. The density is symmetric, so $\varphi(-\sqrt{y}) = \varphi(\sqrt{y}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-y/2}$.
4. Add the two terms:

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{\varphi(-\sqrt{y})}{2\sqrt{y}} + \frac{\varphi(\sqrt{y})}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{2}{2\sqrt{y}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-y/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}} e^{-y/2}
$$

**Answer.** $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}} e^{-y/2}$ for $y \gt 0$.

**Check.** This is the chi-square density with 1 degree of freedom, the same answer
that [9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md) found with
the CDF method. With only the root $\sqrt{y}$, the density would integrate to $\frac{1}{2}$.

### Example 2: A Root Outside the Support

**Problem.** $X$ is uniform on $(-1, 2)$, with density $\frac{1}{3}$. Find the density of $Y = X^2$
with the formula, and compare with Example 2 of [13.1](13.1-CDF-Method.md).

**Solution.**

1. $Y$ lives on $[0, 4]$. The roots of $x^2 = y$ are $-\sqrt{y}$ and $\sqrt{y}$, and $\lvert g'(\pm\sqrt{y}) \rvert = 2\sqrt{y}$.
2. **For $0 \lt y \lt 1$:** both roots are inside $(-1, 2)$, so both have density $\frac{1}{3}$:

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{1/3}{2\sqrt{y}} + \frac{1/3}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{2}{6\sqrt{y}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{y}}
$$

3. **For $1 \lt y \lt 4$:** the root $-\sqrt{y}$ is below $-1$, outside the support, so
   $f_X(-\sqrt{y}) = 0$. Only $\sqrt{y}$ counts:

$$
f_Y(y) = 0 + \frac{1/3}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{1}{6\sqrt{y}}
$$

**Answer.** $\frac{1}{3\sqrt{y}}$ on $(0, 1)$ and $\frac{1}{6\sqrt{y}}$ on $(1, 4)$, the same as
the CDF method gave.

**Check.** Area: $\int_0^1 \frac{1}{3\sqrt{y}} dy = \frac{2}{3}$ and
$\int_1^4 \frac{1}{6\sqrt{y}} dy = \frac{1}{3}(\sqrt{4} - \sqrt{1}) = \frac{1}{3}$. Total 1. The formula is
faster, but you must check each root against the support.

### Example 3: A Signal Sampled at a Random Time

**Problem.** A radio signal is $\sin(t)$. A receiver samples it at a random moment, so the
phase $\Theta$ is uniform on $(0, 2\pi)$, with density $\frac{1}{2\pi}$. Find the density of the
sampled value $Y = \sin \Theta$, and the chance that $\lvert Y \rvert \gt 0.9$.

**Solution.**

1. $Y$ lives on $[-1, 1]$. For $-1 \lt y \lt 1$, the equation $\sin \theta = y$ has exactly two
   roots in $(0, 2\pi)$: one where the wave goes up through $y$, one where it comes down.
2. The slope is $g'(\theta) = \cos \theta$. At both roots $\sin \theta = y$, so
   $\lvert \cos \theta \rvert = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 \theta} = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$.
3. Add the two equal terms:

$$
f_Y(y) = 2 \cdot \frac{1/(2\pi)}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}} = \frac{1}{\pi \sqrt{1 - y^2}}, \qquad -1 \lt y \lt 1
$$

4. For the tail, integrate. The integral of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}}$ is $\operatorname{arcsin} y$, so the
   CDF is $F_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{arcsin} y$. By symmetry:

$$
P(\lvert Y \rvert \gt 0.9) = 2 \big(1 - F_Y(0.9)\big) = 1 - \frac{2}{\pi} \operatorname{arcsin}(0.9) \approx 1 - \frac{2 \times 1.1198}{3.1416} \approx 0.2871
$$

**Answer.** $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1 - y^2}}$ on $(-1, 1)$, the **arcsine distribution**;
$P(\lvert Y \rvert \gt 0.9) \approx 0.287$.

**Check.** The values with $\lvert y \rvert \gt 0.9$ fill only 10% of the range $[-1, 1]$,
yet they hold about 29% of the probability. The wave moves slowly near its peaks
($\cos \theta$ is near 0), so it spends more time there. The density is lowest at
$y = 0$, where it equals $\frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.318$.

## Simulate It

We check Example 3: the density of $\sin \Theta$ at three points (by counting samples in a
thin strip), and the tail probability.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 10_000_000

# Random phase: Y = sin(Theta), Theta uniform on (0, 2 pi).
y = np.sin(rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n_trials))

h = 0.02                                     # strip width
for point in [0.0, 0.5, 0.9]:
    in_strip = np.abs(y - point) <= h / 2
    exact = 1 / (np.pi * np.sqrt(1 - point**2))
    print(f"f_Y({point}): sim {in_strip.mean() / h:.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

exact_tail = 1 - 2 * np.arcsin(0.9) / np.pi
print(f"P(|Y| > 0.9): sim {np.mean(np.abs(y) > 0.9):.4f}, exact {exact_tail:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
f_Y(0.0): sim 0.3198, exact 0.3183
f_Y(0.5): sim 0.3685, exact 0.3676
f_Y(0.9): sim 0.7297, exact 0.7303
P(|Y| > 0.9): sim 0.2873, exact 0.2871
```

The density more than doubles from the middle to $y = 0.9$, just as the formula says.

## Common Mistakes

- **Missing a root.** For $Y = X^2$ with $X$ symmetric, using only $\sqrt{y}$ gives half the
  right density. List all the roots before you compute anything.
- **Keeping a root outside the support.** In Example 2, the root $-\sqrt{y}$ for $y \gt 1$ has
  $f_X = 0$. Count it as zero, do not use the density from the formula of $X$ blindly.
- **Forgetting the absolute value.** On a decreasing piece $g'(x_i) \lt 0$. Without
  $\lvert \cdot \rvert$ some terms come out negative.
- **Using the formula where $g$ is flat.** A flat piece sends a whole interval to one
  value: that is a point mass, not a density. Use the CDF method.
- **Worrying about single points where $g' = 0$.** At $y = 0$ for $x^2$, or $y = \pm 1$ for
  $\sin$, the density may be infinite. One point has no probability, so the density
  can be left undefined there.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** the squared error $(Y - \hat{y})^2$ of a model with normal
  noise has a scaled chi-square distribution (Example 1). Normalizing flows use the
  monotone formula in 1D and the Jacobian in many dimensions.
- **Signal processing:** the arcsine law of Example 3 describes a sampled sine wave, and is
  used to test analog-to-digital converters.
- **Physics:** a speed $v$ and a kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2} m v^2$ are linked by a fold, so the
  energy density has one term for each direction of motion.
- **Statistics:** the absolute value of a normal error, $\lvert Z \rvert$, is the **half-normal**
  distribution (Practice 1), used for sizes of errors.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find the density of $Y = \lvert Z \rvert$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \gt 0$ the roots of $\lvert z \rvert = y$ are $\pm y$, and the slope has size 1 at both:

$$f_Y(y) = \varphi(-y) + \varphi(y) = 2\varphi(y) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}} e^{-y^2/2}, \qquad y \gt 0$$

This is the half-normal distribution.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ is uniform on $[-1, 1]$. Find the density of $Y = X^4$ and check that it
integrates to 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $0 \lt y \lt 1$ the roots are $\pm y^{1/4}$, both inside the support, and $\lvert g'(x) \rvert = 4\lvert x \rvert^3 = 4y^{3/4}$:

$$f_Y(y) = 2 \cdot \frac{1/2}{4 y^{3/4}} = \frac{1}{4} y^{-3/4}, \qquad \int_0^1 \tfrac{1}{4} y^{-3/4} \thinspace dy = \left[ y^{1/4} \right]_0^1 = 1$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (rate 1, mean 1). Find the density of $Y = (X - 1)^2$, the
squared distance from the mean. Be careful: how many roots are in the support?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The roots of $(x - 1)^2 = y$ are $1 - \sqrt{y}$ and $1 + \sqrt{y}$, and $\lvert g'(x) \rvert = 2\lvert x - 1 \rvert = 2\sqrt{y}$ at both. The root $1 - \sqrt{y}$ is in the support $[0, \infty)$ only when $y \le 1$. So:

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{e^{-(1 - \sqrt{y})} + e^{-(1 + \sqrt{y})}}{2\sqrt{y}} \ \text{ for } 0 \lt y \lt 1, \qquad f_Y(y) = \frac{e^{-(1 + \sqrt{y})}}{2\sqrt{y}} \ \text{ for } y \gt 1$$

For example, $P(Y \gt 4) = P(X \gt 3) = e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$, since $X \ge 0$ rules out $X \lt -1$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** $\Theta$ is uniform on $(0, 2\pi)$. Show that $Y = \cos \Theta$ has the same
arcsine density as in Example 3, and find $P(Y \gt 0.5)$ in two ways.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\cos \theta = y$ has two roots in $(0, 2\pi)$, and at both $\lvert -\sin \theta \rvert = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$, so $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1 - y^2}}$ again. With the CDF $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{arcsin} y$:

$$P(Y \gt 0.5) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{arcsin}(0.5) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{\pi} \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{6} = \tfrac{1}{3}$$

Directly: $\cos \theta \gt 0.5$ for $\theta$ in $(0, \frac{\pi}{3})$ or $(\frac{5\pi}{3}, 2\pi)$, a total length of $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ out of $2\pi$, which is $\frac{1}{3}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $X$ have any continuous density $f_X$. Prove with the CDF method that
$Y = X^2$ has density $f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(\sqrt{y}) + f_X(-\sqrt{y})}{2\sqrt{y}}$ for $y \gt 0$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $y \gt 0$, $X^2 \le y$ means $-\sqrt{y} \le X \le \sqrt{y}$, so $F_Y(y) = F_X(\sqrt{y}) - F_X(-\sqrt{y})$. Take the slope with the chain rule, using $\frac{d}{dy}\sqrt{y} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$:

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(\sqrt{y}) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} - f_X(-\sqrt{y}) \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} \right) = \frac{f_X(\sqrt{y}) + f_X(-\sqrt{y})}{2\sqrt{y}}$$

This is the many-to-one formula with two roots.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 3: estimate $P(Y \le 0.25)$ and $P(Y \gt 4)$. (Integrating
the density gives $P(Y \le 0.25) = P(0.5 \le X \le 1.5) = e^{-0.5} - e^{-1.5}$.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
y = (rng.exponential(1.0, 1_000_000) - 1) ** 2      # Y = (X - 1)^2, X ~ Exp(1)

print(f"P(Y <= 0.25): simulation {np.mean(y <= 0.25):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
print(f"P(Y > 4):     simulation {np.mean(y > 4):.4f}, exact {np.exp(-3):.4f}")
```

```text
P(Y <= 0.25): simulation 0.3828, exact 0.3834
P(Y > 4):     simulation 0.0496, exact 0.0498
```

</details>

## Summary

- For a monotone $g$, the density of $Y = g(X)$ is the density of $X$ at the one matching
  point, divided by the stretch $\lvert g'(x) \rvert$ (review of 8.8).
- When $g$ folds the line, several $x$-values give the same $y$: add one term per root.
- A root outside the support of $X$ contributes 0. A flat piece of $g$ makes a point mass,
  and then you need the CDF method.
- The formula is a shortcut for the CDF method of 13.1, applied piece by piece.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_Y(y) = \sum_{x : \thinspace g(x) = y} \frac{f_X(x)}{\lvert g'(x) \rvert}}
$$
