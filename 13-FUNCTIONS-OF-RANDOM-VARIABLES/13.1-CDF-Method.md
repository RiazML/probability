# 13.1 CDF Method

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.1 CDF Method

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 12: The Multivariate Gaussian](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/README.md)

> **Big idea:** To find the distribution of $Y = g(X)$, rewrite the event $g(X) \le y$ as an event about $X$, compute its probability to get the CDF of $Y$, and then take the slope to get the density.

---

## Why It Matters

You often know the distribution of one quantity but need the distribution of
something computed from it: a distance from a position, an energy from a speed, a
loss from a prediction error, the slowest of several jobs. The **CDF method** is the
one tool that always works, for any function, any number of input variables, and
discrete, continuous or mixed answers. Every other method in this module (the
change-of-variables formula, convolution, the max and min rules) is a shortcut
that grew out of it.

## Intuition

You throw a dart at a round board of radius 1 meter, and it lands at a uniformly
random point. How far from the center does it land? A first guess: "the distance
is uniform on 0 to 1 meter". Test it. The dart lands within half a meter of the
center when it hits the inner disk of radius 0.5. That inner disk has area
$\pi (0.5)^2$, which is one quarter of the full area $\pi (1)^2$. So the chance is
0.25, not 0.5.

The trick was simple. We did not try to find the density of the distance directly.
We asked a **"less than or equal"** question, "is the distance at most 0.5?", and
turned it into a question about where the dart lands. Where the dart lands is
something we know. Do the same for every radius $r$: the inner disk of radius $r$
has area $\pi r^2$, so $P(\text{distance} \le r) = r^2$. That is the whole CDF of the
distance. Its slope, $2r$, is the density. Distances near 1 are the most common,
because the outer rings of the board hold the most area.

## Picture

```text
   y = g(x) = x^2
        |
    4 + .                                 .
        |  .                            .
        |    .                        .
    y +-------*======================*-------  horizontal line at height y
        |        .                  .
        |           .            .
        |               .  ..  .
    0 +-----------------+--+--+------------------ x
                      -√y  0  √y

   g(X) <= y   is the same event as   -√y <= X <= √y   (the === part)
```

The event that $g(X)$ is below the line is a set of $x$-values; its probability is
the CDF of $Y$ at $y$.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a random variable (or a random vector) and let $Y = g(X)$. The **CDF
method** finds the distribution of $Y$ in three steps:

1. **Write the event.** For each $y$, find the set of inputs that $g$ sends to $y$ or below:

$$
A_y = \lbrace x : g(x) \le y \rbrace
$$

2. **Compute its probability.** This gives the CDF of $Y$:

$$
F_Y(y) = P(Y \le y) = P(g(X) \le y) = P(X \in A_y)
$$

3. **Take the slope.** If $Y$ is continuous, its density is $f_Y(y) = F_Y'(y)$.

where:

- $g$ is any function: it does not need to be monotone, smooth or one-to-one,
- $A_y$ is a set of $x$-values (an interval, a union of intervals, or a region in
  the plane when $X$ is a random vector),
- $P(X \in A_y)$ is an area under the density of $X$, or a double integral of a joint
  density over a region ([10.1 Joint PMF and PDF](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md)).

**In words:** the chance that $Y$ is at most $y$ is the chance that $X$ lands in the
set of inputs that $g$ maps to $y$ or below. Find that chance for every $y$ and you
have the CDF of $Y$.

Before step 1, always find the **support** of $Y$: the set of values $Y$ can take.
Outside it the CDF is 0 (below) or 1 (above), and the density is 0.

## Why It Is True

The method uses only two facts you already know.

**Fact 1: the two events are the same event.** Fix an outcome $\omega$. Then
$g(X(\omega)) \le y$ is true exactly when $X(\omega)$ is one of the inputs in $A_y$, by
the definition of $A_y$. So the two events contain the same outcomes:

$$
\lbrace \omega : g(X(\omega)) \le y \rbrace = \lbrace \omega : X(\omega) \in A_y \rbrace
$$

Equal events have equal probabilities, so $F_Y(y) = P(X \in A_y)$.

**Fact 2: the density is the slope of the CDF.** This is the relationship from
[8.4 CDF and PDF Relationship](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.4-CDF-and-PDF-Relationship.md),
applied to $Y$.

**The monotone formula is a special case.** If $g$ is increasing with inverse $h$,
then $A_y = \lbrace x : x \le h(y) \rbrace$, so $F_Y(y) = F_X(h(y))$, and the chain rule
gives $f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y)) \thinspace h'(y)$. That is exactly the change-of-variables
formula of [8.8 Change of Variables](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.8-Change-of-Variables.md).
The CDF method is more general because it never needs an inverse: it only needs
the set $A_y$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Distance of a Dart From the Center

**Problem.** A dart lands at a uniform random point $(X, Y)$ in the disk of radius 1.
Find the CDF and density of its distance $R = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}$ from the center, and
find $E[R]$.

**Solution.**

1. Support: $R$ is between 0 and 1.
2. The joint density is constant: $\frac{1}{\pi}$ on the disk, since the disk has area $\pi$.
3. The event $R \le r$ is the event that the dart lands in the inner disk of radius $r$.
   Its probability is the area of that disk times the density:

$$
F_R(r) = P(R \le r) = \pi r^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\pi} = r^2, \qquad 0 \le r \le 1
$$

4. Take the slope:

$$
f_R(r) = \frac{d}{dr} r^2 = 2r, \qquad 0 \le r \le 1
$$

5. The mean, with the integral formula of
   [8.5](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md):

$$
E[R] = \int_0^1 r \cdot 2r \thinspace dr = \left[ \frac{2r^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3}
$$

**Answer.** $F_R(r) = r^2$ and $f_R(r) = 2r$ on $[0, 1]$, and $E[R] = \frac{2}{3}$.

**Check.** $F_R(0.5) = 0.25$, the number from the Intuition. The mean is above
$0.5$, because the outer rings hold more area than the inner ones.

### Example 2: Squaring a Number That Is Not Symmetric

**Problem.** $X$ is uniform on $(-1, 2)$, so its density is $\frac{1}{3}$ there. Find the
density of $Y = X^2$, and $P(Y \le 2)$.

**Solution.**

1. Support: $X^2$ is between 0 (when $X = 0$) and 4 (when $X = 2$). So $Y$ lives on $[0, 4]$.
2. The event: for $y \ge 0$, $X^2 \le y$ means $-\sqrt{y} \le X \le \sqrt{y}$. But $X$ cannot go
   below $-1$. So there are two cases.
3. **Case $0 \le y \le 1$.** Then $-\sqrt{y} \ge -1$, and the whole interval fits inside $(-1, 2)$:

$$
F_Y(y) = P(-\sqrt{y} \le X \le \sqrt{y}) = \frac{2\sqrt{y}}{3}, \qquad f_Y(y) = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{y}}
$$

4. **Case $1 \lt y \le 4$.** Now $-\sqrt{y} \lt -1$, so the left end is cut at $-1$:

$$
F_Y(y) = P(-1 \lt X \le \sqrt{y}) = \frac{\sqrt{y} + 1}{3}, \qquad f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{1}{6\sqrt{y}}
$$

5. For $P(Y \le 2)$, use the second case with $y = 2$:

$$
P(Y \le 2) = \frac{\sqrt{2} + 1}{3} \approx \frac{2.4142}{3} \approx 0.8047
$$

**Answer.** $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{y}}$ on $(0, 1]$ and $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{6\sqrt{y}}$ on $(1, 4]$;
$P(Y \le 2) \approx 0.805$.

**Check.** The two CDF pieces agree at $y = 1$: $\frac{2}{3}$ and $\frac{1 + 1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.
At $y = 4$ the CDF is $\frac{2 + 1}{3} = 1$. The density halves at $y = 1$, because above 1
only the positive side of $X$ can produce the value.

### Example 3: Distance Between Two Random Records

**Problem.** A file has length 1. Two records sit at independent uniform positions
$U_1$ and $U_2$. The disk head moves from one to the other, a distance
$D = \lvert U_1 - U_2 \rvert$. Find the CDF and density of $D$, and $E[D]$.

**Solution.**

1. Support: $D$ is between 0 and 1. The pair $(U_1, U_2)$ is uniform on the unit
   square, with joint density 1, so a probability is just an area.
2. It is easier to find $P(D \gt d)$. The event $\lvert U_1 - U_2 \rvert \gt d$ is two
   corner triangles of the square (see the picture below): $U_2 - U_1 \gt d$ and
   $U_1 - U_2 \gt d$. Each triangle has two sides of length $1 - d$:

$$
P(D \gt d) = 2 \cdot \frac{(1 - d)^2}{2} = (1 - d)^2
$$

3. So the CDF and the density are, for $0 \le d \le 1$:

$$
F_D(d) = 1 - (1 - d)^2, \qquad f_D(d) = 2(1 - d)
$$

4. The mean (we call the integration variable $t$):

$$
E[D] = \int_0^1 t \cdot 2(1 - t) \thinspace dt = \int_0^1 (2t - 2t^2) \thinspace dt = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

**Answer.** $F_D(d) = 1 - (1 - d)^2$, $f_D(d) = 2(1 - d)$ on $[0, 1]$, and $E[D] = \frac{1}{3}$.

```text
   U2
  1 +--------------------+
    |#######             |    ### : U2 - U1 > d   (area (1-d)^2 / 2)
    |#####               |    ooo : U1 - U2 > d   (area (1-d)^2 / 2)
    |###                 |
    |#       band        |    the white band around the diagonal
    |       D <= d      o|    is the event  D <= d
    |                 ooo|
    |               ooooo|
    |             ooooooo|
  0 +--------------------+ U1
    0                    1
```

The two corner triangles are the event $D \gt d$; the band between them is $D \le d$.

**Check.** Short moves are the most common: the density $2(1 - d)$ is highest at
$d = 0$. On average the head travels one third of the file, a classic result in
disk scheduling. The simulation below agrees.

## Simulate It

We check Examples 2 and 3: the CDF of $X^2$ at two points, and the CDF and mean of
$\lvert U_1 - U_2 \rvert$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Example 2: X uniform on (-1, 2), Y = X^2.
y = rng.uniform(-1, 2, n_trials) ** 2
for point in [0.5, 2.0]:
    exact = 2 * np.sqrt(point) / 3 if point <= 1 else (np.sqrt(point) + 1) / 3
    print(f"P(Y <= {point}): sim {np.mean(y <= point):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")

# Example 3: D = |U1 - U2|, claim F(d) = 1 - (1 - d)^2 and E[D] = 1/3.
d = np.abs(rng.random(n_trials) - rng.random(n_trials))
for point in [0.1, 0.5]:
    print(f"P(D <= {point}): sim {np.mean(d <= point):.4f}, "
          f"exact {1 - (1 - point) ** 2:.4f}")
print(f"E[D]:          sim {d.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 3:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(Y <= 0.5): sim 0.4720, exact 0.4714
P(Y <= 2.0): sim 0.8047, exact 0.8047
P(D <= 0.1): sim 0.1900, exact 0.1900
P(D <= 0.5): sim 0.7498, exact 0.7500
E[D]:          sim 0.3336, exact 0.3333
```

Every simulated value matches the CDF method to about three decimal places.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a non-monotone $g$ as monotone.** Writing $F_Y(y) = F_X(\sqrt{y})$ for
  $Y = X^2$ forgets the negative values of $X$. Always write the set $A_y$ first.
- **Forgetting the edges of the support.** In Example 2 the interval
  $[-\sqrt{y}, \sqrt{y}]$ is cut at $-1$ once $y \gt 1$. Draw the picture and check
  whether the set $A_y$ runs past the support of $X$.
- **Differentiating a CDF that has a jump.** If $g$ is flat on a region, such as
  $Y = \max(X, 0)$, then $Y$ has a point mass, and the slope of the CDF misses it. Keep
  the CDF, or treat $Y$ as a mixed variable
  ([8.9 Mixed Distributions](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.9-Mixed-Distributions.md)).
- **Skipping the support of $Y$.** A density without its range is incomplete.
  $2(1 - d)$ is a density only on $[0, 1]$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Statistics:** the chi-square distribution comes from $Z^2$ by exactly this method
  ([9.6 Chi-Square, Student's t and F](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** for a uniform point in a ball in $d$ dimensions,
  $P(R \le r) = r^d$, so almost all points sit near the surface when $d$ is large.
  This is one face of the "curse of dimensionality" (Practice 3).
- **Computer science:** the slowest of several parallel jobs and the fastest of
  several servers are maxima and minima, handled with the CDF method in
  [13.6 Max and Min](13.6-Max-and-Min.md).
- **Engineering:** the distribution of a power $V^2 / R$ or an energy
  $\frac{1}{2} m v^2$ from a random voltage or speed.
- **Everyday life:** two friends each arrive at a uniform time in the same 10-minute
  window; Example 3 (scaled by 10) gives how long the first one waits.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. Use the CDF method to find the density of
$Y = X^3$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Y$ lives on $[0, 1]$. For $0 \le y \le 1$, $X^3 \le y$ means $X \le y^{1/3}$:

$$F_Y(y) = P(X \le y^{1/3}) = y^{1/3}, \qquad f_Y(y) = \tfrac{1}{3} y^{-2/3}$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ is continuous with a strictly increasing CDF $F$. Show that
$U = F(X)$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. (This is called the **probability integral
transform**.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$U$ lives on $[0, 1]$. For $0 \lt u \lt 1$, $F$ is increasing, so $F(X) \le u$ means $X \le F^{-1}(u)$:

$$P(U \le u) = P(X \le F^{-1}(u)) = F(F^{-1}(u)) = u$$

That is the CDF of $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. It is the reverse of the inverse transform
trick in [8.7 Quantile Function](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.7-Quantile-Function.md).

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A point is uniform in a ball of radius 1 in $d$ dimensions. The volume of
a ball of radius $r$ is $c_d r^d$ for some constant $c_d$. Find the CDF of the distance $R$
to the center. For $d = 10$, find $P(R \gt 0.9)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

As in Example 1, $P(R \le r)$ is the volume of the inner ball divided by the volume of the whole ball:

$$F_R(r) = \frac{c_d r^d}{c_d \cdot 1^d} = r^d, \qquad 0 \le r \le 1$$

For $d = 10$:

$$P(R \gt 0.9) = 1 - 0.9^{10} \approx 1 - 0.3487 = 0.6513$$

About 65% of the points lie in the thin outer shell between 0.9 and 1.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** A call lasts $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ minutes (here $\lambda$ is the rate,
so the mean is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$). The phone company counts only full minutes,
$Y = \lfloor X \rfloor$. Find $P(Y = k)$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ What distribution is $Y + 1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$Y = k$ exactly when $k \le X \lt k + 1$. With $F_X(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$:

$$P(Y = k) = F_X(k + 1) - F_X(k) = e^{-\lambda k} - e^{-\lambda (k+1)} = (e^{-\lambda})^k (1 - e^{-\lambda})$$

Write $p = 1 - e^{-\lambda}$. Then $P(Y + 1 = m) = (1 - p)^{m-1} p$ for $m = 1, 2, \dots$, so
$Y + 1 \sim \text{Geom}(1 - e^{-\lambda})$. The CDF method works for discrete answers too.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $U_1$ and $U_2$ are independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$. Find the CDF and
density of the product $W = U_1 U_2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$W$ lives on $(0, 1)$. Fix $U_1 = u$: then $W \le w$ means $U_2 \le \frac{w}{u}$, which has probability $\min(1, \frac{w}{u})$. This is 1 when $u \le w$. Average over $u$ (this is the area under the curve $u_2 = w / u_1$ in the unit square):

$$F_W(w) = \int_0^w 1 \thinspace du + \int_w^1 \frac{w}{u} \thinspace du = w + w(\ln 1 - \ln w) = w - w \ln w$$

Take the slope with the product rule:

$$f_W(w) = 1 - \left( \ln w + w \cdot \tfrac{1}{w} \right) = -\ln w, \qquad 0 \lt w \lt 1$$

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 5 at $w = 0.1$ and $w = 0.5$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
w = rng.random(1_000_000) * rng.random(1_000_000)   # W = U1 U2
for point in [0.1, 0.5]:
    exact = point - point * np.log(point)
    print(f"P(W <= {point}): simulation {np.mean(w <= point):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(W <= 0.1): simulation 0.3304, exact 0.3303
P(W <= 0.5): simulation 0.8468, exact 0.8466
```

</details>

## Summary

- To find the distribution of $Y = g(X)$, ask "when is $g(X) \le y$?" and turn the answer
  into a set $A_y$ of $X$-values.
- The CDF of $Y$ is $P(X \in A_y)$: an area, a sum, or a double integral. The density
  is its slope.
- Find the support of $Y$ first, and watch for cases where $A_y$ hits the edge of the
  support of $X$.
- The method works for any $g$, for several input variables, and for discrete or mixed
  answers. The monotone formula of 8.8 is a special case.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{F_Y(y) = P(g(X) \le y) = P\big(X \in \lbrace x : g(x) \le y \rbrace\big), \qquad f_Y(y) = F_Y'(y)}
$$
