# 13.6 Max and Min

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.6 Max and Min

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [13.5 Sums of Known Distributions](13.5-Sums-of-Known-Distributions.md)

> **Big idea:** The largest value is below $t$ only when every value is below $t$, and the smallest is above $t$ only when every value is above $t$, so for independent variables both distributions are products of the single ones.

---

## Why It Matters

Many real questions are about the **largest** or the **smallest** of several random
quantities. A parallel job finishes when its **slowest** task finishes. A chain breaks at its
**weakest** link. An auction is won by the **highest** bid. A dam must survive the
**highest** flood in 100 years. A request sent to three servers is answered by the
**fastest** one. Sums need convolutions, but maxima and minima need only one clever
step with the CDF, and the answers are short and exact.

## Intuition

You send the same request to 3 servers and use the first answer. Each server answers
within 100 ms with probability $0.8$, independently. What is the chance you wait more than
100 ms? Only if **all three** are slow, which has probability $0.2 \times 0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.008$.
Asking three servers turned a 20% chance of a slow answer into less than 1%. The
fastest answer is the **minimum**, and the minimum is above $t$ only when every one is above $t$.

Now a job is split into 3 parallel tasks, and it ends when all are done. Each task ends
within 100 ms with probability $0.8$. The job ends within 100 ms only if **all three** do:
$0.8^3 = 0.512$. Splitting the job made a slow finish much **more** likely. The job time is
the **maximum**, and the maximum is at most $t$ only when every one is at most $t$.

## Picture

```text
   max <= t   happens exactly when ALL values are at or left of t:

        X3        X1          X5    X2   X4    |
   -----*---------*-----------*-----*----*-----|------------->
                                               t

   min > t    happens exactly when ALL values are right of t:

                     |
   ------------------|----*-------*---*-----------*-----*---->
                     t    X2      X4  X1          X5    X3
```

One event about the maximum (or minimum) is the same as $n$ events about the single
values, all happening together.

## Definition

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be **independent**, with CDFs $F_1, \dots, F_n$. Let
$M = \max(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ be the largest and $L = \min(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ the smallest. Then:

$$
F_M(t) = P(M \le t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} F_i(t), \qquad P(L \gt t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \big(1 - F_i(t)\big)
$$

**In words:** the CDF of the maximum is the product of the CDFs. The survival
function (the chance of being above $t$) of the minimum is the product of the survival
functions.

When the $X_i$ are **i.i.d.** with CDF $F$ and density $f$, this becomes:

$$
F_M(t) = F(t)^n, \quad f_M(t) = n F(t)^{n-1} f(t), \qquad F_L(t) = 1 - \big(1 - F(t)\big)^n, \quad f_L(t) = n \big(1 - F(t)\big)^{n-1} f(t)
$$

where:

- $F(t)^n$ is the chance that all $n$ values are at most $t$,
- $\big(1 - F(t)\big)^n$ is the chance that all $n$ values are above $t$,
- the densities are the slopes of the CDFs.

**In words:** the maximum is near $t$ when one value is near $t$ (density $f(t)$, and there
are $n$ choices for which one) and the other $n - 1$ are below it.

## Why It Is True

**Maximum.** The largest value is at most $t$ exactly when every value is at most $t$.
So the two events are equal, and then independence turns the joint probability into a
product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(M \le t) &= P(X_1 \le t, \ X_2 \le t, \dots, X_n \le t) && \text{(same event)} \\
&= P(X_1 \le t) \cdots P(X_n \le t) = F_1(t) \cdots F_n(t) && \text{(independence)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Minimum.** The smallest value is above $t$ exactly when every value is above $t$:

$$
P(L \gt t) = P(X_1 \gt t, \dots, X_n \gt t) = \big(1 - F_1(t)\big) \cdots \big(1 - F_n(t)\big)
$$

**Densities.** For i.i.d. values, take the slope with the chain rule:
$\frac{d}{dt} F(t)^n = n F(t)^{n-1} F'(t) = n F(t)^{n-1} f(t)$, and
$\frac{d}{dt}\left[ 1 - (1 - F(t))^n \right] = n (1 - F(t))^{n-1} f(t)$.

This is the CDF method of [13.1](13.1-CDF-Method.md) with a lucky event: the event $M \le t$ is
an intersection, and independence makes intersections easy. The i.i.d. version first
appeared in [10.6 IID](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.6-IID.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Roll Two Dice, Keep the Higher

**Problem.** In a board game you roll two fair dice and keep the higher number. Find the
PMF of the kept number $M$, $P(M = 6)$ and $E[M]$.

**Solution.**

1. Each die has $P(X \le k) = \frac{k}{6}$ for $k = 1, \dots, 6$. So $P(M \le k) = \left(\frac{k}{6}\right)^2 = \frac{k^2}{36}$.
2. For a whole-number variable, $P(M = k) = P(M \le k) - P(M \le k - 1)$:

$$
P(M = k) = \frac{k^2 - (k - 1)^2}{36} = \frac{2k - 1}{36}
$$

   So the PMF is $\frac{1}{36}, \frac{3}{36}, \frac{5}{36}, \frac{7}{36}, \frac{9}{36}, \frac{11}{36}$ for $k = 1, \dots, 6$.
3. $P(M = 6) = \frac{11}{36} \approx 0.306$.
4. The mean:

$$
E[M] = \frac{1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot 7 + 5 \cdot 9 + 6 \cdot 11}{36} = \frac{1 + 6 + 15 + 28 + 45 + 66}{36} = \frac{161}{36} \approx 4.47
$$

**Answer.** $P(M = k) = \frac{2k - 1}{36}$, $P(M = 6) = \frac{11}{36} \approx 0.306$, and $E[M] \approx 4.47$.

**Check.** The PMF adds to $\frac{1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11}{36} = \frac{36}{36} = 1$. The mean is well above
$3.5$, the mean of one die. $P(M = 6) = 1 - (\frac{5}{6})^2$ also: "at least one six".

### Example 2: The Highest and Lowest of Uniform Bids

**Problem.** Four bidders in an auction each bid an independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ amount (in
millions of taka). Find the densities and means of the highest bid $M$ and the lowest bid $L$.

**Solution.**

1. For $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$, $F(t) = t$ and $f(t) = 1$ on $[0, 1]$. With $n = 4$:

$$
f_M(t) = 4 t^3, \qquad f_L(t) = 4 (1 - t)^3, \qquad 0 \le t \le 1
$$

2. The mean of the maximum:

$$
E[M] = \int_0^1 t \cdot 4t^3 \thinspace dt = \left[ \frac{4t^5}{5} \right]_0^1 = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8
$$

3. For the minimum, use symmetry: $1 - U_i$ is also uniform, and $\min(U_i) = 1 - \max(1 - U_i)$.
   So $E[L] = 1 - E[M] = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2$.

**Answer.** $f_M(t) = 4t^3$ with $E[M] = 0.8$, and $f_L(t) = 4(1 - t)^3$ with $E[L] = 0.2$.

**Check.** In general $E[\max] = \frac{n}{n + 1}$ and $E[\min] = \frac{1}{n + 1}$: the four bids cut
$[0, 1]$ into five gaps of the same average length $\frac{1}{5}$. Topic
[13.7 Order Statistics](13.7-Order-Statistics.md) explains why, and finds the second and
third highest bids too.

### Example 3: Waiting for the Slowest Task

**Problem.** A job is split into $n = 10$ parallel tasks. Each takes an independent
$\text{Exp}(1)$ time in seconds (rate 1, so the mean is 1 second). The job ends when all tasks end.
Find the mean job time $E[M]$, the chance that the job takes more than 5 seconds, and the
mean time until the **first** task ends.

**Solution.**

1. **First task.** The minimum survives past $t$ only if all 10 do:
   $P(L \gt t) = (e^{-t})^{10} = e^{-10t}$. So $L \sim \text{Exp}(10)$, with mean $\frac{1}{10} = 0.1$ second
   (as in [9.3 Exponential](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.3-Exponential.md)).
2. **Mean of the maximum.** After the first task ends, 9 tasks are still running. By the
   **memoryless** property of the exponential, each of them has a fresh $\text{Exp}(1)$ time
   left. So the gap until the next end is the minimum of 9, an $\text{Exp}(9)$ time, with mean $\frac{1}{9}$.
   Then $\frac{1}{8}$, and so on, down to $\frac{1}{1}$ for the last task. The job time is the sum of
   these gaps, so by linearity of expectation:

$$
E[M] = \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{9} + \dots + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{1} \approx 2.929
$$

3. **The tail.** With $F(t) = 1 - e^{-t}$:

$$
P(M \gt 5) = 1 - (1 - e^{-5})^{10} \approx 1 - (0.993262)^{10} \approx 1 - 0.93463 \approx 0.0654
$$

**Answer.** $E[M] \approx 2.93$ seconds, $P(M \gt 5) \approx 0.065$, and the first task ends after
$0.1$ second on average.

**Check.** One task alone takes more than 5 seconds with probability $e^{-5} \approx 0.0067$, but
with 10 tasks the job does so almost 10 times as often. The mean job time is nearly 3
times the mean task time and grows like $\ln n$. This is the **straggler problem** of
parallel computing.

## Simulate It

We check Example 1 (two dice) and Example 3 (ten parallel tasks).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# Example 1: the larger of two fair dice.
best = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(n_trials, 2)).max(axis=1)
print(f"P(max = 6): sim {np.mean(best == 6):.4f}, exact {11 / 36:.4f}")
print(f"E[max]:     sim {best.mean():.4f}, exact {161 / 36:.4f}")

# Example 3: 10 parallel tasks, each Exp(1) seconds; the job waits for all.
tasks = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_trials, 10))
job, first = tasks.max(axis=1), tasks.min(axis=1)
harmonic = sum(1 / k for k in range(1, 11))
print(f"E[max]:     sim {job.mean():.4f}, exact {harmonic:.4f}")
print(f"P(max > 5): sim {np.mean(job > 5):.4f}, "
      f"exact {1 - (1 - np.exp(-5)) ** 10:.4f}")
print(f"E[min]:     sim {first.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / 10:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(max = 6): sim 0.3052, exact 0.3056
E[max]:     sim 4.4732, exact 4.4722
E[max]:     sim 2.9281, exact 2.9290
P(max > 5): sim 0.0650, exact 0.0654
E[min]:     sim 0.1004, exact 0.1000
```

All five numbers agree with the formulas to about three decimal places.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mixing up the two rules.** The **max** uses $F(t)^n$ (all below). The **min** uses
  $(1 - F(t))^n$ (all above) for its **survival** function, so its CDF is $1 - (1 - F(t))^n$,
  not $1 - F(t)^n$.
- **Forgetting independence.** If the values are dependent (for example, servers in the
  same data center that fail together), the product rule is wrong. Use the joint CDF:
  $P(M \le t) = F_{X_1, \dots, X_n}(t, \dots, t)$ ([10.2](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.2-Joint-CDF.md)).
- **Writing $P(M = k) = P(X = k)^n$ for discrete values.** That is the chance that all values
  equal $k$. Use $P(M = k) = P(M \le k) - P(M \le k - 1)$, as in Example 1.
- **Thinking the max of exponentials is exponential.** The **minimum** of exponentials is
  exponential; the maximum is not (its mean is $\frac{1}{\lambda}(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n})$, not $\frac{1}{n\lambda}$).

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** the job time of parallel tasks (max, Example 3); the response time
  of a request sent to several replicas (min, the "hedged request" trick); the deepest
  path in a random structure.
- **Reliability engineering:** a **series** system (works only if all parts work) lives as long
  as its weakest part, the min; a **parallel** or redundant system (works if any part
  works) lives as long as its strongest part, the max (Practice 2 and 3).
- **AI and machine learning:** max-pooling in neural networks, the best of $n$ samples from a
  model, and the Gumbel-max trick for sampling categories
  ([9.10 Logistic and Gumbel](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md)).
- **Economics:** in an auction the winning bid is a max (Example 2).
- **Climate and engineering:** the highest flood or wind speed in a period is a max, and
  maxima of many values tend to the Gumbel shape (Practice 5).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** You roll 5 fair dice. What is the chance that the highest one is at most 4?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

All five must be at most 4:

$$P(M \le 4) = \left( \tfrac{4}{6} \right)^5 = \tfrac{1024}{7776} \approx 0.1317$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A device has 3 parts in series (it fails when any part fails). Their lifetimes
are independent $\text{Exp}(0.1)$, $\text{Exp}(0.2)$ and $\text{Exp}(0.3)$, in years (rates). Find the chance that the
device works for more than 2 years, and its mean lifetime.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The device lifetime is the minimum. Multiply the survival functions:

$$P(L \gt 2) = e^{-0.1 \cdot 2} \thinspace e^{-0.2 \cdot 2} \thinspace e^{-0.3 \cdot 2} = e^{-0.6 \cdot 2} = e^{-1.2} \approx 0.301$$

So $L \sim \text{Exp}(0.6)$, with mean $\frac{1}{0.6} \approx 1.67$ years. The rates add.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Two mirrored disks (RAID 1) each have an $\text{Exp}(0.2)$ lifetime in years,
independent. Data is lost only when both have failed. Find the chance of data loss within
1 year and the mean time until data loss (assume no repair).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Data loss happens at the maximum. With $F(1) = 1 - e^{-0.2} \approx 0.1813$:

$$P(M \le 1) = F(1)^2 \approx 0.1813^2 \approx 0.0329$$

As in Example 3, $E[M] = \frac{1}{0.2}\left(\frac{1}{2} + 1\right) = 5 \times 1.5 = 7.5$ years, compared with 5 years for one disk.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$ values, show $E[\max] = \frac{n}{n + 1}$ and $E[\min] = \frac{1}{n + 1}$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The maximum has density $n t^{n-1}$ on $[0, 1]$:

$$E[\max] = \int_0^1 t \cdot n t^{n-1} \thinspace dt = \int_0^1 n t^n \thinspace dt = \frac{n}{n + 1}$$

Each $1 - U_i$ is also uniform, and $\min_i U_i = 1 - \max_i (1 - U_i)$, so $E[\min] = 1 - \frac{n}{n + 1} = \frac{1}{n + 1}$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Let $M_n$ be the maximum of $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(1)$ values. Show that for every fixed $x$,
$P(M_n - \ln n \le x) \to \exp(-e^{-x})$ as $n \to \infty$. This limit is the Gumbel CDF.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For $n$ large enough that $x + \ln n \ge 0$:

$$P(M_n \le x + \ln n) = \left( 1 - e^{-(x + \ln n)} \right)^n = \left( 1 - \frac{e^{-x}}{n} \right)^n$$

Use the limit $(1 + \frac{a}{n})^n \to e^{a}$ with $a = -e^{-x}$. The result is $\exp(-e^{-x})$, the standard Gumbel CDF from [9.10](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.10-Logistic-and-Gumbel.md). So the maximum grows like $\ln n$, and its random part has a fixed shape.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 5 for $n = 1000$ at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000
# 100,000 maxima of n Exp(1) values, made in 10 batches to save memory.
m = np.concatenate([rng.exponential(1.0, size=(10_000, n)).max(axis=1)
                    for _ in range(10)]) - np.log(n)

for x in [0.0, 2.0]:
    gumbel = np.exp(-np.exp(-x))
    print(f"P(M - ln n <= {x}): simulation {np.mean(m <= x):.4f}, Gumbel {gumbel:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
P(M - ln n <= 0.0): simulation 0.3677, Gumbel 0.3679
P(M - ln n <= 2.0): simulation 0.8734, Gumbel 0.8734
```

</details>

## Summary

- The max is $\le t$ when all values are $\le t$; the min is $\gt t$ when all values are $\gt t$.
- For independent values, $F_{\max} = \prod F_i$ and $P(\min \gt t) = \prod (1 - F_i(t))$.
- For i.i.d. values, $F_{\max} = F^n$ and $F_{\min} = 1 - (1 - F)^n$; take slopes for densities.
- The minimum of exponentials is exponential with the rates added; the maximum of $n$
  exponentials has mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n})$.
- For discrete values, take differences of the CDF to get the PMF.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{P(\max_i X_i \le t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} F_i(t), \qquad P(\min_i X_i \gt t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \big(1 - F_i(t)\big)}
$$
