# 13.3 Jacobian Multivariate Change of Variables

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 13: Functions of Random Variables](README.md) / 13.3 Jacobian Multivariate Change of Variables

**Level:** 3 (Many Variables) · **Time:** about 60 minutes · **Before this:** [13.2 Change of Variables 1D](13.2-Change-of-Variables-1D.md)

> **Big idea:** An invertible map turns a tiny box of inputs into a tiny parallelogram of outputs whose area is $\lvert \det J \rvert$ times bigger; probability is kept, so the new joint density is the old one divided by that area factor.

---

## Why It Matters

In 1D a function stretches **lengths**, and the density changes by the stretch factor
([13.2](13.2-Change-of-Variables-1D.md)). With two or more random variables, a map
stretches **areas** (or volumes), and the stretch factor is a determinant called the
**Jacobian**. This one formula gives the multivariate normal density
([12.1](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md)), the
Box-Muller method for making normal numbers ([13.8](13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md)),
the Beta-Gamma connection, the Student t and the Cauchy distributions, and the
**normalizing flows** used in generative AI.

## Intuition

Let $(X, Y)$ be uniform on the unit square, so the joint density is 1. Now stretch:
$U = 2X$ and $V = 3Y$. The square becomes a rectangle 2 wide and 3 tall, with area 6.
The total probability is still 1, spread evenly over 6 times more area. So the new
density is $\frac{1}{6}$.

Next, a tilted map: $U = X + Y$ and $V = X - Y$. The corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$,
$(1, 1)$ go to $(0, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(1, -1)$, $(2, 0)$. That is a diamond (a square
turned on its corner) with diagonals of length 2, so its area is
$\frac{2 \times 2}{2} = 2$. The density of $(U, V)$ is $\frac{1}{2}$ on the diamond.

In both cases the rule is: **new density = old density / area factor**. For a linear
map, the area factor is the same everywhere, and it is a number you can compute
from the map's four coefficients: the determinant. For a curved map, the area
factor changes from point to point, and we use the determinant of the local linear
map.

## Picture

```text
        input space                              output space
   x2                                       y2
    |                                        |          .-------.
    |    +-----+                             |        .'       .'
    |    |#####|  area dx1 dx2       g       |      .' ####### .'
    |    |#####|                  ------->   |    .'#######  .'
    |    +-----+                             |    '--------'
    |                                        |   area |det J| dx1 dx2
    +---------------------- x1               +---------------------- y1

   same probability:   f_X(x) dx1 dx2  =  f_Y(y) |det J| dx1 dx2
```

A tiny box of inputs becomes a tiny parallelogram of outputs; the probability inside
stays the same, so the density changes by the area factor $\lvert \det J \rvert$.

## Definition

### A Short Reminder: the 2 by 2 Determinant

A $2 \times 2$ matrix has four numbers. Its **determinant** is one number:

$$
\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \cr c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc
$$

**In words:** multiply down the main diagonal, then subtract the product of the other
diagonal.

Its meaning: the linear map $(x_1, x_2) \mapsto (a x_1 + b x_2, \ c x_1 + d x_2)$ sends the unit
square to a parallelogram with sides $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$, and that parallelogram has
area $\lvert ad - bc \rvert$. (Draw the bounding rectangle of size $(a + b) \times (c + d)$ and
remove the corner triangles and rectangles; what is left has area $ad - bc$.) For
$U = 2X, V = 3Y$ the determinant is $2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 0 = 6$. For $U = X + Y, V = X - Y$ it is
$1 \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot 1 = -2$, area 2. The sign only tells whether the map flips the
picture over, so we use the absolute value.

### The Jacobian Matrix

A curved map $g$ sends $(x_1, x_2)$ to $(y_1, y_2) = (g_1(x_1, x_2), \ g_2(x_1, x_2))$. A
**partial derivative** $\frac{\partial g_1}{\partial x_2}$ is the slope of $g_1$ when $x_2$ changes and
$x_1$ stays fixed. The **Jacobian matrix** collects all four slopes:

$$
J_g = \begin{pmatrix} \partial g_1 / \partial x_1 & \partial g_1 / \partial x_2 \cr \partial g_2 / \partial x_1 & \partial g_2 / \partial x_2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Close to one point, a smooth map acts almost like the linear map $J_g$. So a tiny box of
area $dx_1 \thinspace dx_2$ becomes a tiny parallelogram of area $\lvert \det J_g \rvert \thinspace dx_1 \thinspace dx_2$.

**First example: polar coordinates.** The map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (x, y) = (r \cos \theta, \ r \sin \theta)$
has

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -r \sin \theta \cr \sin \theta & r \cos \theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J = r \cos^2 \theta + r \sin^2 \theta = r
$$

So a small polar box with sides $dr$ and $d\theta$ has area $r \thinspace dr \thinspace d\theta$. This
makes sense: its outer side is an arc of length $r \thinspace d\theta$, which is longer when $r$ is
bigger.

### The Formula

Let $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2)$ have joint density $f_{\mathbf{X}}$, and let $\mathbf{Y} = g(\mathbf{X})$, where
$g$ is **one-to-one** and differentiable on the support of $\mathbf{X}$, with inverse $h = g^{-1}$
(so $\mathbf{x} = h(\mathbf{y})$). Then:

$$
f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) = f_{\mathbf{X}}\big(h(\mathbf{y})\big) \cdot \lvert \det J_h(\mathbf{y}) \rvert = \frac{f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})}{\lvert \det J_g(\mathbf{x}) \rvert} \quad \text{with } \mathbf{x} = h(\mathbf{y})
$$

where:

- $h(\mathbf{y})$ is the one input that produces the output $\mathbf{y}$,
- $J_h$ is the Jacobian matrix of the inverse map, and $\det J_h = 1 / \det J_g$ at matching points,
- $f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) = 0$ for every $\mathbf{y}$ that $g$ cannot produce.

**In words:** go back to the input that produced $\mathbf{y}$, read the old density there, and
divide by how much $g$ stretches area at that point.

The same formula holds in $n$ dimensions, with an $n \times n$ Jacobian and "volume" in
place of "area". With $n = 1$ it is exactly the formula of 8.8.

## Why It Is True

Take a small region $B$ around the output point $\mathbf{y}$. The event $\mathbf{Y} \in B$ is the
same event as $\mathbf{X} \in h(B)$, because $g$ is one-to-one. So the two have equal
probabilities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\mathbf{Y} \in B) &= P\big(\mathbf{X} \in h(B)\big) && \text{(same event)} \\
f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) \cdot \text{area}(B) &\approx f_{\mathbf{X}}\big(h(\mathbf{y})\big) \cdot \text{area}\big(h(B)\big) && \text{(density times area, for small regions)} \\
&\approx f_{\mathbf{X}}\big(h(\mathbf{y})\big) \cdot \lvert \det J_h(\mathbf{y}) \rvert \cdot \text{area}(B) && \text{(}h \text{ is almost linear on } B \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide both sides by $\text{area}(B)$ and let $B$ shrink to the point $\mathbf{y}$. The
approximations become exact, and the formula follows.

For a full proof, write $P(\mathbf{Y} \in B) = \iint_{h(B)} f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) \thinspace d\mathbf{x}$ and use the
substitution rule for double integrals, $d\mathbf{x} = \lvert \det J_h(\mathbf{y}) \rvert \thinspace d\mathbf{y}$ (the
2D version of the 1D rule $dx = h'(y) \thinspace dy$). This gives
$P(\mathbf{Y} \in B) = \iint_B f_{\mathbf{X}}(h(\mathbf{y})) \lvert \det J_h(\mathbf{y}) \rvert \thinspace d\mathbf{y}$ for every region
$B$, so the integrand is the density of $\mathbf{Y}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Sum and Difference of Two Normals

**Problem.** $X$ and $Y$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find the joint density of
$U = X + Y$ and $V = X - Y$. Are $U$ and $V$ independent?

**Solution.**

1. The joint density of $(X, Y)$ is the product $\frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}$
   ([10.5](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)).
2. Invert: adding and subtracting $u = x + y$ and $v = x - y$ gives
   $x = \frac{u + v}{2}$ and $y = \frac{u - v}{2}$.
3. The Jacobian of the inverse:

$$
J_h = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \cr 1/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J_h = -\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1}{2}, \qquad \lvert \det J_h \rvert = \frac{1}{2}
$$

4. Rewrite $x^2 + y^2$ in terms of $u, v$:

$$
x^2 + y^2 = \frac{(u + v)^2 + (u - v)^2}{4} = \frac{2u^2 + 2v^2}{4} = \frac{u^2 + v^2}{2}
$$

5. Apply the formula:

$$
f_{U,V}(u, v) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(u^2 + v^2)/4} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}} e^{-u^2/4} \right) \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}} e^{-v^2/4} \right)
$$

**Answer.** The joint density splits into a function of $u$ times a function of $v$, and each
factor is the $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$ density. So $U$ and $V$ are **independent**, each $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$.

**Check.** $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = 1 + 1 = 2$, as expected. Independence is special to
normals: for two independent uniforms, the sum and the difference are uncorrelated but
**not** independent (if the sum is near 0, the difference must be near 0 too). Compare
[12.8](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.8-Uncorrelated-Implies-Independent.md).

### Example 2: Polar Coordinates of a Normal Pair

**Problem.** $X$ and $Y$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Write the point $(X, Y)$ in polar
coordinates: distance $R$ and angle $\Theta$ in $[0, 2\pi)$. Find the joint density of $(R, \Theta)$,
and $P(R \gt 2)$.

**Solution.**

1. The inverse map is $x = r \cos \theta$, $y = r \sin \theta$, and its Jacobian determinant is $r$
   (the polar example above). The map is one-to-one for $r \gt 0$ and $0 \le \theta \lt 2\pi$.
2. Here $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. Apply the formula:

$$
f_{R,\Theta}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-r^2/2} \cdot r = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}}_{\text{angle}} \cdot \underbrace{r e^{-r^2/2}}_{\text{distance}}, \qquad r \gt 0, \ 0 \le \theta \lt 2\pi
$$

3. The density splits into a function of $\theta$ times a function of $r$. So $\Theta$ and $R$
   are independent, $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$, and $R$ has density $r e^{-r^2/2}$
   (the **Rayleigh distribution**).
4. The tail of $R$:

$$
P(R \gt 2) = \int_2^{\infty} r e^{-r^2/2} \thinspace dr = \left[ -e^{-r^2/2} \right]_2^{\infty} = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353
$$

**Answer.** $\Theta \sim \text{Unif}(0, 2\pi)$ and $R$ has density $r e^{-r^2/2}$, independent;
$P(R \gt 2) \approx 0.135$.

**Check.** The angle is uniform because the normal pair looks the same in every
direction. Since $P(R^2 \gt t) = e^{-t/2}$, the squared distance $R^2$ is $\text{Exp}(\frac{1}{2})$, which is
also the chi-square with 2 degrees of freedom. Topic 13.8 runs this example
backwards to make normal numbers.

### Example 3: Total Time and the Share of the First Stage

**Problem.** A request passes through two stages. The stage times $X$ and $Y$ are
independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ (here $\lambda$ is the rate, so the mean is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$). Find
the joint distribution of the total time $S = X + Y$ and the share of the first stage
$W = \frac{X}{X + Y}$.

**Solution.**

1. Support: $S \gt 0$ and $0 \lt W \lt 1$.
2. Invert: $x = sw$ and $y = s - sw = s(1 - w)$.
3. The Jacobian of the inverse (rows: $x$, $y$; columns: $s$, $w$):

$$
J_h = \begin{pmatrix} w & s \cr 1 - w & -s \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J_h = -sw - s(1 - w) = -s, \qquad \lvert \det J_h \rvert = s
$$

4. The joint density of $(X, Y)$ is $\lambda e^{-\lambda x} \cdot \lambda e^{-\lambda y} = \lambda^2 e^{-\lambda(x + y)}$, and $x + y = s$:

$$
f_{S,W}(s, w) = \lambda^2 e^{-\lambda s} \cdot s = \underbrace{\lambda^2 s \thinspace e^{-\lambda s}}_{\text{Gamma}(2, \lambda)} \cdot \underbrace{1}_{\text{Unif}(0, 1)}, \qquad s \gt 0, \ 0 \lt w \lt 1
$$

**Answer.** $S \sim \text{Gamma}(2, \lambda)$ (shape 2, rate $\lambda$), $W \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, and they are
independent.

**Check.** The Gamma part matches [9.4 Gamma](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.4-Gamma.md):
a sum of two exponentials is $\text{Gamma}(2, \lambda)$. The surprise is $W$: whatever the total
time, the split between the two stages is uniform. With $\lambda = 1$,
$P(S \gt 3) = e^{-3}(1 + 3) \approx 0.1991$. The simulation checks all of this.

## Simulate It

We check Example 3 with $\lambda = 1$: the mean and tail of $S$, the uniform $W$, and that
knowing $S$ does not change the distribution of $W$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
lam = 1.0                                  # rate of each stage

# Two independent Exp(1) stages; S = total time, W = share of stage 1.
x = rng.exponential(1 / lam, n_trials)
y = rng.exponential(1 / lam, n_trials)
s, w = x + y, x / (x + y)

# Claim: S ~ Gamma(2, 1), W ~ Unif(0, 1), and S, W independent.
print(f"E[S]:            sim {s.mean():.4f}, exact {2 / lam:.4f}")
print(f"P(S > 3):        sim {np.mean(s > 3):.4f}, exact {4 * np.exp(-3):.4f}")
print(f"P(W <= 0.3):     sim {np.mean(w <= 0.3):.4f}, exact 0.3000")
print(f"P(W <= 0.3 | S > 3): sim {np.mean(w[s > 3] <= 0.3):.4f}, exact 0.3000")
print(f"P(W <= 0.3 | S < 1): sim {np.mean(w[s < 1] <= 0.3):.4f}, exact 0.3000")
```

Output:

```text
E[S]:            sim 1.9987, exact 2.0000
P(S > 3):        sim 0.1990, exact 0.1991
P(W <= 0.3):     sim 0.2994, exact 0.3000
P(W <= 0.3 | S > 3): sim 0.2996, exact 0.3000
P(W <= 0.3 | S < 1): sim 0.2999, exact 0.3000
```

For long and short total times alike, the share $W$ is below $0.3$ about 30% of the time:
$W$ is uniform and independent of $S$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the Jacobian of the wrong map.** With $f_{\mathbf{X}}(h(\mathbf{y}))$ you need
  $\lvert \det J_h \rvert$, the Jacobian of the **inverse**. With $f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})$ you
  **divide** by $\lvert \det J_g \rvert$. Mixing them up gives $\frac{1}{s}$ in place of $s$ in Example 3.
- **Forgetting the absolute value.** In Examples 1 and 3 the determinant is negative.
  A density can never be negative.
- **Forgetting the new support.** In Example 3, $W$ lives on $(0, 1)$, not on all of
  $\mathbb{R}$. Map the corners or edges of the old support to find the new one.
- **Using a map that is not one-to-one.** If two inputs give the same output (like
  $(x, y)$ and $(-x, -y)$ under $(x^2, y^2)$), add one term per input, as in 13.2.
- **Wanting one output, not two.** To find the density of one function such as $X/Y$,
  invent a second output (for example $V = Y$), use the formula, then integrate $V$ out.
  Practice 4 does this.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a **normalizing flow** pushes simple noise through a
  chain of invertible neural network layers. The log-density of the output is the
  log-density of the noise minus the sum of $\log \lvert \det J \rvert$ over the layers.
- **The multivariate normal:** its density formula comes from $\mathbf{X} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + A\mathbf{Z}$
  with this rule, where $\lvert \det A \rvert = \sqrt{\det \Sigma}$
  ([12.1](../12-MULTIVARIATE-GAUSSIAN/12.1-PDF-of-the-Multivariate-Normal.md)).
- **Simulation:** Box-Muller ([13.8](13.8-Box-Muller-Transform.md)) and uniform
  sampling on a disk (Practice 3) are polar changes of variables.
- **Statistics:** the Student t, F and Beta distributions come from ratios of Gamma or
  normal variables ([9.5](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md),
  [9.6](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.6-Chi-Square-Student-t-and-F.md)).
- **Computer graphics:** changing coordinates on a sphere or a surface needs the area
  factor, or the sampled points bunch up in the wrong places.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $(X, Y)$ is uniform on the unit square. Find the joint density of
$U = 3X + Y$ and $V = 2X + 4Y$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The map is linear, with rows $(3, 1)$ and $(2, 4)$, so the area factor is the same everywhere:

$$\det J_g = 3 \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot 2 = 12 - 2 = 10$$

The unit square becomes a parallelogram with corners $(0, 0)$, $(3, 2)$, $(1, 4)$ and $(4, 6)$, of area 10. The density of $(U, V)$ is $\frac{1}{10}$ on it, and 0 outside.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ and $Y$ are independent $\text{Exp}(1)$. Find the joint density of $U = X$ and
$V = X + Y$. Then find the density of $V$ and the conditional distribution of $U$ given $V = v$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Invert: $x = u$ and $y = v - u$. The Jacobian of the inverse has rows $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 1)$, so $\det J_h = 1$. The support is $0 \lt u \lt v$:

$$f_{U,V}(u, v) = e^{-u} \cdot e^{-(v - u)} \cdot 1 = e^{-v}, \qquad 0 \lt u \lt v$$

Integrate out $u$: $f_V(v) = \int_0^v e^{-v} \thinspace du = v e^{-v}$, the $\text{Gamma}(2, 1)$ density. Given $V = v$, the conditional density of $U$ is $\frac{e^{-v}}{v e^{-v}} = \frac{1}{v}$ on $(0, v)$: uniform, as in Example 3.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** To draw a uniform point on the unit disk, a program sets $R = \sqrt{U_1}$ and
$\Theta = 2\pi U_2$ with $U_1, U_2$ independent $\text{Unif}(0, 1)$, then $(X, Y) = (R \cos \Theta, R \sin \Theta)$.
Show that $(X, Y)$ is uniform on the disk. What goes wrong with $R = U_1$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(R \le r) = P(U_1 \le r^2) = r^2$, so $f_R(r) = 2r$ on $(0, 1)$, and $f_{\Theta}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi}$. By independence $f_{R,\Theta}(r, \theta) = \frac{2r}{2\pi} = \frac{r}{\pi}$. The polar map has $\lvert \det J_g \rvert = r$, so:

$$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{r / \pi}{r} = \frac{1}{\pi}$$

That is the uniform density on a disk of area $\pi$. With $R = U_1$, $f_{R,\Theta} = \frac{1}{2\pi}$ and $f_{X,Y} = \frac{1}{2\pi r} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$: the points crowd near the center, a common bug in graphics code.

</details>

**4. (Hard)** $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Show that $W = Z_1 / Z_2$ has the
Cauchy density $\frac{1}{\pi(1 + w^2)}$. Use the helper output $V = Z_2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Invert: $z_1 = wv$ and $z_2 = v$. The Jacobian of the inverse has rows $(v, w)$ and $(0, 1)$, so $\lvert \det J_h \rvert = \lvert v \rvert$. (The case $Z_2 = 0$ has probability 0.) The joint density is:

$$f_{W,V}(w, v) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(w^2 v^2 + v^2)/2} \lvert v \rvert = \frac{1}{2\pi} \lvert v \rvert e^{-(1 + w^2) v^2 / 2}$$

Integrate out $v$. With $c = 1 + w^2$, the two halves are equal and $\int_0^{\infty} v e^{-cv^2/2} \thinspace dv = \frac{1}{c}$:

$$f_W(w) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{1}{1 + w^2} = \frac{1}{\pi(1 + w^2)}$$

This is the standard Cauchy distribution of [9.9](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.9-Cauchy.md).

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, 1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta, 1)$ are independent (shape first,
rate 1). Show that $S = X + Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha + \beta, 1)$ and $W = \frac{X}{X + Y} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$,
and that they are independent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use the same inverse as Example 3: $x = sw$, $y = s(1 - w)$, with $\lvert \det J_h \rvert = s$. Then $x^{\alpha - 1} y^{\beta - 1} = s^{\alpha + \beta - 2} w^{\alpha - 1}(1 - w)^{\beta - 1}$ and $e^{-x - y} = e^{-s}$:

$$f_{S,W}(s, w) = \frac{s^{\alpha + \beta - 2} w^{\alpha - 1} (1 - w)^{\beta - 1} e^{-s}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} \cdot s = \frac{s^{\alpha + \beta - 1} e^{-s}}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} w^{\alpha - 1} (1 - w)^{\beta - 1}$$

The first factor is the $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \beta, 1)$ density and the second is the $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ density, so they are independent with these distributions. Because both factors integrate to 1, this also proves $B(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}$, as in [9.5](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.5-Beta.md).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Problem 3: the square $[0, 0.5] \times [0, 0.5]$ lies inside the disk, so a
uniform point lands in it with probability $\frac{0.25}{\pi}$. Compare $R = \sqrt{U_1}$ with $R = U_1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
theta = 2 * np.pi * rng.random(n)           # angle = 2 pi U2
u1 = rng.random(n)

# Uniform on the disk means P(point in the square [0, 0.5]^2) = 0.25 / pi.
for name, r in [("R = sqrt(U1)", np.sqrt(u1)), ("R = U1 (wrong)", u1)]:
    x, y = r * np.cos(theta), r * np.sin(theta)
    in_square = (x > 0) & (x < 0.5) & (y > 0) & (y < 0.5)
    print(f"{name:15s}: simulation {in_square.mean():.4f}, uniform {0.25 / np.pi:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
R = sqrt(U1)   : simulation 0.0795, uniform 0.0796
R = U1 (wrong) : simulation 0.1403, uniform 0.0796
```

The wrong method puts almost twice as many points in the square near the center.

</details>

## Summary

- For an invertible map $\mathbf{Y} = g(\mathbf{X})$, a tiny box of inputs becomes a tiny
  parallelogram of outputs with area $\lvert \det J_g \rvert$ times bigger.
- Probability is kept, so the new density is the old density at the matching input,
  divided by that area factor (or times $\lvert \det J_h \rvert$ of the inverse).
- For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, $\det = ad - bc$. For polar coordinates the area factor is $r$.
- To find the density of one function of two variables, add a helper output, apply the
  formula, and integrate the helper out.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) = f_{\mathbf{X}}\big(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\big) \cdot \left\lvert \det J_{g^{-1}}(\mathbf{y}) \right\rvert}
$$
