# 6.1 Expected Value

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.1 Expected Value

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [Module 05: Discrete Random Variables](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/README.md)

> **Big idea:** The expected value of a random variable is the average of its values, weighted by their probabilities, and it is what the long-run average of many repeats settles to.

---

## Why It Matters

A random variable can take many values, but we often need **one number** that
describes it: the average waiting time, the average profit, the average running
time of an algorithm. The expected value is that number. It decides whether a bet
is fair, what an insurance policy should cost, and how fast an algorithm is "on
average". Every other topic in this module, and most of machine learning, is built
on it.

## Intuition

You roll a fair die 600 times. Each face shows up about 100 times. So the total of
all rolls is about

- 100 ones, 100 twos, ..., 100 sixes,
- total about $100 \cdot (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) = 100 \cdot 21 = 2100$.

The average roll is then about $2100 / 600 = 3.5$. Notice that 3.5 is not a face of
the die. You will **never** roll 3.5. It is the average that many rolls settle to.

Now change the die. Say a face is a 6 half of the time, and a 1 the other half. In
600 rolls you get about 300 sixes and 300 ones, total about $300 \cdot 6 + 300 \cdot 1 = 2100$,
average 3.5 again. But if the 6 comes up 9 times in 10, the average moves close
to 6. The average depends on the values **and** on how often each value happens.
That is exactly the idea of a weighted average.

There is also a physical picture. Put a weight $p(x)$ at each position $x$ on a
long ruler. The point where the ruler balances is the expected value. Heavy
weights pull the balance point toward them.

## Picture

```text
  P(X = x)
   0.4 |  #
   0.3 |  #     #
   0.2 |  #     #     #
   0.1 |  #     #     #           #
       +--+-----+-----+-----+-----+--
          0     1     2     3     4      x
                 ^
                 E[X] = 1.1   (the balance point of the bars)
```

The PMF $p(0) = 0.4$, $p(1) = 0.3$, $p(2) = 0.2$, $p(4) = 0.1$ balances at $1.1$:
the expected value is the center of mass of the probability.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with PMF $p(x) = P(X = x)$ (see
[5.3 Probability Mass Function](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.3-Probability-Mass-Function.md)).
The **expected value** of $X$, also called the **mean** or the **expectation**, is

$$
E[X] = \sum_{x} x \thinspace p(x)
$$

where:

- the sum runs over every value $x$ in the **support** of $X$ (the values with $p(x) \gt 0$);
- $x$ is a value that $X$ can take;
- $p(x)$ is the probability that $X$ takes that value.

**In words:** multiply each value by its probability, then add up the results.

We often write $\mu$ (the Greek letter "mu") or $\mu_X$ for $E[X]$.

The definition needs one condition. When $X$ has infinitely many values, the sum
must not depend on the order in which we add the terms. So we say $E[X]$
**exists** only when

$$
\sum_{x} \lvert x \rvert \thinspace p(x) \lt \infty
$$

**In words:** the expected value exists when the weighted sum of the sizes of the
values is finite. For a variable with finitely many values this is always true.

There is a second, equivalent way to write the same number. A random variable is a
function on the sample space (see [5.1 What is a Random Variable](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.1-What-is-a-Random-Variable.md)).
When $\Omega$ is finite or countable, we can add over **outcomes** instead of over
values:

$$
E[X] = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} X(\omega) \thinspace P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace)
$$

**In words:** for every outcome, multiply the number $X$ gives it by the
probability of that outcome, then add.

## Why It Is True

**Why the long-run average is the expected value.** Repeat the experiment $n$ times,
where $n$ is large. The value $x$ comes up about $n \thinspace p(x)$ times, because
$p(x)$ is the long-run fraction of times it happens. So the average of all $n$
results is about

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{average} &\approx \frac{1}{n} \sum_{x} x \cdot \big( n \thinspace p(x) \big) && \text{(value } x \text{ appears about } n \thinspace p(x) \text{ times)} \\
&= \sum_{x} x \thinspace p(x) && \text{(the } n \text{ cancels)} \\
&= E[X] && \text{(definition)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This argument is the intuition. The exact statement is the **Law of Large
Numbers**, proved in [17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md).

**Why the two formulas agree.** Group the outcomes by the value that $X$ gives them.
The event $\lbrace X = x \rbrace$ is the set of outcomes $\omega$ with $X(\omega) = x$, and
its probability is $p(x)$. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\omega \in \Omega} X(\omega) \thinspace P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) &= \sum_{x} \ \sum_{\omega : X(\omega) = x} x \thinspace P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) && \text{(group outcomes by value)} \\
&= \sum_{x} x \sum_{\omega : X(\omega) = x} P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) && \text{(} x \text{ is the same inside the group)} \\
&= \sum_{x} x \thinspace P(X = x) && \text{(add the probabilities of the group)} \\
&= \sum_{x} x \thinspace p(x) && \text{(definition of the PMF)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Regrouping a sum is allowed here because the sum of sizes is finite, which is
exactly the condition for $E[X]$ to exist.

**Two simple facts follow at once.** If $X \ge 0$ always, every term $x \thinspace p(x)$
is at least 0, so $E[X] \ge 0$. If $X = c$ always (a constant), then $E[X] = c \cdot 1 = c$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Fair Die

**Problem.** Roll a fair six-sided die and let $X$ be the number shown. Find $E[X]$.

**Solution.**

1. The values are $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$, each with $p(x) = \frac{1}{6}$.
2. Multiply each value by its probability and add:

$$
E[X] = 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} + 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} + 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} + 5 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} + 6 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5
$$

**Answer.** $E[X] = 3.5$. The average of many rolls settles near 3.5, even though
no single roll can be 3.5.

**Check.** The PMF is symmetric around 3.5 (1 pairs with 6, 2 with 5, 3 with 4), so
the balance point must be in the middle. The simulation below agrees.

### Example 2: Should You Play This Game

**Problem.** A game at a fair costs 5 taka. You roll a die and win twice the
number shown, in taka. Let $Y$ be your **profit** (winnings minus the 5 taka). Is the
game good for you in the long run?

**Solution.**

1. List the profit for each roll. Roll 1 wins 2 taka, so the profit is $2 - 5 = -3$.
   In the same way, the profits for rolls 1 to 6 are $-3, -1, 1, 3, 5, 7$.
2. Each roll has probability $\frac{1}{6}$, so each profit value has probability $\frac{1}{6}$.
3. Use the definition:

$$
E[Y] = \frac{(-3) + (-1) + 1 + 3 + 5 + 7}{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2
$$

**Answer.** $E[Y] = 2$, so you gain 2 taka per game on average. Over 1,000 games you
expect to be about 2,000 taka ahead. The game is good for you (and bad for the owner).

**Check.** The average win is $2 \times 3.5 = 7$ taka and the cost is 5 taka, so the
average profit is $7 - 5 = 2$ taka. This shortcut is **linearity**, the next topic.

### Example 3: Average Time to Fetch Data

**Problem.** A program reads a value. It is in the fast cache with probability
$0.80$ (1 millisecond), on the local disk with probability $0.15$ (10 ms), and
otherwise it must go over the network (100 ms). What is the expected time $T$?

**Solution.**

1. The network case has probability $1 - 0.80 - 0.15 = 0.05$.
2. Use the definition:

$$
E[T] = 1 \times 0.80 + 10 \times 0.15 + 100 \times 0.05 = 0.80 + 1.50 + 5.00 = 7.30
$$

**Answer.** $E[T] = 7.3$ ms.

**Check.** The answer is between the smallest value (1) and the largest (100), as it
must be. Notice that the rare network case (only 5% of reads) gives 5 of the 7.3 ms.
Rare but large values can control the mean. To make this program faster, fix
the network case first.

### Example 4: A Mean That Does Not Exist

**Problem.** Flip a fair coin until the first head. If the first head is on flip $k$,
you win $2^k$ taka. This is the famous **St. Petersburg game**. What is the
expected win $W$?

**Solution.**

1. The first head is on flip $k$ with probability $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^k$, and then $W = 2^k$.
2. Each term of the sum is $2^k \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^k = 1$:

$$
E[W] = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + 8 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} + \cdots = 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty
$$

**Answer.** The sum grows without limit, so $E[W] = \infty$. No finite price is a
"fair" price for this game, yet most people would not pay even 50 taka to play.

**Check.** A simulation of this game never settles: every so often a huge win
pulls the running average up. When the mean is infinite, the Law of Large Numbers
gives no finite target to settle to.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 1 (the running average of die rolls) and Example 3 (the
cache).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# The long-run average of die rolls settles near E[X] = 3.5.
for n_rolls in [10, 100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_rolls)
    print(f"{n_rolls:>9,} rolls: average = {rolls.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Exact E[X]:              {3.5:.4f}")

# Cache example: 1 ms (prob 0.80), 10 ms (0.15), 100 ms (0.05).
times = np.array([1, 10, 100])
probs = np.array([0.80, 0.15, 0.05])
samples = rng.choice(times, size=1_000_000, p=probs)
print(f"Cache: simulated mean {samples.mean():.3f} ms, "
      f"exact {np.sum(times * probs):.3f} ms")
```

Output:

```text
       10 rolls: average = 3.1000
      100 rolls: average = 3.7300
   10,000 rolls: average = 3.4741
1,000,000 rolls: average = 3.4984
Exact E[X]:              3.5000
Cache: simulated mean 7.306 ms, exact 7.300 ms
```

With few rolls the average wanders. With a million rolls it is very close to 3.5.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking the expected value is the most likely value.** For a die, 3.5 can never
  happen. The expected value is an average, not a prediction of one result. The
  most likely value is the **mode**, see [6.8 Median, Mode and Quantiles](6.8-Median-Mode-and-Quantiles.md).
- **Taking a plain average of the values.** $\frac{1 + 10 + 100}{3} = 37$ ms is wrong for
  Example 3. Each value must be weighted by its probability.
- **Forgetting a value.** The probabilities you use must add up to 1. In Example 3,
  the network case was not given directly; we found it as $1 - 0.80 - 0.15$.
- **Expecting a short run to match the mean.** Ten rolls averaging 3.1 is normal.
  The mean describes the long run.
- **Assuming every variable has a mean.** The St. Petersburg game (Example 4) has an
  infinite mean. Check that $\sum_x \lvert x \rvert \thinspace p(x)$ is finite.

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** a model is trained by making the **expected loss**
  small. The training loss is an average over data, which estimates that expected
  value (see [6.10 Expectation as Best Guess](6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md)).
- **Computer science:** "average-case running time" is the expected running time
  over random inputs or random choices, as in quicksort with a random pivot.
- **Insurance and finance:** a fair premium is the expected payout. Companies add a
  margin on top so that they make money in the long run.
- **Games and gambling:** every casino game has a negative expected profit for the
  player. That is how the casino stays in business.
- **Everyday life:** comparing two options by their average outcome, such as two
  routes to work, is an expected-value decision (see [22.3 Expected Value Decisions](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.3-Expected-Value-Decisions.md)).

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** A random variable $X$ takes the values 0, 1 and 2 with probabilities
$0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.2$. Find $E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 0 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 0.3 + 2 \times 0.2 = 0 + 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** A raffle sells 1,000 tickets at 10 taka each. One ticket wins a prize of
5,000 taka. What is the expected profit of one ticket?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Your profit is $5000 - 10 = 4990$ taka with probability $\frac{1}{1000}$, and $-10$ taka
with probability $\frac{999}{1000}$:

$$E[\text{profit}] = 4990 \times 0.001 + (-10) \times 0.999 = 4.99 - 9.99 = -5$$

On average you lose 5 taka per ticket.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A phone worth 30,000 taka is lost or broken during a year with
probability $0.02$. If that happens, the insurance pays 30,000 taka; otherwise it pays
nothing. What is the expected payout? What yearly premium would be "fair"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[\text{payout}] = 30000 \times 0.02 + 0 \times 0.98 = 600$$

A fair premium is 600 taka per year. A real company charges more, to cover costs
and make a profit.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Roll two fair dice and let $S$ be their sum. Use the PMF of $S$
(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 chances out of 36 for the sums 2 to 12) to find $E[S]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Multiply each sum by its count and add:

$$2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 5 + 7 \cdot 6 + 8 \cdot 5 + 9 \cdot 4 + 10 \cdot 3 + 11 \cdot 2 + 12 \cdot 1 = 252$$

$$E[S] = \frac{252}{36} = 7$$

This is $3.5 + 3.5$. The next topic shows why that is no accident.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $a \le X \le b$ always. Prove that $a \le E[X] \le b$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every value $x$ in the support satisfies $x \le b$, and $p(x) \ge 0$, so
$x \thinspace p(x) \le b \thinspace p(x)$. Add over all $x$ and use that the PMF sums to 1:

$$E[X] = \sum_x x \thinspace p(x) \le \sum_x b \thinspace p(x) = b \sum_x p(x) = b$$

In the same way, $x \thinspace p(x) \ge a \thinspace p(x)$, which gives $E[X] \ge a$. So the mean
always lies between the smallest and the largest possible value.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate the game of Example 2 one million times and check that the
average profit is close to 2 taka.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_games = 1_000_000
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_games)
profit = 2 * rolls - 5          # win 2 x roll taka, pay 5 taka
print(f"Average profit: simulation {profit.mean():.4f}, exact {2.0:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Average profit: simulation 1.9963, exact 2.0000
```

</details>

## Summary

- The expected value is a weighted average: each value counts as much as its
  probability.
- It is the long-run average of many independent repeats, and the balance point of
  the PMF.
- It need not be a possible value (a die has mean 3.5).
- Rare but large values can move the mean a lot; some variables have no finite mean.
- You can add over values or over outcomes. Both give the same number.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X] = \sum_{x} x \thinspace p(x)}
$$
