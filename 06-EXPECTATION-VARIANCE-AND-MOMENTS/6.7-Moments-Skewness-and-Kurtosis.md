# 6.7 Moments, Skewness and Kurtosis

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.7 Moments, Skewness and Kurtosis

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [6.6 Properties of Variance](6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)

> **Big idea:** The averages of $X, X^2, X^3, X^4, \dots$ (the moments) describe the shape of a distribution step by step: center, spread, lopsidedness (skewness), and how heavy the tails are (kurtosis).

---

## Why It Matters

The mean and the variance do not tell the whole story. Two distributions can have the
same mean and the same variance, and still look very different: one has a long tail
of rare huge values (like insurance claims or file sizes), the other does not.
**Skewness** and **kurtosis** put numbers on these differences. They warn you when
averages are dominated by rare extreme events, which matters for risk, for data
cleaning, and for choosing a model.

## Intuition

An insurance company sells 1,000 phone policies. In a typical year about 900 customers
claim nothing and about 100 claim a lot. The average claim is small, but the few big
claims are far above it. If you draw the PMF, it has a tall bar near 0 and a long
**tail** to the right. This lopsided shape is called **right-skewed**.

Now think about a fair die. Its PMF is a flat row of six equal bars. It is
**symmetric**: the left half is the mirror image of the right half. It has no tail at
all, because every value is within 2.5 of the mean.

How can we measure these shapes with numbers? The trick is to look at powers of the
distance from the mean. Odd powers keep the sign: values far to the right add big
positive amounts, values far to the left add big negative amounts. So the average of
the **cube** of the distance tells us which side has the longer tail. Even powers
ignore the sign but grow very fast: the average of the **fourth** power is dominated by
the most extreme values, so it tells us how heavy the tails are.

## Picture

```text
                #                #                #
             #  #                #                #  #
          #  #  #  #          #  #  #          #  #  #  #
    #  #  #  #  #  #       #  #  #  #  #       #  #  #  #  #  #
   -+--+--+--+--+--+-     -+--+--+--+--+-     -+--+--+--+--+--+-
    tail to the left       no long tail       tail to the right
      skewness < 0         skewness = 0          skewness > 0
```

The sign of the skewness says on which side the long tail of the distribution lies.

## Definition

Let $X$ have mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma \gt 0$, and let $k$ be a positive whole
number.

The **$k$-th moment** (or **raw moment**) of $X$ is

$$
E[X^k] = \sum_{x} x^k \thinspace p(x)
$$

The **$k$-th central moment** is the same, but measured from the mean:

$$
E\big[(X - \mu)^k\big] = \sum_{x} (x - \mu)^k \thinspace p(x)
$$

**In words:** a moment is the average of a power of $X$; a central moment is the
average of a power of the distance from the mean. Both are LOTUS computations
([6.3](6.3-LOTUS.md)).

The first moment is the mean. The first central moment is always 0. The second
central moment is the variance. The next two, divided by a power of $\sigma$ so that
they have no units, get their own names:

$$
\operatorname{Skew}(X) = \frac{E\big[(X - \mu)^3\big]}{\sigma^3} \qquad\qquad \operatorname{Kurt}(X) = \frac{E\big[(X - \mu)^4\big]}{\sigma^4}
$$

- **Skewness** measures lopsidedness. It is 0 for a symmetric distribution, positive
  when the long tail is on the right, negative when it is on the left.
- **Kurtosis** measures how heavy the tails are: how much of the variance comes from
  rare values far from the mean. It is always at least 1. The normal distribution
  (Module 09, [9.2 Normal Gaussian](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.2-Normal-Gaussian.md))
  has kurtosis 3, so people often report the **excess kurtosis**, $\operatorname{Kurt}(X) - 3$.

**In words:** skewness is the average cubed z-score, and kurtosis is the average
fourth-power z-score, where the z-score is $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$ from [6.6](6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md).

The $k$-th moment **exists** when $E[\lvert X \rvert^k]$ is finite. If it exists, all lower
moments exist too, because $\lvert x \rvert^j \le 1 + \lvert x \rvert^k$ for $j \le k$. Some variables have a
mean but no variance; for example $p(x) \propto 1/x^3$ for $x = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (see
[9.13 Heavy vs Light Tails](../09-CONTINUOUS-DISTRIBUTIONS/9.13-Heavy-vs-Light-Tails.md)).

## Why It Is True

**From raw moments to central moments.** Raw moments are often easier to compute.
Expand the cube and use linearity, remembering that $\mu$ is a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big[(X - \mu)^3\big] &= E\big[X^3 - 3\mu X^2 + 3\mu^2 X - \mu^3\big] && \text{(expand the cube)} \\
&= E[X^3] - 3\mu E[X^2] + 3\mu^2 E[X] - \mu^3 && \text{(linearity)} \\
&= E[X^3] - 3\mu E[X^2] + 2\mu^3 && \text{(} E[X] = \mu \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the same idea as the shortcut $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2$ in
[6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md), one power higher.

**Symmetric means zero skewness.** Say the PMF is symmetric about $\mu$, which means
$X - \mu$ and $\mu - X$ have the same distribution. Then they have the same third moment:

$$
E\big[(X - \mu)^3\big] = E\big[(\mu - X)^3\big] = E\big[-(X - \mu)^3\big] = -E\big[(X - \mu)^3\big]
$$

A number equal to its own negative is 0. The same argument shows that every odd
central moment of a symmetric distribution is 0.

**Shape numbers ignore units.** Let $Y = aX + b$ with $a \gt 0$. Then $Y - \mu_Y = a(X - \mu)$
and $\sigma_Y = a\sigma$ ([6.6](6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)), so

$$
\operatorname{Skew}(Y) = \frac{E\big[a^3 (X - \mu)^3\big]}{a^3 \sigma^3} = \operatorname{Skew}(X)
$$

and in the same way $\operatorname{Kurt}(Y) = \operatorname{Kurt}(X)$. Changing units (taka to
dollars, minutes to hours) does not change the shape. If $a \lt 0$, the skewness changes
sign (the picture is mirrored) and the kurtosis stays the same, since $a^4 \gt 0$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Shape of a Die

**Problem.** Find the skewness and kurtosis of a fair die roll.

**Solution.**

1. From [6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md): $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma^2 = \frac{35}{12}$.
2. The PMF is symmetric about $3.5$, so $\operatorname{Skew}(X) = 0$.
3. The distances from the mean are $\pm 0.5$, $\pm 1.5$, $\pm 2.5$. Their fourth powers are
   $0.0625$, $5.0625$, $39.0625$, each appearing twice:

$$
E\big[(X - \mu)^4\big] = \frac{2 \times (0.0625 + 5.0625 + 39.0625)}{6} = \frac{88.375}{6} \approx 14.729
$$

4. $\sigma^4 = \left(\frac{35}{12}\right)^2 = \frac{1225}{144} \approx 8.507$, so

$$
\operatorname{Kurt}(X) = \frac{14.729}{8.507} \approx 1.731
$$

**Answer.** Skewness 0 and kurtosis about $1.73$ (excess kurtosis about $-1.27$).

**Check.** The die has no tails at all; every value is within $1.5\sigma$ of the mean. So
its kurtosis is well below the normal value of 3.

### Example 2: Insurance Claims

**Problem.** A claim $X$ (in thousands of taka) is 0 with probability $0.9$ and 10 with
probability $0.1$. Find its mean, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis.

**Solution.**

1. Mean: $\mu = 0 \times 0.9 + 10 \times 0.1 = 1$.
2. The distances from the mean are $0 - 1 = -1$ (probability $0.9$) and $10 - 1 = 9$
   (probability $0.1$).
3. Variance: $\sigma^2 = (-1)^2 (0.9) + 9^2 (0.1) = 0.9 + 8.1 = 9$, so $\sigma = 3$.
4. Third central moment:

$$
E\big[(X - \mu)^3\big] = (-1)^3 (0.9) + 9^3 (0.1) = -0.9 + 72.9 = 72
$$

5. Fourth central moment:

$$
E\big[(X - \mu)^4\big] = (-1)^4 (0.9) + 9^4 (0.1) = 0.9 + 656.1 = 657
$$

6. Divide by powers of $\sigma = 3$:

$$
\operatorname{Skew}(X) = \frac{72}{3^3} = \frac{72}{27} \approx 2.667, \qquad \operatorname{Kurt}(X) = \frac{657}{3^4} = \frac{657}{81} \approx 8.111
$$

**Answer.** Mean 1 (that is, 1,000 taka), SD 3, skewness about $2.67$, kurtosis about $8.11$.

**Check.** The skewness is large and positive: the long tail is on the right, as the
story said. Almost all of the third and fourth moments come from the rare value 10.
The simulation below agrees.

### Example 3: Using Raw Moments

**Problem.** In a code review, the number of bugs found $X$ is 0, 1 or 2 with
probabilities $0.5$, $0.3$, $0.2$. Find the skewness using raw moments.

**Solution.**

1. Raw moments by LOTUS:
   - $E[X] = 0.3 + 2 \times 0.2 = 0.7$
   - $E[X^2] = 0.3 + 4 \times 0.2 = 1.1$
   - $E[X^3] = 0.3 + 8 \times 0.2 = 1.9$
2. Variance: $\sigma^2 = 1.1 - 0.7^2 = 1.1 - 0.49 = 0.61$.
3. Third central moment, using the formula above:

$$
E\big[(X - \mu)^3\big] = 1.9 - 3(0.7)(1.1) + 2(0.7)^3 = 1.9 - 2.31 + 0.686 = 0.276
$$

4. $\sigma^3 = 0.61^{3/2} \approx 0.4764$, so $\operatorname{Skew}(X) \approx \frac{0.276}{0.4764} \approx 0.579$.

**Answer.** The skewness is about $0.58$: mildly right-skewed.

**Check.** Directly: the distances are $-0.7$, $0.3$, $1.3$, and
$(-0.343)(0.5) + (0.027)(0.3) + (2.197)(0.2) = -0.1715 + 0.0081 + 0.4394 = 0.276$. Same answer.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2 and Example 1. The `scipy.stats` functions `skew` and
`kurtosis` compute the sample versions of the formulas above; `fisher=False` asks for
plain kurtosis instead of excess kurtosis.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Insurance claim: 0 with probability 0.9, 10 (thousand taka) with 0.1.
claims = rng.choice([0, 10], size=n_trials, p=[0.9, 0.1])

print(f"Mean:     simulation {claims.mean():.4f}, exact {1:.4f}")
print(f"Variance: simulation {claims.var():.4f}, exact {9:.4f}")
print(f"Skewness: simulation {stats.skew(claims):.4f}, exact {8 / 3:.4f}")
print(f"Kurtosis: simulation {stats.kurtosis(claims, fisher=False):.4f}, "
      f"exact {73 / 9:.4f}")

# A fair die is symmetric (skewness 0) and flat (kurtosis 2121/1225).
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
print(f"Die skewness: simulation {stats.skew(rolls):.4f}, exact {0:.4f}")
print(f"Die kurtosis: simulation {stats.kurtosis(rolls, fisher=False):.4f}, "
      f"exact {2121 / 1225:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Mean:     simulation 1.0018, exact 1.0000
Variance: simulation 9.0144, exact 9.0000
Skewness: simulation 2.6633, exact 2.6667
Kurtosis: simulation 8.0934, exact 8.1111
Die skewness: simulation -0.0012, exact 0.0000
Die kurtosis: simulation 1.7331, exact 1.7314
```

Here $\frac{8}{3} = \frac{72}{27}$, $\frac{73}{9} = \frac{657}{81}$, and $\frac{2121}{1225}$ is the exact die kurtosis
from Example 1. Higher moments are noisier to estimate, because they depend so
much on rare values.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to divide by $\sigma^3$ or $\sigma^4$.** The raw central moment has units
  (taka cubed). Skewness and kurtosis are the versions without units.
- **Mixing up kurtosis and excess kurtosis.** Software often reports excess kurtosis
  (kurtosis minus 3). In `scipy`, `kurtosis()` gives the excess version unless you pass
  `fisher=False`. Always say which one you mean.
- **Thinking kurtosis measures "peakedness".** It is driven mainly by the tails: rare
  values far from the mean. A distribution can have a flat top and a high kurtosis.
- **Concluding "skewness 0 means symmetric".** Symmetric implies skewness 0, but not the
  other way round. Some lopsided distributions also have skewness 0.
- **Assuming every moment exists.** Heavy-tailed variables can have an infinite
  variance or even an infinite mean. Then sample moments never settle down.

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance:** returns are often negatively skewed with high kurtosis ("fat tails"), so
  big crashes happen more often than a normal model predicts (see
  [22.13 Fat Tails](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.13-Fat-Tails.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** skewed features (incomes, counts, file sizes) are often
  transformed, for example with a logarithm, before training. Optimizers such as
  Adam keep running estimates of the first and second moments of the gradients.
- **Computer science:** latency and file-size distributions are strongly right-skewed,
  which is why engineers look at tail percentiles, not only the mean
  ([6.8](6.8-Median-Mode-and-Quantiles.md)).
- **Generating functions:** all moments at once can be packed into one function, the
  moment generating function ([15.2 Moments from the MGF](../15-GENERATING-FUNCTIONS/15.2-Moments-from-the-MGF.md)).
- **Data checks:** a sample skewness or kurtosis far from what a model expects is a
  warning sign that the model is wrong.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ is a Bernoulli variable: 1 with probability $p$, 0 otherwise. Find
$E[X^k]$ for every $k \ge 1$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Since $X$ is 0 or 1, $X^k = X$ for every $k \ge 1$. So

$$E[X^k] = 0^k (1 - p) + 1^k \thinspace p = p$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Why is the first central moment $E[X - \mu]$ always 0?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By linearity, $E[X - \mu] = E[X] - \mu = \mu - \mu = 0$. The distances above and below the mean
always balance, which is why the mean is the balance point.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The claim $X$ of Example 2 has skewness $\frac{8}{3}$. What is the skewness of
$-X$? Of $5X + 3$? What are their kurtoses?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A negative scale flips the sign of the skewness, and a positive scale and a shift leave
it unchanged. So $\operatorname{Skew}(-X) = -\frac{8}{3}$ and $\operatorname{Skew}(5X + 3) = \frac{8}{3}$.
Kurtosis is unchanged by any non-zero scale and any shift, so both have kurtosis $\frac{73}{9} \approx 8.11$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Show that a $\text{Bern}(p)$ variable with $0 \lt p \lt 1$ has skewness
$\frac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{p(1 - p)}}$. Check it against Example 2.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is $p$, so the distances are $1 - p$ (probability $p$) and $-p$ (probability $1 - p$):

$$E\big[(X - p)^3\big] = p(1 - p)^3 + (1 - p)(-p)^3 = p(1 - p)\big[(1 - p)^2 - p^2\big] = p(1 - p)(1 - 2p)$$

Divide by $\sigma^3 = \big(p(1 - p)\big)^{3/2}$ to get $\frac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{p(1 - p)}}$. The claim in Example 2 is $10 \times$
a $\text{Bern}(0.1)$ variable, and scaling does not change skewness:
$\frac{1 - 0.2}{\sqrt{0.09}} = \frac{0.8}{0.3} \approx 2.667$. It matches.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that $\operatorname{Kurt}(X) \ge 1$ for every $X$ with a finite fourth moment
and $\sigma \gt 0$. When is it exactly 1?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Let $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$, so $E[Z^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Z) + (E[Z])^2 = 1$ and $\operatorname{Kurt}(X) = E[Z^4]$.
Apply the fact $E[Y^2] \ge (E[Y])^2$ from [6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md) to $Y = Z^2$:

$$\operatorname{Kurt}(X) = E[Z^4] = E[(Z^2)^2] \ge (E[Z^2])^2 = 1$$

Equality needs $\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) = 0$, so $Z^2 = 1$ with probability 1. Then $X$ is $\mu - \sigma$ or
$\mu + \sigma$, and to have mean $\mu$ each must have probability $\frac{1}{2}$. A fair coin flip
scored as $\pm 1$ has kurtosis exactly 1.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 4 for $p = 0.3$ by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p = 0.3
x = (rng.random(1_000_000) < p).astype(float)   # Bernoulli(0.3)
exact = (1 - 2 * p) / np.sqrt(p * (1 - p))
print(f"Skewness: simulation {stats.skew(x):.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Skewness: simulation 0.8736, exact 0.8729
```

</details>

## Summary

- The $k$-th moment is $E[X^k]$; the $k$-th central moment is $E[(X - \mu)^k]$.
- Mean (1st moment) gives the center; variance (2nd central) gives the spread.
- Skewness (3rd, scaled) gives the lopsidedness: its sign shows the side of the long tail.
- Kurtosis (4th, scaled) shows how heavy the tails are; the normal has 3, and it is never
  below 1.
- Skewness and kurtosis do not change with units.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Skew}(X) = E\left[\left(\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^3\right], \qquad \operatorname{Kurt}(X) = E\left[\left(\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^4\right]}
$$
