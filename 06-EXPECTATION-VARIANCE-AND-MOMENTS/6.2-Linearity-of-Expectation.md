# 6.2 Linearity of Expectation

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.2 Linearity of Expectation

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [6.1 Expected Value](6.1-Expected-Value.md)

> **Big idea:** The expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, always, even when the variables depend on each other.

---

## Why It Matters

Finding the PMF of a sum is often hard. Finding the expected value of a sum is
almost always easy: add the expected values of the parts. This one rule turns
many hard counting problems into one-line answers. It is the most used tool in
this module, and it works **without** any independence assumption, which makes it
very powerful in computer science, where parts of a system usually depend on each
other.

## Intuition

A shop sells tea and cake. On an average day it earns 1,500 taka from tea and
900 taka from cake. What does it earn on an average day in total? Of course
2,400 taka. You do not need to know how the tea and cake sales move together.
Maybe on rainy days people buy more of both. Maybe they buy tea **instead** of
cake. It does not matter: averages add.

Here is why. Over 100 days, the total from tea is about $100 \times 1500$ taka and the
total from cake is about $100 \times 900$ taka. The grand total is the sum of these two
totals, whatever the pattern. Divide by 100 days and you get $1500 + 900 = 2400$.

Scaling works the same way. If every price doubles, the average income doubles.
If the shop pays a fixed rent of 600 taka a day, the average profit drops by
exactly 600 taka.

## Picture

```text
   outcome ω   P({ω})   X(ω)   Y(ω)   X(ω)+Y(ω)
   ---------   ------   ----   ----   ---------
      HH        1/4      2      1         3
      HT        1/4      1      1         2
      TH        1/4      1      0         1
      TT        1/4      0      0         0
   ---------   ------   ----   ----   ---------
   weighted average:    1.0  +  0.5  =   1.5
```

Two fair coin flips. $X$ is the number of heads, $Y$ is 1 when the first flip is a
head. They are dependent, yet the average of the last column is the sum of the
averages of the two columns before it.

## Definition

**Linearity of expectation** says: for any random variables $X$ and $Y$ on the same
sample space whose expected values exist, and any constants $a$, $b$, $c$,

$$
E[aX + bY + c] = a E[X] + b E[Y] + c
$$

**In words:** the average of a weighted sum is the same weighted sum of the
averages.

Two special cases are used all the time:

$$
E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] \qquad\qquad E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b
$$

**In words:** expectation splits over a sum, and a scale factor or a shift can be
pulled outside.

For $n$ variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$:

$$
E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[X_i]
$$

**In words:** the expected total is the total of the expected parts.

There is **no** condition about independence. The variables can depend on each
other in any way.

## Why It Is True

We use the outcome form of the expected value from [6.1 Expected Value](6.1-Expected-Value.md):
$E[X] = \sum_{\omega} X(\omega) P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace)$, for a finite or countable
sample space $\Omega$. The new variable $aX + bY + c$ gives the number
$aX(\omega) + bY(\omega) + c$ to each outcome $\omega$. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[aX + bY + c] &= \sum_{\omega} \big( aX(\omega) + bY(\omega) + c \big) P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) && \text{(outcome form)} \\
&= a \sum_{\omega} X(\omega) P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) + b \sum_{\omega} Y(\omega) P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) + c \sum_{\omega} P(\lbrace \omega \rbrace) && \text{(split the sum)} \\
&= a E[X] + b E[Y] + c \cdot 1 && \text{(outcome form, and } P(\Omega) = 1 \text{)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Splitting the sum into three sums is allowed because each of the three sums
converges absolutely (that is what "the expected values exist" means).

Look at what the proof never used: it never asked how $X$ and $Y$ are related. Each
outcome simply carries both numbers, and we add them. That is why independence is
not needed.

For $n$ variables, use the two-variable rule again and again (induction):
$E[X_1 + \cdots + X_n] = E[X_1 + \cdots + X_{n-1}] + E[X_n]$, and so on down to one
variable. Module 10 gives a second proof using the joint PMF, in
[10.1 Joint PMF and PDF](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.1-Joint-PMF-and-PDF.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Sum of Many Dice

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice. What is the expected sum? What about ten dice?

**Solution.**

1. Let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be the two rolls. From [6.1](6.1-Expected-Value.md),
   $E[X_1] = E[X_2] = 3.5$.
2. By linearity:

$$
E[X_1 + X_2] = E[X_1] + E[X_2] = 3.5 + 3.5 = 7
$$

3. For ten dice, the same rule gives

$$
E[X_1 + \cdots + X_{10}] = 10 \times 3.5 = 35
$$

**Answer.** The expected sum is 7 for two dice and 35 for ten dice.

**Check.** In Practice 4 of [6.1](6.1-Expected-Value.md) we found $E[S] = 7$ the long
way, using all 11 values of the PMF. Linearity gives it in one line. For ten dice the
PMF has 51 values, so the long way would be painful.

### Example 2: Two Cards Without Replacement

**Problem.** A hat holds 10 cards numbered 1 to 10. You draw two cards **without**
putting the first one back. Let $X$ be the first number and $Y$ the second. Find
$E[X + Y]$.

**Solution.**

1. $X$ and $Y$ are **dependent**: if $X = 10$, then $Y$ cannot be 10.
2. On its own, the first card is equally likely to be any of the 10 numbers:

$$
E[X] = \frac{1 + 2 + \cdots + 10}{10} = \frac{55}{10} = 5.5
$$

3. On its own, the second card is also equally likely to be any of the 10 numbers.
   (Before you look at anything, the second card of a shuffled pile is as random as
   the first.) So $E[Y] = 5.5$.
4. Linearity does not care about the dependence:

$$
E[X + Y] = 5.5 + 5.5 = 11
$$

**Answer.** $E[X + Y] = 11$.

**Check.** The simulation below gets 11.004. Note that a product is different:
$E[XY] \approx 29.33$, not $5.5 \times 5.5 = 30.25$. Linearity is about sums only.

### Example 3: Expected Profit of a Tea Stall

**Problem.** A tea stall sells $X$ cups a day, with $E[X] = 120$. Each cup sells for
15 taka. The rent is 600 taka per day. What is the expected daily profit?

**Solution.**

1. The profit is $15X - 600$ taka. This has the form $aX + b$ with $a = 15$ and $b = -600$.
2. By linearity:

$$
E[15X - 600] = 15 \thinspace E[X] - 600 = 15 \times 120 - 600 = 1800 - 600 = 1200
$$

**Answer.** The expected profit is 1,200 taka per day.

**Check.** We did not need the PMF of $X$ at all, only its mean.

### Example 4: Loading a Web Page

**Problem.** A web page loads three files one after another. Their load times $T_1$,
$T_2$, $T_3$ have means 40 ms, 25 ms and 60 ms. On a slow network all three are slow
together, so the times are dependent. What is the expected total load time?

**Solution.**

1. The total time is $T = T_1 + T_2 + T_3$.
2. By linearity, which needs no independence:

$$
E[T] = E[T_1] + E[T_2] + E[T_3] = 40 + 25 + 60 = 125
$$

**Answer.** $E[T] = 125$ ms.

**Check.** The dependence changes how **spread out** the total is (see
[6.6 Properties of Variance](6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md)), but not its mean.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 2: two dependent cards. It also shows that the same trick
fails for products.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Draw 2 of the cards 1..10 WITHOUT replacement: X and Y are dependent.
keys = rng.random((n_trials, 10))
cards = np.argsort(keys, axis=1)[:, :2] + 1   # first two cards of a shuffle
x, y = cards[:, 0], cards[:, 1]

print(f"E[X + Y]:  simulation {np.mean(x + y):.4f}, exact {11:.4f}")
# Linearity says nothing about products: E[XY] is not E[X]E[Y] here.
print(f"E[XY]:     simulation {np.mean(x * y):.4f}, exact {2640 / 90:.4f}")
print(f"E[X]E[Y]:  {np.mean(x) * np.mean(y):.4f}  (exact 5.5 x 5.5 = 30.25)")
```

Output:

```text
E[X + Y]:  simulation 11.0040, exact 11.0000
E[XY]:     simulation 29.3665, exact 29.3333
E[X]E[Y]:  30.2718  (exact 5.5 x 5.5 = 30.25)
```

The exact $E[XY]$ comes from averaging $ij$ over the 90 ordered pairs of different
cards: $\frac{55^2 - 385}{90} = \frac{2640}{90} \approx 29.33$, where 385 is
$1^2 + 2^2 + \cdots + 10^2$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking linearity needs independence.** It does not. Example 2 and Example 4
  use dependent variables, and the rule still holds.
- **Using it for products.** In general $E[XY] \ne E[X] \thinspace E[Y]$. For one die,
  $E[X \cdot X] = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17$ but $E[X] \thinspace E[X] = 12.25$. The product
  rule holds for independent variables (see [10.5 Independence of Random Variables](../10-JOINT-DISTRIBUTIONS/10.5-Independence-of-Random-Variables.md)).
- **Pushing expectation inside a non-linear function.** $E[X^2] \ne (E[X])^2$ and
  $E[1/X] \ne 1/E[X]$ in general. The right tool is LOTUS, the next topic.
- **Forgetting the constant.** $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$, not $aE[X]$. A fixed cost
  shifts the mean by its full amount.
- **Applying it to variance.** $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y)$ is not always
  $\operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$. See [6.6](6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** the expected running time of an algorithm is the sum of the
  expected costs of its steps, even when the steps depend on each other.
- **Randomized algorithms:** the analysis of quicksort writes the number of
  comparisons as a sum of indicators and applies linearity (see
  [6.4 Indicator Trick](6.4-Indicator-Trick.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** the loss of a model on a dataset is a sum over
  examples, so the expected loss is the sum of the per-example expected losses.
  This is why averaging gradients over a mini-batch works.
- **Finance:** the expected return of a portfolio is the weighted sum of the expected
  returns of its assets, whatever their correlation.
- **Everyday life:** the expected cost of a trip is the sum of the expected costs of
  transport, food and lodging.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $E[X] = 4$ and $E[Y] = -1$. Find $E[3X - 2Y + 5]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[3X - 2Y + 5] = 3 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot (-1) + 5 = 12 + 2 + 5 = 19$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** You roll 100 fair dice. What is the expected total?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X_1 + \cdots + X_{100}] = 100 \times 3.5 = 350$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** The temperature in Celsius, $C$, has mean 25. The temperature in
Fahrenheit is $F = 1.8C + 32$. What is $E[F]$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[F] = 1.8 \thinspace E[C] + 32 = 1.8 \times 25 + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Deal 5 cards from a shuffled deck of 52, without replacement. Count
an ace as 1, number cards as their number, and jack, queen, king as 11, 12, 13.
What is the expected total of the 5 cards?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each card on its own is equally likely to be any of the 52 cards. The values 1 to 13
appear 4 times each, so one card has mean

$$\frac{4 \cdot (1 + 2 + \cdots + 13)}{52} = \frac{4 \cdot 91}{52} = 7$$

The cards are dependent, but linearity still works:

$$E[\text{total}] = 5 \times 7 = 35$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove $E[X_1 + \cdots + X_n] = E[X_1] + \cdots + E[X_n]$ for every $n \ge 1$, using
only the two-variable rule.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use induction on $n$. For $n = 1$ there is nothing to prove. Suppose the rule holds for
$n - 1$ variables. Let $S = X_1 + \cdots + X_{n-1}$. Then $X_1 + \cdots + X_n = S + X_n$, and
the two-variable rule gives

$$E[S + X_n] = E[S] + E[X_n] = \big(E[X_1] + \cdots + E[X_{n-1}]\big) + E[X_n]$$

where the second step uses the rule for $n - 1$ variables. So the rule holds for $n$,
and by induction for every $n$.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Simulate Practice 4: deal 5 cards many times and average the totals.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 200_000

# Deal 5 cards from 52 (values 1..13, four of each), without replacement.
deck = np.repeat(np.arange(1, 14), 4)
keys = rng.random((n_trials, 52))
hands = deck[np.argsort(keys, axis=1)[:, :5]]
print(f"Average total: simulation {hands.sum(axis=1).mean():.4f}, exact {35:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Average total: simulation 35.0265, exact 35.0000
```

</details>

## Summary

- The expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values.
- Constants come out: $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$.
- No independence is needed. This is what makes linearity so powerful.
- Linearity is about sums and constant multiples only. Products and non-linear
  functions need other tools.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[aX + bY + c] = a E[X] + b E[Y] + c}
$$
