# 6.6 Properties of Variance

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.6 Properties of Variance

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [6.5 Variance and Standard Deviation](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)

> **Big idea:** Shifting a random variable does not change its spread, and scaling it by $a$ multiplies the standard deviation by $\lvert a \rvert$ and the variance by $a^2$.

---

## Why It Matters

We change units all the time: Celsius to Fahrenheit, minutes to seconds, taka to
dollars. We add fixed fees and multiply by prices. Each time, we want to know the new
spread without starting over. The rules in this topic give it in one line. They
also lead to **standardization**, the way to put any variable on a common scale with
mean 0 and standard deviation 1, which is used across statistics and machine
learning.

## Intuition

Exam scores in a class have mean 60 and standard deviation 10. The teacher adds 5
bonus points to everyone. The mean becomes 65, but the scores are just as spread out
as before: the gap between any two students has not changed. A **shift** moves the
whole distribution, but does not stretch it.

Now the teacher doubles every score instead. A gap of 10 points between two
students becomes a gap of 20 points. So the standard deviation doubles, from 10 to
20. The variance is measured in **squared** points, so it grows by $2^2 = 4$, from 100
to 400. A **scale** stretches distances, and squares stretch them twice.

What about a negative scale, like multiplying by $-1$? The picture is flipped, but the
gaps are the same size. So the spread does not change. That is why the rule uses
$a^2$ (or $\lvert a \rvert$), which is never negative.

## Picture

```text
   X                #  #  #  #  #  #                                SD 1.71
   X + 8                                    #  #  #  #  #  #        SD 1.71
   2X                  #     #     #     #     #     #              SD 3.42
                 +--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+--+
                 0     2     4     6     8    10    12    14    16
```

The values of a die roll $X$: adding 8 slides them along without changing the gaps,
while doubling them doubles every gap.

## Definition

For any random variable $X$ with finite variance and any constants $a$ and $b$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)
$$

**In words:** adding a constant does not change the variance, and multiplying by $a$
multiplies the variance by $a^2$.

Taking square roots gives the rule for the standard deviation:

$$
\operatorname{SD}(aX + b) = \lvert a \rvert \thinspace \operatorname{SD}(X)
$$

**In words:** the standard deviation is multiplied by the size of the scale factor,
and the shift has no effect.

Three special cases are worth remembering:

- $\operatorname{Var}(X + b) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$: shifting changes nothing.
- $\operatorname{Var}(-X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$: flipping changes nothing.
- $\operatorname{Var}(b) = 0$: a constant has no spread.

**Standardization.** If $X$ has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma \gt 0$, the
**standardized** variable, or **z-score**, is

$$
Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

and it always has $E[Z] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Z) = 1$.

**In words:** a z-score says how many standard deviations $X$ is above (or below) its
mean.

## Why It Is True

Let $\mu = E[X]$. By linearity ([6.2](6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)), the new mean is
$E[aX + b] = a\mu + b$. Now use the definition of variance:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) &= E\Big[\big( (aX + b) - (a\mu + b) \big)^2\Big] && \text{(definition, with the new mean)} \\
&= E\big[ (aX - a\mu)^2 \big] && \text{(the } b \text{ cancels)} \\
&= E\big[ a^2 (X - \mu)^2 \big] && \text{(factor out } a \text{)} \\
&= a^2 E\big[ (X - \mu)^2 \big] && \text{(linearity)} \\
&= a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) && \text{(definition)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line is the key: the shift $b$ moves both the variable and its mean, so it
disappears from the distance. For the standard deviation, $\sqrt{a^2} = \lvert a \rvert$.

**Standardization** is the case $a = \frac{1}{\sigma}$ and $b = -\frac{\mu}{\sigma}$, because
$Z = \frac{1}{\sigma} X - \frac{\mu}{\sigma}$:

$$
E[Z] = \frac{1}{\sigma}\mu - \frac{\mu}{\sigma} = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Z) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2} = 1
$$

**What about sums?** Variance is **not** linear. For two variables, one fact is
worth knowing now: if $X$ and $Y$ are **independent**, then

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)
$$

When they are dependent, an extra term called the covariance appears. Both facts are
proved in [11.3 Variance of a Sum](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md).
Here we only use the rule to compare two situations that are easy to mix up:

- $X + X = 2X$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X + X) = 4 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- $X_1 + X_2$ with $X_1, X_2$ independent copies of $X$ has $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = 2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Celsius to Fahrenheit

**Problem.** The noon temperature in a city in April has mean 25 degrees Celsius and
standard deviation 4 degrees Celsius. In Fahrenheit, $F = 1.8C + 32$. Find the mean,
standard deviation and variance of $F$.

**Solution.**

1. Mean, by linearity: $E[F] = 1.8 \times 25 + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77$.
2. Standard deviation: $\operatorname{SD}(F) = 1.8 \times 4 = 7.2$. The $+32$ has no effect.
3. Variance:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(F) = 1.8^2 \times 4^2 = 3.24 \times 16 = 51.84
$$

**Answer.** Mean 77 degrees Fahrenheit, standard deviation $7.2$ degrees Fahrenheit,
variance $51.84$ (degrees Fahrenheit squared).

**Check.** $7.2^2 = 51.84$, so the standard deviation and the variance agree.

### Example 2: The Spread of a Dice Game

**Problem.** In the dice game of [6.1](6.1-Expected-Value.md), Example 2, you pay 5 taka,
roll a die $X$, and win $2X$ taka. Your profit is $Y = 2X - 5$. Find
$\operatorname{SD}(Y)$.

**Solution.**

1. From [6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md), $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{35}{12}$.
2. Apply the rule with $a = 2$, $b = -5$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 2^2 \cdot \frac{35}{12} = \frac{140}{12} = \frac{35}{3} \approx 11.67
$$

3. $\operatorname{SD}(Y) = \sqrt{35/3} \approx 3.416$ taka. Also $2 \times 1.708 = 3.416$.

**Answer.** The profit has mean 2 taka and standard deviation about $3.42$ taka.

**Check.** The profits are $-3, -1, 1, 3, 5, 7$, at distances $5, 3, 1, 1, 3, 5$ from the
mean 2. The average squared distance is $\frac{25 + 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 + 25}{6} = \frac{70}{6} \approx 11.67$. It matches.

### Example 3: Comparing Exam Scores With Z-Scores

**Problem.** Mina scored 84 on a mathematics exam (mean 60, SD 12) and 80 on a physics
exam (mean 70, SD 4). On which exam did she do better compared with the class?

**Solution.**

1. Mathematics: $z = \frac{84 - 60}{12} = \frac{24}{12} = 2$.
2. Physics: $z = \frac{80 - 70}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5$.

**Answer.** Physics. Her physics score is $2.5$ standard deviations above the mean;
her mathematics score is only 2.

**Check.** The raw scores (84 versus 80) point the other way. Standardization removes
the different centers and spreads, so the comparison is fair.

### Example 4: One Big Bet or Two Small Bets

**Problem.** You win or lose 100 taka on a fair coin flip, so the result $X$ is $+100$ or
$-100$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Compare (a) one bet of 200 taka, which pays $2X$,
with (b) two independent bets of 100 taka, which pay $X_1 + X_2$.

**Solution.**

1. $E[X] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 100^2 = 10000$, so $\operatorname{SD}(X) = 100$ taka.
2. (a) $\operatorname{Var}(2X) = 4 \times 10000 = 40000$, so the SD is 200 taka.
3. (b) For independent bets, $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = 10000 + 10000 = 20000$, so the
   SD is $\sqrt{20000} \approx 141.4$ taka.

**Answer.** Both have mean 0 and the same total stake, but the two independent bets
are less risky: SD about 141 taka versus 200 taka.

**Check.** With two separate flips, a loss on one is often cancelled by a win on the
other. With one big bet, there is no cancelling. This is the idea of
**diversification**.

## Simulate It

The code checks the scaling rule, the difference between $X + X$ and $X_1 + X_2$, and
standardization, all with dice.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000
var_die = 35 / 12

x1 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)   # one die
x2 = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)   # a second, independent die

# Shift and scale: Var(2X - 5) = 4 Var(X). The shift does nothing.
print(f"Var(2X - 5):   simulation {np.var(2 * x1 - 5):.4f}, exact {4 * var_die:.4f}")
# Doubling ONE roll versus adding TWO independent rolls.
print(f"Var(X1 + X1):  simulation {np.var(x1 + x1):.4f}, exact {4 * var_die:.4f}")
print(f"Var(X1 + X2):  simulation {np.var(x1 + x2):.4f}, exact {2 * var_die:.4f}")
# Standardizing gives mean 0 and variance 1.
z = (x1 - 3.5) / np.sqrt(var_die)
print(f"Standardized:  mean {z.mean():.4f}, variance {z.var():.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Var(2X - 5):   simulation 11.6608, exact 11.6667
Var(X1 + X1):  simulation 11.6608, exact 11.6667
Var(X1 + X2):  simulation 5.8231, exact 5.8333
Standardized:  mean -0.0011, variance 0.9995
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing $\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a \operatorname{Var}(X)$.** The factor is $a^2$. Only the
  standard deviation scales by $\lvert a \rvert$.
- **Letting the shift change the variance.** $\operatorname{Var}(X + 100) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
  A fixed fee or a bonus changes the mean, not the spread.
- **Getting a negative variance from a negative scale.** $\operatorname{Var}(-3X) = 9 \operatorname{Var}(X)$,
  not $-3 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- **Treating $X + X$ like two independent copies.** $\operatorname{Var}(X + X) = 4 \operatorname{Var}(X)$,
  but $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = 2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$ for independent copies.
- **Adding variances of dependent variables.** $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$
  needs independence (or at least zero covariance). See [11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md).

## Where It Is Used

- **AI and machine learning:** inputs are standardized ("feature scaling") so that every
  feature has mean 0 and variance 1 before training. Batch normalization does the
  same inside a neural network.
- **Statistics:** z-scores compare values from different scales, and the variance of an
  average of $n$ independent values is $\sigma^2 / n$, which is why more data gives more
  precise estimates ([17.3 Weak Law of Large Numbers](../17-LIMIT-THEOREMS/17.3-Weak-Law-of-Large-Numbers.md)).
- **Finance:** converting a risk to another currency scales its standard deviation by the
  exchange rate; diversification lowers risk, as in Example 4.
- **Engineering:** an amplifier with gain $a$ multiplies the noise standard deviation by
  $\lvert a \rvert$.
- **Everyday life:** a fixed delay on every trip does not make your arrival time any
  less predictable; only the varying part does.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 9$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(3X - 4)$ and $\operatorname{SD}(3X - 4)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$\operatorname{Var}(3X - 4) = 3^2 \times 9 = 81, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(3X - 4) = \sqrt{81} = 9$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Explain in one sentence why $\operatorname{Var}(-X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Flipping the sign mirrors the distribution, which keeps every distance from the mean
the same size; in the formula, $(-1)^2 = 1$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** A task takes a random time with mean 5 minutes and standard deviation 2
minutes. Give the mean, standard deviation and variance in **seconds**.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Seconds $= 60 \times$ minutes, so $a = 60$ and $b = 0$:

$$E = 60 \times 5 = 300, \qquad \operatorname{SD} = 60 \times 2 = 120, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = 120^2 = 14400$$

So 300 seconds, 120 seconds, and 14,400 seconds squared.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** For a fair die $X$, find constants $a \gt 0$ and $b$ such that $aX + b$ has
mean 0 and variance 1.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Standardize: $a = \frac{1}{\sigma}$ and $b = -\frac{\mu}{\sigma}$, with $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{35/12} \approx 1.708$:

$$a \approx \frac{1}{1.708} \approx 0.5855, \qquad b \approx -\frac{3.5}{1.708} \approx -2.049$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $X_1, X_2, X_3, X_4$ are four independent rolls of a fair die. Find the
variance of their average $\bar{X} = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + X_3 + X_4}{4}$. Use the sum rule for
independent variables and the scaling rule.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By the sum rule for independent variables, $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2 + X_3 + X_4) = 4 \cdot \frac{35}{12}$.
Dividing by 4 multiplies the variance by $\frac{1}{16}$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{1}{16} \cdot 4 \cdot \frac{35}{12} = \frac{35}{48} \approx 0.729$$

In general, the average of $n$ independent copies has variance $\sigma^2 / n$. The spread of an
average shrinks as $n$ grows.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 5 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

## Summary

- A shift does not change the spread: $\operatorname{Var}(X + b) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- A scale by $a$ multiplies the variance by $a^2$ and the SD by $\lvert a \rvert$.
- The z-score $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$ always has mean 0 and variance 1.
- Variance is not linear: $\operatorname{Var}(X + X) = 4 \operatorname{Var}(X)$, while two independent
  copies give $2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)}
$$
