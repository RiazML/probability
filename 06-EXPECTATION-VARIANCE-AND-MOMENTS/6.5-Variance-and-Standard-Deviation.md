# 6.5 Variance and Standard Deviation

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.5 Variance and Standard Deviation

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 45 minutes · **Before this:** [6.4 Indicator Trick](6.4-Indicator-Trick.md)

> **Big idea:** Variance is the average squared distance from the mean, and its square root, the standard deviation, tells you how far a typical value lands from the mean.

---

## Why It Matters

Two random variables can have the same mean and still behave very differently. A
salary of 50,000 taka every month and a salary that is either 0 or 100,000 taka have
the same average, but nobody would call them equal. **Variance** measures this
spread. In finance it measures risk, in engineering it measures noise, and in
machine learning it measures how much a model's predictions jump around.

## Intuition

Two buses go to your university. Bus A always comes exactly 10 minutes after you
reach the stop. Bus B comes either right away or after 20 minutes, each half of the
time. Both have a **mean** wait of 10 minutes. But with bus B you never know if you
will be early or late. Bus B has more spread.

How do we put a number on spread? Look at how far each value is from the mean. For
bus A the distance is always 0. For bus B it is always 10 minutes (0 is 10 below, 20
is 10 above). A natural measure is "the typical distance from the mean". For
mathematical reasons we first average the **squared** distances, which gives the
**variance**, and then take the square root to come back to minutes, which gives the
**standard deviation**. For bus B, the squared distance is always 100, so the
variance is 100 and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{100} = 10$ minutes.

## Picture

```text
   Bus A: always 10 minutes            Bus B: 0 or 20 minutes

   P(W = w)                            P(W = w)
   1.0 |           #                   1.0 |
   0.5 |           #                   0.5 | #                   #
       +-+---------+---------+-            +-+---------+---------+-
         0        10        20               0        10        20

       mean 10, SD 0                       mean 10, SD 10
       (no spread)                         (values 10 away from the mean)
```

Same mean, different spread: the standard deviation tells them apart.

## Definition

Let $X$ have mean $\mu = E[X]$. The **variance** of $X$ is the expected squared distance
from the mean:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = \sum_{x} (x - \mu)^2 \thinspace p(x)
$$

where $p(x)$ is the PMF of $X$ and the second form is LOTUS ([6.3](6.3-LOTUS.md)) with
$g(x) = (x - \mu)^2$.

**In words:** for each value, square its distance from the mean, and average these
squares using the probabilities.

The **standard deviation** is the square root of the variance:

$$
\sigma = \operatorname{SD}(X) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}
$$

**In words:** the standard deviation is the spread measured in the **same units** as
$X$ (minutes, taka, meters), while the variance is in squared units.

We write $\sigma^2$ for the variance. A very useful way to compute it is the
**shortcut formula**:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2
$$

**In words:** the variance is the mean of the square minus the square of the mean.

The variance exists (is finite) when $E[X^2]$ is finite.

## Why It Is True

**The shortcut formula.** Expand the square and use linearity. Remember that
$\mu$ is a constant, a plain number:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] && \text{(definition)} \\
&= E\big[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2\big] && \text{(expand the square)} \\
&= E[X^2] - 2\mu E[X] + \mu^2 && \text{(linearity)} \\
&= E[X^2] - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 && \text{(} E[X] = \mu \text{)} \\
&= E[X^2] - \mu^2 && \text{(simplify)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Variance is never negative.** Each term $(x - \mu)^2 \thinspace p(x)$ is at least 0, so
$\operatorname{Var}(X) \ge 0$. Combined with the shortcut, this proves a fact that is used
everywhere:

$$
E[X^2] \ge (E[X])^2
$$

**In words:** the average of the squares is at least the square of the average.

**Variance is zero only for a constant.** If $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0$, then a sum of
non-negative terms $(x - \mu)^2 \thinspace p(x)$ is 0, so every term is 0. For each $x \ne \mu$
the factor $(x - \mu)^2$ is positive, so $p(x)$ must be 0. Therefore $P(X = \mu) = 1$:
$X$ is (with probability 1) the constant $\mu$.

**Why squares?** Squares are never negative, so distances below and above the mean
cannot cancel (the plain average distance $E[X - \mu]$ is always 0). The average
absolute distance $E[\lvert X - \mu \rvert]$ also works, but squares follow much nicer
algebra: the shortcut formula above, and the rules for sums in
[6.6](6.6-Properties-of-Variance.md) and [11.3](../11-COVARIANCE-AND-CORRELATION/11.3-Variance-of-a-Sum.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Variance of a Die

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. Find the variance and standard deviation, in two ways.

**Solution.**

1. We know $\mu = 3.5$ and, from [6.3](6.3-LOTUS.md), $E[X^2] = \frac{91}{6}$.
2. **Shortcut:**

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{91}{6} - 3.5^2 = \frac{91}{6} - \frac{49}{4} = \frac{182}{12} - \frac{147}{12} = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917
$$

3. **Definition:** the distances from 3.5 are $-2.5, -1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5$, and their
   squares are $6.25, 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25, 6.25$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{6.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 + 6.25}{6} = \frac{17.5}{6} \approx 2.917
$$

4. Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{35/12} \approx 1.708$.

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917$ and $\sigma \approx 1.708$.

**Check.** The distances from the mean are $0.5$, $1.5$ or $2.5$, so a "typical"
distance of about $1.7$ makes sense. The simulation agrees.

### Example 2: Variance of a Yes/No Variable

**Problem.** $X$ is 1 with probability $p$ and 0 with probability $1 - p$ (a
**Bernoulli** variable). Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. For which $p$ is it largest?

**Solution.**

1. $E[X] = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot (1 - p) = p$.
2. Since $X$ is 0 or 1, $X^2 = X$, so $E[X^2] = p$.
3. Shortcut:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = p(1 - p)
$$

4. $p(1 - p)$ is a downward parabola with roots at 0 and 1, so it is largest in the
   middle, at $p = \frac{1}{2}$, where it equals $\frac{1}{4}$.

**Answer.** $\operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1 - p)$, largest ($\frac{1}{4}$) when $p = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Check.** At $p = 0$ or $p = 1$ the result is certain, and the variance is 0, as it
should be. A fair coin is the most uncertain yes/no variable.

### Example 3: Two Investments With the Same Mean

**Problem.** Investment A gains 1,000 taka for sure. Investment B gains 3,000 taka or
loses 1,000 taka, each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. Compare their means and standard
deviations.

**Solution.**

1. Means: $E[A] = 1000$, and

$$
E[B] = 3000 \times 0.5 + (-1000) \times 0.5 = 1500 - 500 = 1000
$$

2. A is a constant, so $\operatorname{Var}(A) = 0$.
3. For B, both values are 2,000 taka away from the mean of 1,000, so each squared
   distance is $2000^2 = 4{,}000{,}000$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(B) = 4000000 \times 0.5 + 4000000 \times 0.5 = 4000000 \ \text{taka}^2
$$

4. $\operatorname{SD}(B) = \sqrt{4000000} = 2000$ taka.

**Answer.** Same mean (1,000 taka), but B has a standard deviation of 2,000 taka and
A has 0.

**Check.** The variance is in "taka squared", which has no everyday meaning. The
standard deviation, 2,000 taka, is easy to read: B's result is typically 2,000
taka away from its mean. Investors call this spread **risk** (see
[22.4 Variance and Risk](../22-PROBABILITY-FOR-LIFE/22.4-Variance-and-Risk.md)).

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 1 with both formulas, and the standard deviation of
investment B in Example 3.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Die roll: Var(X) = 35/12, SD = sqrt(35/12).
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n_trials)
mu = rolls.mean()
var_def = np.mean((rolls - mu) ** 2)              # E[(X - mu)^2]
var_short = np.mean(rolls ** 2) - mu ** 2         # E[X^2] - mu^2
print(f"Die Var: definition {var_def:.4f}, shortcut {var_short:.4f}, "
      f"exact {35 / 12:.4f}")
print(f"Die SD:  simulation {np.sqrt(var_def):.4f}, exact {np.sqrt(35 / 12):.4f}")

# Investment B: +3000 or -1000 taka, each with probability 1/2.
gain_b = rng.choice([3000, -1000], size=n_trials)
print(f"B mean: simulation {gain_b.mean():8.1f}, exact {1000:8.1f}")
print(f"B SD:   simulation {gain_b.std():8.1f}, exact {2000:8.1f}")
```

Output:

```text
Die Var: definition 2.9152, shortcut 2.9152, exact 2.9167
Die SD:  simulation 1.7074, exact 1.7078
B mean: simulation   1000.4, exact   1000.0
B SD:   simulation   2000.0, exact   2000.0
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to square the mean in the shortcut.** It is $E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$, not
  $E[X^2] - E[X]$. For a die, $\frac{91}{6} - 3.5$ is wrong; $\frac{91}{6} - 12.25$ is right.
- **Mixing up $E[X^2]$ and $(E[X])^2$.** The first squares each value and then averages;
  the second averages and then squares. Their difference is the variance.
- **Getting a negative variance.** That is always an arithmetic error. Check the
  calculation; a common cause is rounding $E[X^2]$ and $\mu$ too early.
- **Reporting variance in the wrong units.** If $X$ is in minutes, the variance is in
  minutes squared. Report the standard deviation when you want to talk about spread
  in everyday units.
- **Thinking the standard deviation is the largest possible distance.** Values can be
  many standard deviations from the mean. How likely that is depends on the
  distribution (see [16.2 Chebyshev Inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.2-Chebyshev-Inequality.md)).

## Where It Is Used

- **Finance:** the standard deviation of a return is the standard measure of risk.
- **AI and machine learning:** the bias-variance trade-off describes how a model's
  error comes from being wrong on average (bias) and from jumping around between
  training sets (variance).
- **Computer science:** the variance of a randomized algorithm's running time tells
  you how often it is much slower than average; the variance of a Monte Carlo
  estimate tells you how many samples you need ([19.3 Monte Carlo Estimation](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.3-Monte-Carlo-Estimation.md)).
- **Engineering and science:** measurement noise is described by its standard
  deviation.
- **Everyday life:** a bus that is late "by 5 minutes on average, give or take 1
  minute" is easier to plan around than one that is "5 minutes late, give or take 15".

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ takes the values 0, 1, 2 with probabilities $\frac{1}{4}$, $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{4}$.
Find $E[X]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 0 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = 1$$

$$E[X^2] = 0 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = 1.5$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 1.5 - 1^2 = 0.5$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X = 7$ always. What are $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\operatorname{SD}(X)$?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mean is 7 and every value is 0 away from it, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0$ and
$\operatorname{SD}(X) = 0$. A constant has no spread.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X$ takes the values 1, 2, 3 with probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$, $0.3$. Find
$\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\operatorname{SD}(X)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 0.2 + 1.0 + 0.9 = 2.1$$

$$E[X^2] = 1 \times 0.2 + 4 \times 0.5 + 9 \times 0.3 = 0.2 + 2.0 + 2.7 = 4.9$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4.9 - 2.1^2 = 4.9 - 4.41 = 0.49, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(X) = \sqrt{0.49} = 0.7$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** On an exam, a student scores 0 with probability $0.1$, 50 with
probability $0.6$ and 100 with probability $0.3$. Find the mean and standard deviation
of the score.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 0 \times 0.1 + 50 \times 0.6 + 100 \times 0.3 = 0 + 30 + 30 = 60$$

$$E[X^2] = 0 + 2500 \times 0.6 + 10000 \times 0.3 = 1500 + 3000 = 4500$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4500 - 60^2 = 4500 - 3600 = 900, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(X) = 30$$

The score is typically about 30 points away from 60.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Suppose $a \le X \le b$ always. Prove that $\operatorname{Var}(X) \le \frac{(b - a)^2}{4}$.
(Hint: the product $(X - a)(b - X)$ is never negative.)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Since $a \le X \le b$, both $X - a$ and $b - X$ are at least 0, so
$(X - a)(b - X) = -X^2 + (a + b)X - ab \ge 0$. Take expected values (linearity), with $\mu = E[X]$:

$$-E[X^2] + (a + b)\mu - ab \ge 0, \quad \text{so} \quad E[X^2] \le (a + b)\mu - ab$$

Subtract $\mu^2$ from both sides and factor:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2 \le (a + b)\mu - ab - \mu^2 = (\mu - a)(b - \mu)$$

Two non-negative numbers with a fixed sum $(\mu - a) + (b - \mu) = b - a$ have the largest
product when they are equal, so

$$(\mu - a)(b - \mu) \le \left(\frac{b - a}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{(b - a)^2}{4}$$

Equality holds when $X$ is $a$ or $b$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each, like bus B.

</details>


</details>

## Summary

- Variance is the average squared distance from the mean: $E[(X - \mu)^2]$.
- The standard deviation is its square root, in the same units as $X$.
- The shortcut $E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$ is usually the fastest way to compute it.
- Variance is never negative, and it is 0 only for a constant. So $E[X^2] \ge (E[X])^2$.
- Same mean does not mean same risk.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2}
$$
