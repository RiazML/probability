# 6.8 Median, Mode and Quantiles

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.8 Median, Mode and Quantiles

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 40 minutes · **Before this:** [6.7 Moments, Skewness and Kurtosis](6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)

> **Big idea:** The median is the middle value, the mode is the most likely value, and a quantile is the value below which a given fraction of the probability lies; all three are read from the PMF or the CDF, not computed as averages.

---

## Why It Matters

The mean is not always the best summary. When a distribution is skewed, the mean is
pulled toward the long tail, and it can be far from what a "typical" value looks
like. The **median** and **mode** describe the typical value better in such cases. The
**quantiles** describe the tails: engineers judge a web service by its 99th
percentile latency, not only by its average. These ideas come back for continuous
variables in [8.7 Quantile Function](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.7-Quantile-Function.md).

## Intuition

A small company has 10 employees. Nine earn 30 thousand taka a month. The boss earns
600 thousand. The **mean** salary is $(9 \times 30 + 600) / 10 = 87$ thousand taka. But nobody
in the company earns anything close to 87 thousand! Nine people earn much less and one
earns much more.

Now line up the salaries from smallest to largest. The value in the middle is 30
thousand: half of the people earn at most that, and half earn at least that. That is
the **median**. The most common salary is also 30 thousand. That is the **mode**. Both
describe a typical employee much better than the mean does.

If the boss gets a raise to 6,000 thousand taka, the mean jumps to 627 thousand, but the
median and mode do not move at all. The median only cares about the **order** of the
values, not about how far out the extreme values are.

## Picture

```text
  F(x)
  1.00 |                                    +-----
  0.83 |                              +-----+
  0.67 |                        +-----+
  0.50 |. . . . . . . . . +-----+     <- the level 0.5 runs along
  0.33 |            +-----+              the flat step from 3 to 4
  0.17 |      +-----+
  0.00 +------+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----
              1     2     3     4     5     6      x
```

The CDF of a fair die: to find a quantile, go across at height $q$ until you meet the
staircase. At $q = 0.5$ the line meets a flat step, so every point from 3 to 4 is a median.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with PMF $p(x)$ and CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ (see
[5.4 Cumulative Distribution Function](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.4-Cumulative-Distribution-Function.md)).

A **median** of $X$ is any number $m$ with

$$
P(X \le m) \ge \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad P(X \ge m) \ge \frac{1}{2}
$$

**In words:** at least half of the probability is at or below $m$, and at least half is at
or above $m$.

A discrete variable can have a whole interval of medians, as the die in the picture
does. When one number is needed, this book uses the smallest one (see quantiles below);
many people and programs use the midpoint of the interval instead.

A **mode** of $X$ is a value where the PMF is largest:

$$
\text{mode} = \text{a value } x \text{ that maximizes } p(x)
$$

**In words:** the mode is the single most likely value. There can be several modes when
several values tie.

For a number $q$ between 0 and 1, the **$q$-quantile** of $X$ is the smallest value at which
the CDF reaches $q$:

$$
Q(q) = \min \lbrace x : F(x) \ge q \rbrace
$$

**In words:** $Q(q)$ is the first value where the probability of being at or below it reaches
$q$. At least a fraction $q$ of the probability is at or below $Q(q)$. (The smallest such
value always exists, because a CDF jumps up at a point and keeps the upper value there.)

Special quantiles have names: $Q(0.5)$ is the median; $Q(0.25)$ and $Q(0.75)$ are the
**first** and **third quartiles**; $Q(k/100)$ is the **$k$-th percentile**, and engineers write
it as "p99" for $k = 99$. The distance $Q(0.75) - Q(0.25)$ is the **interquartile range**
(IQR), a measure of spread that, like the median, ignores extreme values.

## Why It Is True

**The quantile at one half is always a median.** Let $m = Q(0.5)$, the smallest $x$ with
$F(x) \ge \frac{1}{2}$. We check the two conditions of the definition.

1. $P(X \le m) = F(m) \ge \frac{1}{2}$, by the choice of $m$.
2. Every $x \lt m$ has $F(x) \lt \frac{1}{2}$, because $m$ is the **smallest** value with
   $F \ge \frac{1}{2}$. As $x$ grows up to $m$, the events $\lbrace X \le x \rbrace$ grow up to
   $\lbrace X \lt m \rbrace$, so by [3.9 Continuity of Probability](../03-AXIOMS-OF-PROBABILITY/3.9-Continuity-of-Probability.md)
   $P(X \lt m)$ is the limit of numbers below $\frac{1}{2}$, and $P(X \lt m) \le \frac{1}{2}$. Taking the complement:

$$
P(X \ge m) = 1 - P(X \lt m) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

Both conditions hold, so $Q(0.5)$ is a median. A median always exists, even when the mean
does not (like the St. Petersburg game in [6.1](6.1-Expected-Value.md)).

**Why the median ignores extremes.** The conditions for $m$ only involve the
probabilities $P(X \le m)$ and $P(X \ge m)$. If you move a value that is already above $m$
even further up, these probabilities do not change, so $m$ stays a median. The mean, in
contrast, changes by (distance moved) $\times$ (its probability), which can be huge.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Median, Mode and Quartiles of a Die

**Problem.** For a fair die, find all medians, the mode, $Q(0.5)$, $Q(0.25)$ and $Q(0.75)$.

**Solution.**

1. The CDF is $F(1) = \frac{1}{6}$, $F(2) = \frac{2}{6}$, $F(3) = \frac{3}{6}$, $F(4) = \frac{4}{6}$,
   $F(5) = \frac{5}{6}$, $F(6) = 1$.
2. Medians: for any $m$ from 3 to 4, $P(X \le m) \ge P(X \le 3) = \frac{1}{2}$ and
   $P(X \ge m) \ge P(X \ge 4) = \frac{1}{2}$. For $m$ below 3, $P(X \le m) \le \frac{2}{6}$, which is too
   small; for $m$ above 4, $P(X \ge m) \le \frac{2}{6}$, also too small. So the medians are exactly
   the interval $[3, 4]$.
3. Mode: all six faces have the same probability, so every face is a mode.
4. $Q(0.5)$: the first value with $F(x) \ge 0.5$ is $x = 3$.
5. $Q(0.25)$: $F(1) = 0.167 \lt 0.25$ and $F(2) = 0.333 \ge 0.25$, so $Q(0.25) = 2$.
6. $Q(0.75)$: $F(4) = 0.667 \lt 0.75$ and $F(5) = 0.833 \ge 0.75$, so $Q(0.75) = 5$.

**Answer.** Medians: every $m$ in $[3, 4]$ (the midpoint is $3.5$). Every face is a mode.
$Q(0.5) = 3$, $Q(0.25) = 2$, $Q(0.75) = 5$, so the IQR is 3.

**Check.** The die is symmetric about $3.5$, and $3.5$ is in the middle of the interval of
medians. For a symmetric distribution, the mean and the middle of the medians agree.

### Example 2: Salaries in a Small Company

**Problem.** Pick a random employee of the company in the Intuition, and let $S$ be the
salary in thousands of taka: $S = 30$ with probability $0.9$ and $S = 600$ with probability
$0.1$. Find the mean, median and mode.

**Solution.**

1. Mean: $E[S] = 30 \times 0.9 + 600 \times 0.1 = 27 + 60 = 87$.
2. Median: try $m = 30$. $P(S \le 30) = 0.9 \ge 0.5$ and $P(S \ge 30) = 1 \ge 0.5$. Both hold.
   For $m \lt 30$, $P(S \le m) = 0$; for $m \gt 30$, $P(S \ge m) \le 0.1$. So 30 is the only median.
3. Mode: $p(30) = 0.9$ is the largest, so the mode is 30.

**Answer.** Mean 87 thousand taka, median 30 thousand, mode 30 thousand.

**Check.** The distribution is right-skewed (a long tail toward the boss), and the mean is
pulled toward the tail, far above the median. This pattern (mean above median) is
typical of right-skewed distributions like incomes and house prices.

### Example 3: Percentile Latency of a Web Service

**Problem.** The response time $T$ of a web service, in milliseconds, has this PMF:

| $t$ (ms) | 10 | 20 | 200 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $p(t)$ | 0.90 | 0.08 | 0.015 | 0.005 |

Find the mean, the median (p50), p95, p99 and p99.9.

**Solution.**

1. Mean: $E[T] = 10(0.90) + 20(0.08) + 200(0.015) + 1000(0.005) = 9 + 1.6 + 3 + 5 = 18.6$.
2. CDF: $F(10) = 0.90$, $F(20) = 0.98$, $F(200) = 0.995$, $F(1000) = 1$.
3. p50: $F(10) = 0.90 \ge 0.5$, so $Q(0.5) = 10$.
4. p95: $F(10) = 0.90 \lt 0.95$ but $F(20) = 0.98 \ge 0.95$, so $Q(0.95) = 20$.
5. p99: $F(20) = 0.98 \lt 0.99$ but $F(200) = 0.995 \ge 0.99$, so $Q(0.99) = 200$.
6. p99.9: $F(200) = 0.995 \lt 0.999$, so $Q(0.999) = 1000$.

**Answer.** Mean $18.6$ ms; p50 = 10 ms, p95 = 20 ms, p99 = 200 ms, p99.9 = 1000 ms.

**Check.** The mean (18.6 ms) is almost twice the typical time (10 ms) and says nothing about
the 1 user in 200 who waits a full second. A web page that makes 100 such requests will
very often hit at least one slow one, which is why engineers watch p99 and p99.9.

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 3. The option `method="inverted_cdf"` makes `numpy` use exactly our
definition: the smallest value whose (sample) CDF reaches $q$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_requests = 1_000_000

# Request latency in ms and its PMF.
values = np.array([10, 20, 200, 1000])
probs = np.array([0.90, 0.08, 0.015, 0.005])
latency = rng.choice(values, size=n_requests, p=probs)

# "inverted_cdf" = smallest value whose CDF reaches q (our definition).
for q, exact in [(0.50, 10), (0.95, 20), (0.99, 200), (0.999, 1000)]:
    est = np.quantile(latency, q, method="inverted_cdf")
    print(f"Q({q}): simulation {est:6.0f} ms, exact {exact:6d} ms")
print(f"Mean:     simulation {latency.mean():6.2f} ms, exact {np.sum(values * probs):6.2f} ms")
```

Output:

```text
Q(0.5): simulation     10 ms, exact     10 ms
Q(0.95): simulation     20 ms, exact     20 ms
Q(0.99): simulation    200 ms, exact    200 ms
Q(0.999): simulation   1000 ms, exact   1000 ms
Mean:     simulation  18.64 ms, exact  18.60 ms
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the mean as "the typical value" for skewed data.** For incomes, house prices or
  latencies, report the median (and some percentiles) as well.
- **Expecting one unique median.** A discrete variable can have a whole interval of medians,
  like $[3, 4]$ for a die. Say which convention you use.
- **Confusing the mode with the mean.** The mode is the most likely single value; the mean
  is a weighted average and may not even be a possible value.
- **Asking for a quantile exactly at a step of the CDF from data.** In Example 3,
  $F(10) = 0.90$ exactly, so a sample's 0.9-quantile can come out as 10 or 20 depending on
  tiny random changes. Different software also uses different conventions there.
- **Reading $Q(q)$ as the point where $P(X \le x) = q$ exactly.** For a discrete variable, $F$ jumps, so
  usually no $x$ gives exactly $q$. The quantile is where $F$ first **reaches or passes** $q$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** service level objectives are written with percentiles ("p99
  latency under 200 ms"), because users notice the slow tail, not the mean.
- **AI and machine learning:** the median is the best prediction under absolute error
  ([6.10](6.10-Expectation-as-Best-Guess.md)), and **quantile regression** predicts
  percentiles to give prediction intervals. A classifier's output label is the **mode** of
  its predicted distribution.
- **Statistics:** box plots show the median and quartiles, and the IQR is used to flag
  outliers.
- **Economics:** median income is reported instead of mean income because a few very rich
  people pull the mean up.
- **Everyday life:** "half of the deliveries arrive within 2 days" is a median; "95% within 5
  days" is a percentile.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** Roll two dice and let $S$ be the sum. Find its mode and its median.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The mode is 7, the most likely sum ($\frac{6}{36}$). For the median, $P(S \le 7) = \frac{21}{36} \ge \frac{1}{2}$
and $P(S \ge 7) = \frac{21}{36} \ge \frac{1}{2}$, so 7 is a median. Values just below 7 give
$P(S \le m) = \frac{15}{36} \lt \frac{1}{2}$, and values just above 7 give $P(S \ge m) = \frac{15}{36} \lt \frac{1}{2}$,
so 7 is the only median.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** $X$ has PMF $p(1) = 0.1$, $p(2) = 0.2$, $p(3) = 0.4$, $p(4) = 0.3$. Find the mean,
median, mode, $Q(0.25)$ and $Q(0.9)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 0.1 + 0.4 + 1.2 + 1.2 = 2.9$$

The CDF is $0.1, 0.3, 0.7, 1.0$ at $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$. The median is $Q(0.5) = 3$ (and it is unique,
since $P(X \ge 3) = 0.7$ and $P(X \le 3) = 0.7$). The mode is 3. $Q(0.25) = 2$ because $F(1) = 0.1 \lt 0.25 \le F(2) = 0.3$,
and $Q(0.9) = 4$ because $F(3) = 0.7 \lt 0.9$.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** $X$ is 0 or 1 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Find all medians of $X$, and $Q(0.5)$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For any $m$ with $0 \le m \le 1$: $P(X \le m) \ge P(X = 0) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $P(X \ge m) \ge P(X = 1) = \frac{1}{2}$.
So every $m$ in $[0, 1]$ is a median. $Q(0.5) = 0$, since $F(0) = \frac{1}{2}$ already reaches $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** In Example 2, the boss's salary rises from 600 to 6,000 thousand taka. What
happens to the mean and the median?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[S] = 30 \times 0.9 + 6000 \times 0.1 = 27 + 600 = 627$$

The mean jumps from 87 to 627 thousand taka. The median stays at 30 thousand, because
$P(S \le 30) = 0.9$ and $P(S \ge 30) = 1$ have not changed.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** $N$ is the number of trials until the first success, with success probability
$p = 0.1$ on each independent trial, so $P(N \le k) = 1 - 0.9^k$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ and
$E[N] = \frac{1}{p} = 10$ (see [7.3 Geometric](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md)). Find the median of $N$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

We need the smallest $k$ with $1 - 0.9^k \ge 0.5$, that is, $0.9^k \le 0.5$:

$$k \ge \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln 0.9} \approx \frac{-0.6931}{-0.1054} \approx 6.58$$

So $Q(0.5) = 7$. Check: $1 - 0.9^6 \approx 0.469 \lt 0.5$ and $1 - 0.9^7 \approx 0.522 \ge 0.5$. It is the only
median, because $P(N \ge 8) = 0.9^7 \approx 0.478 \lt 0.5$. The median (7) is below the mean (10), as
expected for a right-skewed distribution.

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 5 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
p = 0.1
trials = rng.geometric(p, size=1_000_000)   # trials until first success
print(f"Median: simulation {np.quantile(trials, 0.5, method='inverted_cdf'):.0f}, exact 7")
print(f"Mean:   simulation {trials.mean():.4f}, exact {1 / p:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Median: simulation 7, exact 7
Mean:   simulation 9.9981, exact 10.0000
```

</details>

## Summary

- The median splits the probability in half; a discrete variable may have an interval of
  medians.
- The mode is the most likely value; there may be several.
- The $q$-quantile is the first value where the CDF reaches $q$; the median is $Q(0.5)$.
- The median and quantiles ignore how extreme the extreme values are; the mean does not.
- For right-skewed distributions, the mean is usually above the median.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{Q(q) = \min \lbrace x : F(x) \ge q \rbrace}
$$
