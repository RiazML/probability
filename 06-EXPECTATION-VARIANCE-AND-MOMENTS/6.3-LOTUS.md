# 6.3 LOTUS

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.3 LOTUS

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [6.2 Linearity of Expectation](6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md)

> **Big idea:** To find the expected value of $g(X)$, you do not need the PMF of $g(X)$: just average $g(x)$ using the PMF of $X$.

---

## Why It Matters

We often care about a **function** of a random variable: the square of an error, the
profit from a random demand, the running time $n^2$ of an algorithm on a random
input size. LOTUS gives the expected value of any such function in one step. It is
also the tool we need for variance ([6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)), for
moments ([6.7](6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)), and later for moment
generating functions.

## Intuition

You roll a fair die and win the **square** of the number, in taka. What is your
average win? One way: work out the PMF of the win, which takes the values 1, 4, 9,
16, 25, 36, each with probability $\frac{1}{6}$, and average those. Here that is easy.

But now think about why it works. In 600 rolls, the face 4 comes up about 100 times,
and each time you win 16 taka. So you can go through the faces of the die one by
one, and for each face add "what you win on that face" times "how often that face
comes up". You never need to build a new PMF. You just re-label each face with
its prize.

The name **LOTUS** stands for "Law Of The Unconscious Statistician". It is a joke:
people use this rule so naturally that they forget it needs a proof.

## Picture

```text
     x     p(x)     g(x) = x^2               y     P(Y = y)
   ----    ----     ----------             ----    --------
    -2     0.2  -->     4  ---+
     2     0.2  -->     4  ---+--------->    4       0.4
    -1     0.2  -->     1  ---+
     1     0.2  -->     1  ---+--------->    1       0.4
     0     0.2  -->     0  ------------->    0       0.2

   LOTUS uses the left side.        The long way builds the right side.
```

Both sides give the same average, $E[X^2] = 2$, because merging rows with the same
value of $g(x)$ only adds their probabilities.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with PMF $p_X(x)$, and let $g$ be any function
from numbers to numbers. The **law of the unconscious statistician (LOTUS)** says

$$
E[g(X)] = \sum_{x} g(x) \thinspace p_X(x)
$$

where:

- the sum runs over the support of $X$ (not of $g(X)$);
- $g(x)$ is the value of the new variable when $X = x$;
- $p_X(x)$ is the PMF of the **original** variable $X$.

The formula holds whenever $\sum_x \lvert g(x) \rvert \thinspace p_X(x) \lt \infty$, which is
the condition for $E[g(X)]$ to exist.

**In words:** to average $g(X)$, average the numbers $g(x)$, weighting each one by
the probability of $x$.

The most important special cases are the **moments**: $E[X^2] = \sum_x x^2 \thinspace p_X(x)$,
and more generally $E[X^k] = \sum_x x^k \thinspace p_X(x)$.

## Why It Is True

Let $Y = g(X)$. From [5.6 Functions of a Random Variable](../05-DISCRETE-RANDOM-VARIABLES/5.6-Functions-of-a-Random-Variable.md),
the PMF of $Y$ collects all the $x$ values that map to the same $y$:

$$
p_Y(y) = \sum_{x : g(x) = y} p_X(x)
$$

Now compute $E[Y]$ from its definition and regroup:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[g(X)] = E[Y] &= \sum_{y} y \thinspace p_Y(y) && \text{(definition of expected value)} \\
&= \sum_{y} y \sum_{x : g(x) = y} p_X(x) && \text{(PMF of } Y \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{y} \ \sum_{x : g(x) = y} g(x) \thinspace p_X(x) && \text{(inside the group, } y = g(x) \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{x} g(x) \thinspace p_X(x) && \text{(each } x \text{ is in exactly one group)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step works because the groups $\lbrace x : g(x) = y \rbrace$, one for each $y$,
split the support of $X$ into pieces that do not overlap. Adding group by group, or
$x$ by $x$, gives the same total (the sum converges absolutely, so the order does
not matter).

**A check with linearity.** Take $g(x) = ax + b$. LOTUS gives
$\sum_x (ax + b) \thinspace p_X(x) = a \sum_x x \thinspace p_X(x) + b \sum_x p_X(x) = aE[X] + b$,
which matches [6.2](6.2-Linearity-of-Expectation.md).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Both Ways for the Picture

**Problem.** $X$ is equally likely to be $-2, -1, 0, 1, 2$. Find $E[X^2]$ with LOTUS,
and then the long way. Compare with $(E[X])^2$.

**Solution.**

1. LOTUS: each value has probability $0.2$, so

$$
E[X^2] = 0.2 \cdot \big( (-2)^2 + (-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 \big) = 0.2 \cdot (4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4) = 0.2 \cdot 10 = 2
$$

2. The long way: $Y = X^2$ takes the value 0 with probability $0.2$, the value 1 with
   probability $0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4$, and the value 4 with probability $0.4$. So

$$
E[Y] = 0 \times 0.2 + 1 \times 0.4 + 4 \times 0.4 = 0 + 0.4 + 1.6 = 2
$$

3. By symmetry $E[X] = 0$, so $(E[X])^2 = 0$.

**Answer.** $E[X^2] = 2$, while $(E[X])^2 = 0$. They are very different.

**Check.** Both methods agree, as the proof promises.

### Example 2: The Square of a Die Roll

**Problem.** Roll a fair die. Find $E[X^2]$.

**Solution.**

$$
E[X^2] = \frac{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 + 6^2}{6} = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17
$$

**Answer.** $E[X^2] = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17$.

**Check.** $(E[X])^2 = 3.5^2 = 12.25$, which is smaller. Squaring large values makes
them count more, so the average of squares is bigger than the square of the average.
This gap is the **variance** ([6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md)).

### Example 3: How Many Cakes to Bake

**Problem.** A bakery bakes 3 cakes each morning, at a cost of 100 taka each, and sells
each cake for 250 taka. Unsold cakes are thrown away. The daily demand $D$ (number of
customers who want a cake) has this PMF:

| $d$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $p(d)$ | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.1 |

Find the expected daily profit.

**Solution.**

1. The bakery sells $\min(D, 3)$ cakes, because it cannot sell more than 3. So the profit is
   $g(D) = 250 \min(D, 3) - 300$ taka.
2. Make a table of $g(d)$:
   - $d = 0$: $g = 0 - 300 = -300$
   - $d = 1$: $g = 250 - 300 = -50$
   - $d = 2$: $g = 500 - 300 = 200$
   - $d = 3, 4, 5$: $g = 750 - 300 = 450$
3. Use LOTUS:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[g(D)] &= (-300)(0.1) + (-50)(0.2) + (200)(0.3) + (450)(0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1) \\
&= -30 - 10 + 60 + 180 = 200
\end{aligned}
$$

**Answer.** The expected profit is 200 taka per day.

**Check.** A tempting shortcut is to plug in the mean demand. Here
$E[D] = 0(0.1) + 1(0.2) + 2(0.3) + 3(0.2) + 4(0.1) + 5(0.1) = 2.3$, and
$g(2.3) = 250 \times 2.3 - 300 = 275$ taka. That is too high, because on busy days the
extra customers are lost while on quiet days the cakes are wasted. In general
$E[g(D)] \ne g(E[D])$. The simulation confirms 200.

### Example 4: Average Running Time of a Quadratic Algorithm

**Problem.** An algorithm takes $n^2$ microseconds on an input of size $n$. The input
size $N$ is 10, 20 or 30, each with probability $\frac{1}{3}$. Find the expected running
time, and compare it with the time for the average size.

**Solution.**

1. LOTUS with $g(n) = n^2$:

$$
E[N^2] = \frac{10^2 + 20^2 + 30^2}{3} = \frac{100 + 400 + 900}{3} = \frac{1400}{3} \approx 466.7
$$

2. The average size is $E[N] = \frac{10 + 20 + 30}{3} = 20$, and $20^2 = 400$.

**Answer.** The expected time is about 466.7 microseconds, not 400.

**Check.** Running the algorithm on an "average" input underestimates the average
running time. For any **convex** cost (one that curves upward, like $n^2$), the plug-in
answer is too small. This is **Jensen's inequality**, see
[16.3 Jensen Inequality](../16-INEQUALITIES-AND-CONCENTRATION/16.3-Jensen-Inequality.md).

## Simulate It

The code checks Example 3: the bakery's average profit, next to the wrong
"plug in the mean" answer.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_days = 1_000_000

# Daily demand D for cakes and its PMF.
values = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
probs = np.array([0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, 0.1])
demand = rng.choice(values, size=n_days, p=probs)

# The bakery bakes 3 cakes (100 taka each) and sells them at 250 taka.
profit = 250 * np.minimum(demand, 3) - 300

exact = np.sum((250 * np.minimum(values, 3) - 300) * probs)   # LOTUS
wrong = 250 * min(np.sum(values * probs), 3) - 300            # g(E[D])
print(f"E[profit]: simulation {profit.mean():.2f}, LOTUS {exact:.2f}")
print(f"g(E[D]) (wrong):        {wrong:.2f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[profit]: simulation 200.06, LOTUS 200.00
g(E[D]) (wrong):        275.00
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Plugging the mean into the function.** $E[g(X)] = g(E[X])$ is true for linear
  $g(x) = ax + b$, and false in general. Examples 1, 3 and 4 all show the gap.
- **Using the PMF of the wrong variable.** In LOTUS the weights are $p_X(x)$, the PMF of
  $X$, not of $g(X)$. You do not need the PMF of $g(X)$ at all.
- **Forgetting to apply $g$ to every value.** In Example 3, the values $d = 4$ and
  $d = 5$ still give profit 450, not $250 \times 4 - 300$. Write the table of $g(x)$ first.
- **Squaring the sum of probabilities.** $E[X^2]$ squares the **values**, not the
  probabilities: $\sum_x x^2 \thinspace p(x)$, not $\sum_x x \thinspace p(x)^2$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Variance and moments:** $E[X^2]$, $E[(X - \mu)^2]$ and $E[X^k]$ are all LOTUS
  computations ([6.5](6.5-Variance-and-Standard-Deviation.md), [6.7](6.7-Moments-Skewness-and-Kurtosis.md)).
- **AI and machine learning:** the expected loss $E[\ell(Y, \hat{y})]$ of a prediction
  $\hat{y}$ is LOTUS with $g(y) = \ell(y, \hat{y})$. The cross-entropy loss is the expected
  value of $-\log$ of a probability.
- **Computer science:** average-case analysis of an algorithm with cost $g(n)$ on a
  random input size $n$ (Example 4).
- **Operations and business:** stock levels, like the bakery in Example 3. This is
  the classic "newsvendor problem".
- **Information theory:** entropy is $E[-\log_2 p(X)]$, LOTUS with $g(x) = -\log_2 p(x)$.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ takes the values 1, 2, 3 with probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$, $0.3$.
Find $E[X^2]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X^2] = 1 \times 0.2 + 4 \times 0.5 + 9 \times 0.3 = 0.2 + 2.0 + 2.7 = 4.9$$

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Flip two fair coins and let $X$ be the number of heads. Find $E[2^X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$X$ is 0, 1, 2 with probabilities $\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}$:

$$E[2^X] = 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.25 + 1 + 1 = 2.25$$

Note $2^{E[X]} = 2^1 = 2$, which is smaller.

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Roll a fair die. Find $E[1/X]$ and compare it with $1/E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[1/X] = \frac{1}{6} \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{5} + \tfrac{1}{6}\right) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{49}{20} = \frac{49}{120} \approx 0.408$$

But $1/E[X] = 1/3.5 \approx 0.286$. They are not equal.

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Roll a fair die. Find $E[\lvert X - 3.5 \rvert]$, the average distance
from the mean.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The distances for faces 1 to 6 are $2.5, 1.5, 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5$:

$$E[\lvert X - 3.5 \rvert] = \frac{2.5 + 1.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 1.5 + 2.5}{6} = \frac{9}{6} = 1.5$$

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Use LOTUS and linearity to show that for any constant $c$,
$E[(X - c)^2] = E[X^2] - 2c \thinspace E[X] + c^2$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By LOTUS, $E[(X - c)^2] = \sum_x (x - c)^2 \thinspace p(x)$. Expand the square and split the sum:

$$\sum_x (x^2 - 2cx + c^2) \thinspace p(x) = \sum_x x^2 \thinspace p(x) - 2c \sum_x x \thinspace p(x) + c^2 \sum_x p(x) = E[X^2] - 2c \thinspace E[X] + c^2$$

The last step uses LOTUS for $E[X^2]$, the definition of $E[X]$, and the fact that the
PMF sums to 1.

</details>

## Summary

- LOTUS: average $g(x)$ with the weights $p_X(x)$. You never need the PMF of $g(X)$.
- It works because grouping the $x$ values by their $g(x)$ only adds probabilities.
- In general $E[g(X)] \ne g(E[X])$; equality is guaranteed only for linear $g$.
- LOTUS is the engine behind variance, moments and expected loss.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[g(X)] = \sum_{x} g(x) \thinspace p_X(x)}
$$
