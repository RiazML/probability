# 6.9 Tail-Sum Formula

[Probability](../README.md) / [Module 06: Expectation, Variance and Moments](README.md) / 6.9 Tail-Sum Formula

**Level:** 2 (Random Variables) · **Time:** about 35 minutes · **Before this:** [6.8 Median, Mode and Quantiles](6.8-Median-Mode-and-Quantiles.md)

> **Big idea:** For a variable that takes the values $0, 1, 2, \dots$, the mean is the sum of the tail probabilities $P(X \ge 1) + P(X \ge 2) + P(X \ge 3) + \cdots$.

---

## Why It Matters

For some variables, the PMF is awkward but the **tail** probabilities $P(X \ge k)$ are easy.
This happens for waiting times (the first $k - 1$ tries all failed), for the maximum of
several values (not all of them are below $k$), and for lifetimes (the machine still works
at time $k$). The tail-sum formula gives the mean directly from these tails. It is also a
neat example of the indicator trick from [6.4](6.4-Indicator-Trick.md).

## Intuition

A student climbs stairs to the library. Each day she climbs some number $X$ of steps,
between 0 and 4. You want her average number of steps per day.

One way: for each day, count her steps, then average. Another way: stand on step 1 and
count on what fraction of days she passes you. Then stand on step 2 and do the same, and
so on. Every step she climbs is counted once, by the person standing on that step. So the
average number of steps per day is

(fraction of days she reaches step 1) + (fraction of days she reaches step 2) + ...

That is the tail-sum formula: count by **steps** instead of by **days**.

## Picture

```text
                   k = 1   k = 2   k = 3   k = 4
   X = 1, prob p(1) [#]
   X = 2, prob p(2) [#]     [#]
   X = 3, prob p(3) [#]     [#]     [#]
   X = 4, prob p(4) [#]     [#]     [#]     [#]
                   ------  ------  ------  ------
   column total:   P(X≥1)  P(X≥2)  P(X≥3)  P(X≥4)
```

Each box in row $x$ has weight $p(x)$. Adding by rows gives $\sum_x x \thinspace p(x) = E[X]$;
adding by columns gives $\sum_k P(X \ge k)$. Same boxes, same total.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a random variable whose values are whole numbers $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (a
**non-negative integer** variable). Then

$$
E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(X \ge k)
$$

where $P(X \ge k)$ is the **tail probability** at $k$: the chance that $X$ reaches $k$ or more.

**In words:** the mean of a counting variable is the sum, over every level $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$,
of the probability that $X$ reaches that level.

Since $P(X \ge k) = P(X \gt k - 1)$, the same formula can be written as

$$
E[X] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} P(X \gt k)
$$

**In words:** you can also add the probabilities that $X$ is strictly above $0, 1, 2, \dots$

The formula is also true when $E[X] = \infty$: then both sides are infinite. A version for
continuous variables, $E[X] = \int_0^\infty P(X \gt t) \thinspace dt$, appears in
[8.5 Expectation and Variance with Integrals](../08-CONTINUOUS-RANDOM-VARIABLES/8.5-Expectation-and-Variance-with-Integrals.md).

## Why It Is True

**Proof 1: swap the order of a double sum.** Write each tail probability as a sum of PMF
values, then add the same boxes in the other order, as in the picture:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(X \ge k) &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \ \sum_{x=k}^{\infty} p(x) && \text{(}P(X \ge k) \text{ adds } p(x) \text{ for all } x \ge k \text{)} \\
&= \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} \ \sum_{k=1}^{x} p(x) && \text{(each pair has } 1 \le k \le x \text{; sum over } k \text{ first)} \\
&= \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} x \thinspace p(x) && \text{(}p(x) \text{ is added } x \text{ times)} \\
&= E[X] && \text{(the } x = 0 \text{ term is } 0 \text{ anyway)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Changing the order of the sums is allowed because every term $p(x)$ is non-negative.

**Proof 2: indicators.** A whole number $X \ge 0$ is the number of levels $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ with
$k \le X$. For example, if $X = 3$, the levels 1, 2 and 3 are reached. So

$$
X = I_{\lbrace X \ge 1 \rbrace} + I_{\lbrace X \ge 2 \rbrace} + I_{\lbrace X \ge 3 \rbrace} + \cdots
$$

Take expected values. Each indicator has mean $P(X \ge k)$ by [6.4](6.4-Indicator-Trick.md),
and linearity (which also holds for an infinite sum of non-negative terms) gives the formula.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: A Die Again

**Problem.** Use the tail-sum formula to find the mean of a fair die roll.

**Solution.**

1. The die shows $k$ or more in $7 - k$ of the 6 faces, so $P(X \ge k) = \frac{7 - k}{6}$ for
   $k = 1, \dots, 6$, and 0 for $k \ge 7$.
2. Add:

$$
E[X] = \frac{6}{6} + \frac{5}{6} + \frac{4}{6} + \frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5
$$

**Answer.** $E[X] = 3.5$, as in [6.1](6.1-Expected-Value.md).

**Check.** Same answer as the direct formula. Here the direct way is just as easy; the next
examples show where the tail sum wins.

### Example 2: The Larger of Two Dice

**Problem.** Roll two fair dice and let $M$ be the larger of the two numbers. Find $E[M]$.

**Solution.**

1. $M \le j$ means **both** dice are at most $j$. The dice are independent, so
   $P(M \le j) = \left(\frac{j}{6}\right)^2$.
2. The tail: $P(M \ge k) = 1 - P(M \le k - 1) = 1 - \left(\frac{k - 1}{6}\right)^2$.
3. List the six tails, in 36ths:
   - $k = 1$: $1 - 0 = \frac{36}{36}$
   - $k = 2$: $1 - \frac{1}{36} = \frac{35}{36}$
   - $k = 3$: $1 - \frac{4}{36} = \frac{32}{36}$
   - $k = 4$: $1 - \frac{9}{36} = \frac{27}{36}$
   - $k = 5$: $1 - \frac{16}{36} = \frac{20}{36}$
   - $k = 6$: $1 - \frac{25}{36} = \frac{11}{36}$
4. Add:

$$
E[M] = \frac{36 + 35 + 32 + 27 + 20 + 11}{36} = \frac{161}{36} \approx 4.472
$$

**Answer.** $E[M] = \frac{161}{36} \approx 4.47$.

**Check.** The maximum should be above the mean of one die, $3.5$, and below 6. It is. Also,
the PMF of $M$ is $\frac{1}{36}, \frac{3}{36}, \frac{5}{36}, \frac{7}{36}, \frac{9}{36}, \frac{11}{36}$ for $1, \dots, 6$, and
$\frac{1 + 6 + 15 + 28 + 45 + 66}{36} = \frac{161}{36}$. Same answer.

### Example 3: Resending a Network Packet

**Problem.** A device sends a packet over a noisy wireless link. Each try gets through with
probability $0.8$, independently of the other tries. It keeps trying until the packet gets
through. Let $N$ be the number of tries. Find $E[N]$.

**Solution.**

1. $N \ge k$ means the first $k - 1$ tries all failed. Each fails with probability $0.2$, so
   $P(N \ge k) = 0.2^{k-1}$.
2. Add the tails. This is a geometric series, $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1 - r}$ for $0 \le r \lt 1$:

$$
E[N] = 1 + 0.2 + 0.2^2 + 0.2^3 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1 - 0.2} = \frac{1}{0.8} = 1.25
$$

**Answer.** $E[N] = 1.25$ tries on average.

**Check.** In general, with success probability $p$ the same steps give $E[N] = \frac{1}{p}$, the
mean of the geometric distribution (see [7.3 Geometric](../07-DISCRETE-DISTRIBUTIONS/7.3-Geometric.md)).
With $p = 0.8$ most packets need only one try, so a mean just above 1 makes sense.

## Simulate It

The code checks Examples 2 and 3. It computes the mean twice from the same data: as a plain
average, and as a sum of tail fractions.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_trials = 1_000_000

# Max of two dice: E[M] = sum over k of P(M >= k) = 161/36.
m = np.maximum(rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials), rng.integers(1, 7, n_trials))
tail_sum = sum(np.mean(m >= k) for k in range(1, 7))   # tail sum from data
print(f"Max of two dice: average {m.mean():.4f}, tail sum {tail_sum:.4f}, "
      f"exact {161 / 36:.4f}")

# Packet sent until it gets through, success probability 0.8 per try.
tries = rng.geometric(0.8, size=n_trials)
tail_sum = sum(np.mean(tries >= k) for k in range(1, 30))
print(f"Transmissions:   average {tries.mean():.4f}, tail sum {tail_sum:.4f}, "
      f"exact {1 / 0.8:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
Max of two dice: average 4.4715, tail sum 4.4715, exact 4.4722
Transmissions:   average 1.2501, tail sum 1.2501, exact 1.2500
```

The average and the tail sum agree to every digit, because for data the formula is an exact
identity: it only counts the same boxes in a different order.

## Common Mistakes

- **Starting the sum at $k = 0$ with $\ge$.** $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} P(X \ge k)$ includes
  $P(X \ge 0) = 1$, which makes the answer 1 too big. Use $\ge$ from $k = 1$, or $\gt$ from $k = 0$.
- **Using it for variables that can be negative.** The formula needs $X \ge 0$. For a
  variable that can be negative, the negative part needs its own tail sum.
- **Using it for non-integer values.** For values like $0.5$ or $2.7$, the sum over whole
  numbers $k$ is wrong; use the integral version from Module 08.
- **Mixing up $P(X \ge k)$ and $P(X \le k)$.** The formula uses the **upper** tail. If you know
  the CDF, convert: $P(X \ge k) = 1 - F(k - 1)$.

## Where It Is Used

- **Computer science:** expected numbers of retries, probes in hashing, or rounds of a
  randomized algorithm, where "it takes at least $k$ rounds" is easy to bound.
- **Reliability engineering:** the mean lifetime of a component is the sum (or integral) of
  its **survival probabilities** $P(\text{still working at time } k)$.
- **AI and machine learning:** the expected number of samples until a good one appears, as
  in rejection sampling ([19.5 Rejection Sampling](../19-SIMULATION-AND-MONTE-CARLO/19.5-Rejection-Sampling.md)).
- **Order statistics:** means of maxima and minima, as in Example 2 (see
  [13.6 Max and Min](../13-FUNCTIONS-OF-RANDOM-VARIABLES/13.6-Max-and-Min.md)).
- **Insurance:** expected number of years a customer stays, from the yearly probabilities of
  still being a customer.

## Practice

**1. (Easy)** $X$ takes values in $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, 3 \rbrace$ with $P(X \ge 1) = 0.8$, $P(X \ge 2) = 0.5$ and
$P(X \ge 3) = 0.1$. Find $E[X]$.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$$E[X] = 0.8 + 0.5 + 0.1 = 1.4$$

Check with the PMF: $p(1) = 0.3$, $p(2) = 0.4$, $p(3) = 0.1$, so $E[X] = 0.3 + 0.8 + 0.3 = 1.4$.

</details>

**2. (Easy)** Use the form $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} P(X \gt k)$ to find the mean of a die roll.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(X \gt k) = \frac{6 - k}{6}$ for $k = 0, \dots, 5$:

$$E[X] = \frac{6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5$$

</details>

**3. (Medium)** Roll three fair dice. Find the expected value of the largest number.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(\max \ge k) = 1 - \left(\frac{k - 1}{6}\right)^3$. The subtracted parts add to $\frac{0 + 1 + 8 + 27 + 64 + 125}{216} = \frac{225}{216}$, so

$$E[\max] = 6 - \frac{225}{216} = \frac{1071}{216} \approx 4.958$$

</details>

**4. (Medium)** Roll two fair dice. Find the expected value of the **smaller** number $L$.
Then check that $E[L] + E[M] = 7$, where $M$ is the larger one.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$L \ge k$ means both dice are at least $k$, so $P(L \ge k) = \left(\frac{7 - k}{6}\right)^2$:

$$E[L] = \frac{36 + 25 + 16 + 9 + 4 + 1}{36} = \frac{91}{36} \approx 2.528$$

Since $L + M$ is the sum of the two dice, linearity gives $E[L] + E[M] = 7$. Indeed
$\frac{91}{36} + \frac{161}{36} = \frac{252}{36} = 7$.

</details>

**5. (Hard)** Prove that for a non-negative integer variable,
$E[X^2] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (2k - 1) \thinspace P(X \ge k)$. Check it for a die.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a whole number $x \ge 0$, the odd numbers add up to a square: $1 + 3 + \cdots + (2x - 1) = x^2$.
So $X^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (2k - 1) I_{\lbrace X \ge k \rbrace}$, because exactly the terms with $k \le X$ are switched on.
Take expected values (all terms are non-negative):

$$E[X^2] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (2k - 1) \thinspace E[I_{\lbrace X \ge k \rbrace}] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (2k - 1) \thinspace P(X \ge k)$$

For a die, $P(X \ge k) = \frac{7 - k}{6}$:

$$E[X^2] = \frac{1 \cdot 6 + 3 \cdot 5 + 5 \cdot 4 + 7 \cdot 3 + 9 \cdot 2 + 11 \cdot 1}{6} = \frac{6 + 15 + 20 + 21 + 18 + 11}{6} = \frac{91}{6}$$

This matches [6.3](6.3-LOTUS.md).

</details>

**6. (Simulate)** Check Practice 3 by simulation.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(1_000_000, 3))
m = rolls.max(axis=1)                          # max of three dice
exact = sum(1 - ((k - 1) / 6) ** 3 for k in range(1, 7))
print(f"E[max of 3 dice]: simulation {m.mean():.4f}, exact {exact:.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
E[max of 3 dice]: simulation 4.9577, exact 4.9583
```

</details>

## Summary

- For $X$ in $\lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace$, the mean is the sum of the upper tails.
- It counts the same boxes as $\sum_x x \thinspace p(x)$, in a different order.
- It shines when $P(X \ge k)$ is easier than $P(X = k)$: waiting times, maxima, minima.
- Start at $k = 1$ with $\ge$, or at $k = 0$ with $\gt$.

The one formula to remember:

$$
\boxed{E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(X \ge k) \qquad (X \in \lbrace 0, 1, 2, \dots \rbrace)}
$$
